A basket of clear umbrellas at the entrance is the pretty layer of a rain plan, and it photographs well because you can still see faces. It is not a substitute for a sided tent, an indoor cutoff time, or a ceremony that can move. Have the umbrellas, and have the actual backup.

The short version. Buy a pack of clear, windproof umbrellas — enough for couples to share, not necessarily one per guest — and set them in a stand or basket at the entrance with a short sign. Keep a few for the wedding party and the photographer. Use them to walk between a covered ceremony, a tent, and cars. Decide, in writing, the time and the place you will move if the rain is more than a pretty drizzle. A clear umbrella is a photograph. A sided tent is a wedding.

Pretty rain, and rain that is actually a problem

There is a version of a rainy wedding that everyone is picturing: a soft drizzle, clear umbrellas, the photographer delighted, and vows that still happen exactly where you planned. That version exists. It is also not the only version. A real rain plan has to survive wind, a downpour during the processional, and a yard that turns to mud between the chairs and the tent.

Couples who have gambled on an outdoor ceremony are honest about this. The umbrellas were lovely. They did not keep a hundred people dry for twenty minutes, and they did not keep the sound system safe. If your only backup is "we'll hold umbrellas," you do not have a backup. Our backyard wedding weather plan is the longer essay on tents, rooms, and the cutoff that is not "we'll see how we feel." This post is the kit that sits on top of that plan.

Have both. A sided tent, or a room in the house, is the plan. The clear umbrellas are how people look and stay reasonably dry getting from the car to the tent, and from the tent to the ceremony if you are still outside in a light rain.

How many umbrellas, and which kind

You do not need one umbrella per guest. A 47-inch canopy covers two people walking together, and many guests will arrive already carrying their own. Buy enough for about half of your guests to be under one, plus a few extras for the wedding party, the officiant, and the photographer. For 80 guests, a 14-pack of large clear umbrellas is a solid start; add a second pack if the walk from parking to the tent is long, or if the forecast looks truly wet. For 40 guests, one pack is usually enough.

Clear umbrellas are the right call for photos because faces stay visible. A bubble umbrella comes down a little farther and keeps shoulders drier, which is kinder in wind-driven rain. Regular black travel umbrellas will work in a pinch and will look like a funeral procession in the aisle photos. If you already own a mix, put the clear ones in the ceremony basket and keep the rest for the parking walk.

Wind matters more than people expect. "Windproof" still means a cheap umbrella can invert if a gust hits the lawn. Buy the larger, sturdier pack, and have a couple of extras. Open and close one when they arrive so you are not discovering a broken rib on the day. Store them in the house, not in a hot shed where the plastic can warp.

Where to put them, and how to say they are for guests

Set a wicker stand or a tall basket at the place people actually arrive, not at the far end of the aisle. A small sign that says "please take an umbrella" is necessary. People will not help themselves to something that looks like decor. If you have a covered porch, put the stand just under the roofline so the first guest does not get soaked while they are still deciding.

Keep a second, smaller stash for the wedding party in the getting-ready room, already open and checked. The couple, the person walking someone down the aisle, and the officiant should not be hunting through a basket as the music starts. Give the photographer two umbrellas with a note, or they will end up using their own, or none.

On the way back in, you need a place for wet umbrellas that is not the dance floor. A plastic bin, a boot tray, or a towel-lined crate by the entrance keeps water in one spot. A few reusable shoe covers in the same basket can help on a truly muddy walk, and they are optional. Hand towels for wet hands and damp chairs are not.

The cutoff: when to put the umbrellas down and move

Write this down before the week of the wedding, and tell the officiant, the photographer, and whoever is in charge on the day. A useful version is: if it is raining lightly at the start time, we stay outside under umbrellas for a short ceremony. If it is a downpour, or lightning, or the wind is turning umbrellas inside out, we move to the tent or the living room at least thirty minutes before guests arrive, and we do not wait to "see if it passes." Weather passes and does not pass on your schedule.

A sided tent, or a room that can actually hold your guest count, is the real backup. If you do not have one, umbrellas will not invent it. Look at tent rental cost and the weather plan early enough to book sides and flooring, not the morning of. Clear umbrellas can still go out at the tent door, for the walk from the cars.

Lightning is a full stop. You cannot pretty your way through it. Move everyone inside, even if the living room is tight, and wait. The photographs will be of a slightly crowded, very alive room, and that is a better memory than a stubborn ceremony in a storm.

Mud is the other surprise. A runner, boards, or a different path from parking can matter more than an extra pack of umbrellas. Someone should walk the route in the morning, in shoes they do not care about, and decide whether guests can make it in dress shoes.

What to skip

Skip a paper parasol, which wilts and is not an umbrella. Skip black golf umbrellas as your only option if you care how the aisle looks. Skip setting the basket out the night before in an open yard. Skip hoping the forecast will stay at "20 percent." And skip telling guests "come rain or shine" without also telling them where to go if it shines less than you hoped. Put the backup location on the website and in a text the morning of, and let the umbrellas do the pretty part of an otherwise grown-up plan.

A short clear-umbrella checklist

Buy enough large clear umbrellas for about half your guests to walk in twos, plus a few for the wedding party and the photographer. Set them at the entrance with a sign, and put a bin and towels where people will come back in. Write a cutoff for moving the ceremony, and book the tent or the room that makes that cutoff real. Then, if you get the pretty drizzle, you will be ready for the photographs and for the rest of the day.
