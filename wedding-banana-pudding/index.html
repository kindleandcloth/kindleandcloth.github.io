Banana pudding for a wedding crowd is a scratch vanilla pudding, layered with wafers and bananas in 9x13 pans, and finished with whipped cream. The pudding can be made ahead. The bananas and the wafers cannot sit in it overnight without turning brown and going to paste.

The short version. Cook a stovetop vanilla pudding — milk, yolks, sugar, cornstarch, butter, vanilla — and cool it. Layer it in 9x13 pans with vanilla wafers and sliced bananas close to service, then top with whipped cream. Two pans will serve about 24; four pans, about 48. This is not a brownie-and-bar dessert table, and it is not a pie bar or a cookie table, which already have their own posts. A knife and a board for the bananas, and a cool place for the assembled pans, will do most of the last-hour work.

A pudding you cook, and a pan you do not assemble yesterday

The tools that actually earn their keep here are specific. A beverage cooler, to keep a cold dish actually cold on a warm afternoon, a pyrex 9x13, a trifle bowl glass large, a macaroni and cheese pan deep, a lasagna pan deep ceramic, which is the actual tool this part of the day uses, not a generic stand-in, a pie carrier insulated, a drink dispenser spigot replacement, and a trifle bowl glass large, when you want a deep bowl you can layer and actually see.

Banana pudding is often treated as a dump-and-chill dessert, which is how it earns a reputation for brown fruit and soggy wafers. The wedding version should taste like vanilla, cream, and bananas that were still themselves when you sliced them. That means a scratch pudding, a cool-down, and a late assembly. Instant mix will work in an emergency. It will not taste like you meant this dessert to be the one sweet thing on the table.

Two 9x13 pans, scooped in modest cups or squares, will serve about 24. Four pans will serve about 48. This is a spoon dessert, not a tidy slice, so put out small bowls or cups, not only plates and a cake server. If it is one dessert among several, you can make less, but banana pudding disappears when it is good, because it is familiar and it does not feel as heavy as a frosted sheet cake.

It belongs on a dessert table, or as the single sweet at a casual dinner, not as a late-night add-on you assemble at 10 p.m. after the snack table is already out, and not as a stand-in for a dinner at home that never quite got a dessert plan. The bananas will wait in their peels. The pudding will wait in a bowl. The assembled pans will not wait all afternoon in a warm yard. Keep them cool, and bring them out in waves if the hour is long.

A chocolate sheet cake already lives elsewhere on this site as a category on the dessert-table guide. Cookie tables and pie bars are different desserts, with their own holding rules. Banana pudding is worth its own post because the timing is specific, and because a tray of pudding that was layered at breakfast will look and taste like a different dish by dinner.

If anyone in the list cannot eat dairy or eggs, this is not their dessert, and a second sweet should be labeled and actually good, not a lonely bowl of berries. If bananas are a problem for someone, the same rule applies. You cannot make one pan do every dietary job, and you should not hide a dairy pudding behind a vague “trifle” sign. A second sweet can be as simple as a labeled plate from the cookie table, or a fruit platter, as long as it is actually there when the pudding comes out, not promised and then forgotten in a refrigerator.

Scratch vanilla pudding, wafers, and bananas that still look like bananas

Cook the pudding in a heavy pot so it does not scorch. Whisk sugar and cornstarch together first, then whisk in the yolks and a little cold milk to make a smooth slurry, and pour that into the rest of the milk as it heats. Stir all the time, especially at the bottom and the corners, until the pudding thickens and a bubble or two breaks the surface. Take it off the heat, stir in butter and vanilla, and strain it if you see any scrambled bits. Press plastic wrap or a buttered paper directly onto the surface so a skin does not form, and cool it until it is no longer warm. Warm pudding will wilt wafers on contact and will push the bananas toward brown.

Use bananas that are ripe, with a few spots, and still firm. Green bananas taste like nothing. Soft ones mash when you slice them and go brown faster. Slice them just before you layer, not at breakfast “to be ready.” A little toss with a squeeze of lemon can slow the browning if the assembly is a short wait; it should not make the pudding taste like lemon. Work in batches, pan by pan, so cut fruit is not sitting on a board for an hour.

Vanilla wafers belong in a single layer, then pudding, then bananas, then more wafers, then more pudding, ending with pudding so the top can take cream. Do not build a nine-layer monument in a 9x13. Two or three layers will serve cleanly and will set enough to scoop. Leave a little pudding for the top so wafers are not peeking out and drying. A board next to the pans, and towels for the inevitable drip, will keep the table from looking like a cafeteria by the second scoop.

Whipped cream goes on just before service, or in a bowl beside the pans if you are nervous about a warm room. Whip it to soft peaks, sweetened a little, and do not beat it into butter. Aerosol cream will collapse and taste like the can. If the room is truly hot, skip a full frosted top and offer a bowl of cream with a spoon, kept cold, so each serving gets a spoonful that has not been sitting on a pudding skin in the sun.

The pudding itself can be made a day ahead and refrigerated. The wafers can stay in their boxes. The bananas can stay in a bowl on the counter, unpeeled, until the last hour. That is the mise en place. What you should not do is layer the pans after rehearsal dinner and congratulate yourself on being done. You will serve sweet banana soup with beige wafers, and you will know it as soon as you lift the wrap. I would rather finish the last pan in a quiet kitchen during cocktail hour, or even just after dinner, than pretend a twelve-hour sit is a make-ahead triumph. The pudding will already be cold. The work that remains is slicing, layering, and wiping the rim of the pan. It is a short job, and it is the job that makes the dessert taste like bananas and vanilla, instead of like a pan that has been waiting all day to be noticed. Guests can tell the difference, even if they cannot name it.

Scratch banana pudding in 9x13 pans

The recipe below is for two 9x13 pans, about 24 servings, or four pans if you double it. Use the larger batch if this is the main dessert. Cool the pudding completely, and assemble close to service.

Scratch Banana Pudding in 9x13 Pans

A stovetop vanilla pudding layered in 9x13 pans with vanilla wafers and sliced bananas, topped with whipped cream. Make the pudding ahead. Assemble close to service so the bananas stay pale and the wafers do not go to paste. Two pans serve about 24; four pans, about 48.

2 or 4 pans (about 24 or 48) · Prep 30 minutes plus cooling · Cook about 15 minutes

Lemon juice, optional, for the bananas1 teaspoon2 teaspoons
  1. In a small bowl, whisk the sugar and cornstarch. Whisk in the yolks and about a cup of the cold milk until smooth. Heat the remaining milk in a heavy pot over medium until it is steaming. Whisk the yolk mixture into the pot and cook, stirring constantly, until the pudding thickens and a bubble or two breaks the surface, about 8 to 12 minutes. Do not walk away. Take the pot off the heat, stir in the butter and vanilla, and strain if needed. Press wrap directly onto the surface and cool completely, then refrigerate until you are ready to assemble, up to a day.
  2. Just before service, slice the bananas. Toss them with the lemon juice if you are using it. In each 9x13 pan, make a layer of wafers, a layer of pudding, a layer of bananas, then repeat, ending with pudding. Two or three layers are enough. Keep the pans cool.
  3. Whip the cream with the sugar to soft peaks. Spread it on the pans just before you set them out, or serve it in a cold bowl alongside. Scoop into small bowls or cups. Do not assemble the pans a day ahead, and do not leave a dairy pudding in a warm yard. Bring out a second pan when the first is low, rather than parking both in the sun.

Scooping, a cool hold, and a table that can take a drip

Set the pans on a towel or a tray, with a large spoon in each, and bowls stacked where a guest can reach them without stretching over the dessert. One person can scoop for the first rush if the line is tight; after that, people can serve themselves. A messy top is fine. A melting top in direct sun is not. If the reception is outdoors and warm, park the pans in a cool indoor room and bring one out at a time, the way you would bring out a bowl of cream. A cooler with ice packs, not loose ice against the glass, can hold a reserve pan for a short time if the house is already full. Do not sit a 9x13 in a puddle of meltwater. Wipe the bottom before it goes on the table.

Put a small sign on the pan, in ordinary words: banana pudding, with dairy and eggs. People who need to know will not always ask, and a dessert that looks like a fruit salad can surprise them. If you also have cookies or a cake, give this pudding its own corner so scoops do not land on a dry cookie plate.

Leftover assembled pudding will keep, covered and cold, and it will taste more like a soft, banana-heavy leftover than like the first scoop. That can be a kindness at midnight or the next morning. It is not a reason to assemble early on purpose. If you have a full unused pan still un-layered, you can layer it later for the household and it will taste like the dessert you meant to serve.

A short banana-pudding checklist

Cook the pudding a day ahead if you want, cool it with wrap on the surface, and keep it cold. Leave the bananas unpeeled and the wafers boxed until the last hour. Layer two or four 9x13 pans close to service, top with whipped cream, and keep the pans cool. Scoop, do not slice, and refill from a cold reserve if the hour is long.

Do not build the pans the night before and call the job done. Banana pudding for a wedding is a scratch pot of vanilla, a late slice of fruit, and a wafer that still has a little structure. That is the whole method, and it is enough. If you do this in order — pudding first, fruit last, cream at the table — you will serve a familiar dessert that still tastes freshly put together, which is the rarest thing on a wedding dessert table and the whole reason to make banana pudding instead of another tray of bars.
