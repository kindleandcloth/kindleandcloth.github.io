A Farewell Brunch the Day After the Wedding, for the People Who Stayed

A farewell brunch is a shorter, smaller morning for the people who stayed or traveled, not a second reception and not the breakfast you ate while you were getting dressed.

The short version. Invite the people who are still in town, give them a clear start and end, and serve food you can put out without cooking a new event. Coffee, a tray or two, fruit, and something you can leave on the table will do more than a made-to-order station. This is not a brunch reception, and it is not the wedding-morning breakfast for the getting-ready room. If you already wrote a weekend itinerary, this is the last line of it. A baking dish you can refill and a pot of coffee are enough.

Top picks

  • Best size: the people who stayed over, plus anyone with a long drive, not the whole wedding list.
  • Best hour: late enough that you can sleep, early enough that travelers can still make a plane or a school-night road.
  • Best table: food that is already cooked or assembled, coffee that does not depend on one person, and a clear end time.

The morning after is a different meal

If you are going to buy anything for this, buy the tools the work actually uses. A beverage dispensers, which is the actual tool this part of the day uses, not a generic stand-in, a pyrex 9x13, a macaroni and cheese pan deep, a lasagna pan deep ceramic, a coffee urn 55 cup, if coffee is actually part of the plan, not a single drip pot hiding in the kitchen, a hot chocolate dispenser, a fine mesh cocoa sifter, and a espresso maker stovetop moka, if the dessert actually needs coffee, not a tablespoon of instant in cold water.

The wedding is over. A farewell brunch is not a chance to redo the toast, open a second gift table, or serve a new themed menu. It is a way to feed the people who slept on a sofa, drove in, or have a few hours before they leave, and to say a quieter thank-you than the night allowed.

Keep the list short on purpose. Out-of-town family, the wedding party if they are still here, the friend who stayed to take down chairs, a neighbor who hosted parking — these are the usual names. The cousin who came for the ceremony and went home at ten does not need a second invitation unless you truly want them there and they can come without rearranging a Monday.

Tell people, in the itinerary or a text, that there will be a morning gathering, where it is, when it starts, and when you mean to end. “Brunch sometime” produces a trickle that lasts until mid-afternoon and traps the hosts in a kitchen they have already cleaned once. A start time and a two-hour window are a kindness, including to yourselves.

You do not have to host it at the same house as the wedding, but it is often easiest if you do, or at a parent’s house already full of people. A restaurant can work for a very small list if you have a reservation and a way to pay that does not turn into a scramble at the register. For a backyard weekend, a house table is usually the honest plan. The yard can be used if the morning is mild; it should not be a second ceremony site.

Dress is ordinary. People will arrive in whatever they slept in or will travel in. You can be more dressed if you want photographs, but do not invent a dress code for a leftover morning. The work of the day is coffee, plates, and a goodbye that is not rushed off a porch with no food.

If the wedding ran late, protect a little sleep and start later rather than pretending you will be bright at eight. Travelers would rather have a 10:30 start that actually happens than a 9 a.m. you cannot keep. If a guest has a very early flight, say goodbye the night before and do not build the brunch around a departure you cannot feed.

Who is invited, and how long it lasts

Write the list the way you would write any other small morning: names, and whether they already know the address. If the wedding invitation mentioned a Sunday brunch, you have already invited whoever received that line, and you should honor it or send a clear correction early. If the brunch was only in a private itinerary, the list can stay the smaller one.

Children who stayed over should be fed; you do not need a separate kids’ menu, only something they will actually eat and a place to sit that is not a formal setting. Pets, neighbors dropping in, and a vendor who is coming back for a tent should be thought about, not surprised by an open door and no coffee.

Two hours is plenty. Ninety minutes can be enough if the list is small and people have flights. Stay later only if you truly want a long morning and the house can stand it. An end time lets grandparents rest, lets you start thank-you notes or a walk through the yard, and lets guests leave without wondering whether they are the last ones.

If someone cannot come, do not rebuild the morning around them. A text the day before, a leftover plate dropped at a hotel, or a goodbye the night of is enough. The brunch is for the people who are here.

Out-of-town guests will ask about leftover cake, a lost jacket, or a ride. Put a small table or a chair near the door for things going home, and decide in advance who has a car and a little time. A farewell brunch that also becomes a lost-and-found and a shuttle desk will feel chaotic unless one person, not the couple, is holding that list.

Food that can wait, and a table that is already tired

Cook on Saturday only if the food can sit, or cook very little on Sunday morning. A breakfast casserole or a tray of baked eggs, a bowl of fruit, a bread or a leftover pastry, yogurt if you like it, and coffee will feed a short list. If you already have a savory leftover — a tray of roasted vegetables, a ham, a pot of beans — serve it at room temperature or reheated to a safe temperature, labeled if it contains something a guest needs to know. Do not invent a new station you still have to flip or toast to order.

A waffle or pancake iron on Sunday morning is a different post, and it is a lot of work after a wedding. If you love that idea and have a helper who is not exhausted, it can be a small treat for a tiny list. For most farewell brunches, a pan that comes out of the oven, or a bakery box, is the kinder plan. Save the waffle bar for a reception that was designed for it, not for the morning after.

Coffee should not depend on one person filling a single machine cup by cup. A large pot, an urn, or a dispenser of water and a second pot of decaf if you know you need it, with milk and a few mugs, will do. Tea can be a kettle and a box. Juice in a pitcher is enough. You do not need a full bar at 10 a.m. unless you already meant to offer one, and even then keep it small.

Set the table simply. A runner, a stack of plates, towels for messes, and glasses that can do juice or water are enough. Use what is already clean from the night before only if it is actually clean. A sink full of wedding glasses is not a brunch inventory. Paper plates are allowed. This morning is not a second tablescape.

Allergies and a vegetarian guest still matter, even on a short list. Label the egg dish if it has meat, keep a fruit bowl that is only fruit, and do not hide a ham in a casserole without a note. You already know this list; you do not need a new dietary survey. If the morning is larger than a dining table, set food in a line that ends at the seats, not in a cluster that blocks the only path to the coffee. People will stand and talk; they still need a place to set a plate down.

The house, the leftovers, and getting people on the road

The house will already be tired. Do what you can on Saturday night — trash out, a clear table, a dishwasher run — and accept that Sunday will start with a counter that is not magazine-ready. Clear one table for food and one surface near the door for bags and lost things. Leave the gift table closed if you can; opening presents during brunch turns a short morning into a long one, and it leaves paper all over a room you wanted to reset.

Leftovers should be dealt with honestly. Send travelers with a wrapped slice or a container only if they want it and can travel with it. Do not load a departing guest down with a tray they cannot take through security or keep cold. What stays should be refrigerated, or eaten, or thrown away. A wedding refrigerator is a food-safety problem if it is a door that no longer closes.

Parking, the same neighbor courtesy you used the day before, and a quiet hour still apply. A Sunday morning is often easier on a street, but it is still a gathering. If you said music would end Saturday, do not start a speaker at 9 a.m. If a rental company is coming for a tent, tell brunch guests where they can and cannot stand, and keep coffee out of the path of the crew.

Give people what they need to leave: the wifi if they must check a flight, a printed or texted address for the house if someone is coming from a hotel, and a realistic sense of how long the drive to the airport is on a Sunday. If you have extra water or a banana for the car, put them by the door. Then let people go. A long lingering goodbye is lovely until someone misses a boarding time.

You can sit down. The hosts should eat, and they should not spend the whole window at the stove. If one person wants to pour coffee, the other can sit. If a parent wants to host this part, let them, and be a guest at your own table for an hour. That is allowed, and it is often the point. If you are leaving for a trip later in the day, say so in the invitation so people do not plan to linger into your departure, and pack before the coffee starts, not after.

A short farewell-brunch checklist

Before the wedding, decide whether there will be a morning gathering, who is invited, and where it will be. Put the hour on the itinerary or in a text. Shop for coffee, milk, fruit, and one tray that can be assembled or baked without a new project.

The night before, clear a table, run the dishes you will need, and set out mugs and plates. Put travel notes and leftover containers in one place. Decide who is the person to ask about rides and lost jackets.

In the morning, start the coffee first, put the food out, and greet people. End when you said you would. Pack the car things, thank the people who are leaving, and let the house be a house again.

A farewell brunch is a short, kind last meal, not a second wedding. Keep the list to the people who are still here, keep the food simple, and keep the end time. The day after already has enough work in it. Coffee, a tray, and a real goodbye are enough, and they are the whole job. If the morning goes well, it will feel like a second, quieter table, not like an encore. That is the aim, and it is worth planning for in the same notebook as the rest of the weekend, then letting it stay small. You will be glad you did, once the last car has pulled away.
