Wedding Tiramisu in 9x13 Pans: A Make-Ahead Dessert Without Raw Yolks

Wedding tiramisu is a make-ahead dessert you can assemble in 9x13 pans: espresso-soaked ladyfingers and a mascarpone cream made with whipped cream, not raw yolks. Keep it cold, and dust the cocoa close to service. Two pans serve about twenty-four; four pans, about forty-eight.

The short version. Dip ladyfingers in cool espresso, layer them in 9x13 pans with a mascarpone cream that is only mascarpone, whipped cream, sugar, and vanilla, and chill the pans thoroughly. Dust cocoa just before you set them out. This is the opposite of banana pudding, which should not be assembled the day before. It is not a pie bar or a cookie table. A cool hold, and a cooler for a reserve pan if the house is already full, will do more than a last-minute assembly in a crowded kitchen.

Top picks

  • Best cream: mascarpone folded with whipped cream, with no raw yolks in the bowl.
  • Best assembly: the day before, or the morning of, so the pans can chill and the ladyfingers can soften without turning to mush.
  • Best finish: cocoa dusted close to service, and a pan that stays cold until it is scooped.

The make-ahead dessert, without raw yolks

If you are going to buy anything for this, buy the tools the work actually uses. A pyrex 9x13, which is the pan this recipe is actually built around, a fine mesh cocoa sifter, a macaroni and cheese pan deep, a lasagna pan deep ceramic, a espresso maker stovetop moka, if the dessert actually needs coffee, not a tablespoon of instant in cold water, a trifle bowl glass large, a ziti baking pan, and a trifle bowl glass large, when you want a deep bowl you can layer and actually see.

Classic tiramisu is often made with a zabaglione of yolks and sugar, sometimes uncooked, and that is a poor idea for a backyard crowd. A wedding pan should be a dessert you can serve without wondering who might be unhappy about raw eggs, and without a custard that has to be cooked, cooled, and folded at the last hour. Mascarpone beaten with a little sugar and folded into whipped cream will set in the cold, taste like the dessert people want, and keep the method inside a home kitchen.

It is also, for once, a dessert that wants to be assembled ahead. Banana pudding browns and goes soft if you layer it early. Tiramisu needs a few hours, or a night, so the espresso soaks in and the cream firms enough to scoop. That is the reason it belongs on a wedding menu. You can finish the pans after rehearsal, keep them cold, and dust them when dinner is ending, instead of building a dessert during cocktail hour.

Two 9x13 pans, scooped in modest squares or cups, will serve about twenty-four. Four pans will serve about forty-eight. This is a rich dessert. People will take a smaller piece than they take of a sheet cake, and they will still come back if it is good. If it is one sweet among several on a dessert table, the smaller batch may be enough. If it is the only dessert, make the four pans, or three, and keep one back, cold.

Coffee is part of the flavor, not only a drink at the end. A coffee bar can sit nearby, but the espresso for the pans should be made earlier, cooled, and set aside. Do not dip ladyfingers in a scalding pot, and do not brew the soak while guests are arriving. Cool coffee is a better soak than a bitter, hot one that cooks the fingers on contact.

If anyone in the list cannot have caffeine, dairy, gluten, or alcohol, this may not be their dessert. Ladyfingers are wheat. The cream is dairy. The soak is coffee, and some versions add a little rum or marsala. Say all of that on a label, in ordinary words, and put a second sweet on the table that is actually safe for them. A lone bowl of berries is fine if it is really there.

Ladyfingers, espresso, and a mascarpone cream

Use crisp savoiardi-style ladyfingers, the dry ones, not a soft sponge that will collapse at the first dip. Brew strong espresso or a very strong coffee, sweeten it lightly if you like, and cool it completely. If you want a splash of rum or marsala in the soak, add it now, taste, and remember to put it on the sign. The fingers should be dipped quickly — a second or two per side — and laid in the pan. A long soak makes mush. A dry finger under the cream will stay too firm. Work over a shallow bowl, and set a sheet nearby for drips and extras.

The cream is straightforward and should be cold. Beat the mascarpone with the sugar and vanilla until it is smooth, not over-whipped into grain. In a second bowl, whip the cream to soft or medium peaks, then fold the two together until there are no streaks. It should be thick enough to spread and still soft enough to settle into the ladyfingers. If the mascarpone is very cold and lumpy, let it take off the intense chill for a few minutes and beat it smooth before you add sugar, rather than fighting lumps into the cream.

Two layers are enough in a 9x13: a layer of dipped fingers, a layer of cream, another layer of fingers, and a final layer of cream, smoothed. Do not build a four-layer monument that will slump when scooped. Press the fingers gently into a single layer, breaking a few to fill gaps, and keep the soak even so one corner is not a dry biscuit and the other a sponge. Cover the pans and refrigerate them at least four hours, and overnight if you can. They should be cold through, not cool only at the edge.

Cocoa belongs at the end. A full dusting the night before will go damp and blotchy in the refrigerator. Just before service, dust a even layer of unsweetened cocoa over the top, and wipe the rims of the pans. A towel under each pan, and a board or a tray if you are carrying them through a doorway, will keep the walk from becoming a cocoa fingerprint trail.

Do not assemble tiramisu in a warm garage and call the night air a refrigerator. Dairy and a coffee-soaked cake need a real cold hold. If the house refrigerator is already full, that is a planning problem to solve the day before, not a reason to park pans in a cooler of loose ice that will wet the bottoms and never get the centers cold enough.

Tiramisu in 9x13 pans

The recipe below is for two 9x13 pans, about twenty-four servings, or four pans if you double it. There are no raw yolks. Assemble ahead, chill thoroughly, and dust the cocoa close to service.

Wedding Tiramisu in 9x13 Pans

Espresso-soaked ladyfingers layered in 9x13 pans with a mascarpone cream made from mascarpone, whipped cream, sugar, and vanilla — no raw yolks. Assemble ahead, keep cold, and dust cocoa close to service. Two pans serve about 24; four pans, about 48.

In a large bowl, beat the mascarpone with the sugar and vanilla until smooth. In a second bowl, whip the cream to soft or medium peaks. Fold the cream into the mascarpone until no streaks remain. Keep the bowl cold while you work.

Spread half of the cream for that pan over the fingers. Repeat with a second layer of dipped fingers and a final layer of cream, and smooth the top. A cold hold, cocoa at the end, and a clean scoop

Set a pan on a towel, with a wide spatula or a large spoon, and stack small plates or cups where people can reach them. The first scoops will be the neatest. After that, the pan will look like a pan, which is fine. What is not fine is a soft, warm tiramisu that slumps into the coffee layer because it sat on a sunny dessert table. Keep the reserve pan in the refrigerator, or in a cooler with ice packs — not loose ice against the glass — and bring it out when the first pan is low. Wipe the bottom before it goes on the table.

A small sign does the work of a dozen questions. People will ask if there is raw egg, if there is alcohol, if there is caffeine. Answer on the card. If you also have cookies or a cake, give this dessert its own corner so cocoa and cream do not land on a dry cookie plate, and so the scoop line does not block the rest of the table.

Leftover tiramisu, covered and kept cold, is one of the better next-day desserts you can have in a house after a wedding. The fingers will be softer, and the cocoa may need a fresh dusting. That is a kindness at midnight or in the morning. It is also the proof that this was the right make-ahead sweet: it wanted the night in the refrigerator, and it can take another one. If you have a full unused pan still in the refrigerator, leave it there until you need it. An extra pan on the table, warming in the sun, is not hospitality. It is a dessert losing the only advantage it had, which was the cold.

I would rather finish both pans the night before, with a clean counter and a cooled pot of coffee, than try to dip a hundred ladyfingers while someone is looking for matches and a guest is asking where to stand for photographs. The work is not hard. It is only wet, and it wants a quiet half hour, and that half hour is easier to find on Friday than it is at six o’clock on Saturday.

A short tiramisu checklist

Cool the espresso. Make a mascarpone cream with whipped cream and no raw yolks. Dip the ladyfingers briefly, layer them in 9x13 pans, and chill the pans for hours or overnight. Dust the cocoa close to service, keep the dessert cold, and label the dairy, the coffee, the wheat, and any alcohol.

Do not treat tiramisu like a last-minute trifle, and do not treat a backyard like a walk-in cooler. A wedding tiramisu is a pan you can finish the day before, serve cold, and still be proud of, because the soak and the chill were part of the recipe, not a delay you failed to plan around. If you give the pans the night, and the cocoa the last minute, the dessert will taste like the thing people hoped it would be when they saw the name on the table, and you will not be in the kitchen assembling it while the toasts are already happening in the yard. That is a make-ahead dessert doing the job it was chosen for, and it is the reason to pick tiramisu for a wedding table in the first place, instead of a last-minute sweet that needs the cook during the hour when the cook should be out of the kitchen and in the backyard with everyone else already there.
