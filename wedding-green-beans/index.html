Green Beans for a Wedding Crowd: How Many to Buy, How to Make Them Ahead, and a Lemon-Almond Recipe

Green beans are the side dish that makes a wedding buffet look finished: bright, a little crisp, and easy to like. For a crowd, the trick is to blanch them a day or two ahead, chill them fast, and reheat them in the last half hour. Plan about a quarter pound of fresh beans per guest, and a little more if they're the only vegetable.

The short version. Buy about 1/4 pound of untrimmed fresh green beans per guest when they share the plate with another side, closer to 1/3 pound when they're the only vegetable. Trim them, blanch them in big batches of well-salted boiling water for three to four minutes, shock them in ice water, dry them, and refrigerate up to two days. On the day, reheat them in garlic butter, in a hot oven or a wide pan, finish with lemon and toasted almonds, and hold them hot at 135°F or above. Add the lemon juice at the very end so the green stays green.

Why green beans earn a place on the buffet

Look at a wedding buffet from the end of the line. There's a glossy main, a pan of something cheesy, rolls in a basket, and then, if someone planned well, a long pan of bright green beans with almonds scattered over the top. That pan is doing more than it looks like. It's the color on the plate. It's the thing your aunt who doesn't eat pasta will actually fill up on. And it's the side that makes a casual menu feel like dinner.

Green beans are also cheap, available all year, and forgiving. They don't need a special pan or a rental. Kids eat them. Vegetarian and gluten-free guests can eat them if you keep the recipe simple. Here's the honest part: a pound of green beans for a family dinner is a two-minute job, and twenty-five pounds for a wedding is a production. The pot is never big enough, the water stops boiling the second you add them, and an overcooked batch turns olive-gray in a chafing dish. This guide is about doing it the way caterers do: cook ahead, chill fast, reheat late.

How many green beans to buy

Most catering guides land on a cooked portion of about 3 to 4 ounces of green beans per guest. Trimming the stem ends costs you roughly 10 to 15 percent of the weight, so you buy a bit more than you serve. A buffet also runs heavier than a plated dinner; people serve themselves generously, and the last guest in line should get the same pan as the first.

Scale down a little if your crowd has a lot of small children, and up a little for a long, late reception or a crowd that's been on its feet all day. If the menu already has a salad and a second vegetable, the left column is plenty.

Fresh, frozen, or canned? Fresh beans taste best and look the brightest. Frozen cut green beans are already trimmed and blanched, which saves hours; buy about 3 1/2 to 4 ounces per guest and reheat them straight from frozen. Canned beans are the cheapest and softest, and they shine in a long-simmered Southern pot with bacon and onion. A large #10 can gives you roughly 20 to 25 half-cup servings drained, so a crowd of 100 needs five or six. All three are fine. What doesn't work is the crisp-bright look with canned beans; choose the recipe to match the can.

Where to buy: a warehouse club often sells trimmed fresh green beans in 2-pound bags, which can save an evening of snapping ends. A grocery produce department will usually order a case if you ask a week ahead. Check that the beans snap cleanly and aren't limp or rusty when you pick them up.

Trimming and storing a mountain of beans

Trimming is the slow part, and it's a great job for helpers with a glass of wine. Line up a handful of beans with the stem ends even, and cut them all at once with a sharp knife on a board. Flip, line up the tail ends if you like (many cooks leave the thin tail on; it's tender), and cut again. One person can trim about 3 to 4 pounds in 20 minutes this way, which is far faster than snapping each bean.

If you want the long, thin French-cut look that a lot of holiday green bean dishes use, a green bean frencher slices them lengthwise as you push them through. It's a fun station for a helper and makes the beans cook in a couple of minutes, but it also makes them more delicate, so they're better for a quick sauté than for a long hold.

Store trimmed raw beans unwashed, wrapped loosely in paper towels, in the refrigerator, and plan to cook them within two or three days. Wash them right before blanching. Vented produce saver containers keep piles of trimmed beans from getting crushed in a full refrigerator, and they stack, which matters when the fridge is already holding a cake and three cases of sparkling water.

Lemon-garlic green beans with toasted almonds for a crowd

This is a classic blanch-then-finish method, the same idea behind green beans almondine: the beans are cooked until just crisp-tender, chilled in ice water to lock in the color, and finished with garlic butter, lemon, and almonds right before serving. The cooking happens a day or two ahead. On the day, it's a 20-minute reheat. The recipe is written for about 24 guests as one of two sides; the second column doubles it for about 48.

Make-Ahead Lemon-Garlic Green Beans with Toasted Almonds for a Crowd

Fresh green beans blanched and chilled up to two days ahead, then reheated in garlic butter and finished with lemon zest, lemon juice, and toasted sliced almonds. About 24 servings as one of two sides; about 48 doubled.

About 24 or 48 servings · Prep 30 minutes · Blanch about 15 minutes · Reheat 10 to 15 minutes

Directions

  1. Toast the almonds (up to 3 days ahead). Spread the almonds on a sheet pan and toast at 350°F for 6 to 8 minutes, stirring once, until golden and fragrant. Cool and store airtight at room temperature.
  2. Set up. Fill a large stockpot about two-thirds with water, add the salt, and bring to a hard boil. Fill a sink or a very large bowl with ice water. Lay clean kitchen towels on the counter.
  3. Blanch in batches. Add about 2 pounds of beans at a time. Cook 3 to 4 minutes, from the moment they go in, until bright green and crisp-tender; bite one to check. Thin French-cut beans need only about 2 minutes.
  4. Shock. Lift the beans straight into the ice water with a spider strainer and leave them until completely cold, about 3 minutes. Return the water to a full boil before the next batch, and add ice as it melts.
  5. Dry and chill. Drain the beans well and spread them on the towels; roll them up gently to dry. Pack them into containers or zip-top bags lined with paper towels and refrigerate up to 2 days.
  6. Reheat (the day of). Oven method for a crowd: heat the oven to 425°F. Melt the butter with the olive oil and garlic over low heat for 2 minutes, until the garlic is fragrant but not brown. Toss the cold beans with the garlic butter, the finishing salt, and the pepper, and spread them in a single layer on two or three rimmed sheet pans. Roast 10 to 14 minutes, tossing once, until hot through, at least 165°F in the thickest spot. Stovetop method for smaller batches: warm a quarter of the garlic butter in a wide sauté pan, add a quarter of the beans, and toss over medium-high heat for 3 to 5 minutes until hot.
  7. Finish. Transfer to a warm serving pan. Grate the lemon zest over the beans, squeeze over the juice, toss, and taste for salt. Scatter the almonds on top, and a pinch of flaky salt if you like. Serve right away.

Make-ahead: the blanched, chilled beans keep 2 days in the refrigerator; the almonds keep 3 days airtight; the garlic butter can be made the day before and rewarmed. Hold reheated beans at 135°F or above in a chafing dish and bring out fresh pans in smaller amounts rather than holding one big pan for an hour; the color dulls the longer they sit hot. Add the lemon juice only at the end, because acid turns green beans olive over time. Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours. For a nut-free table, swap the almonds for toasted breadcrumbs or crisp shallots and label it.

For a double batch, two big pots of water side by side are faster than one, and a broad 6-quart sauté pan with high sides lets you finish a few pounds at a time on the stove without beans flying everywhere. A Microplane zester takes the zest off four lemons in a minute and leaves the bitter white pith behind.

If you are buying almonds for 50 or 100 guests, a bag of sliced almonds in bulk costs far less per ounce than the small baking-aisle packets, and the leftovers go straight into the granola for the morning-after brunch.

A make-ahead timeline

A week out: order the beans, or confirm a warehouse club run. Decide fresh, frozen, or canned, and decide who owns this dish on the day. Count your burners and your oven racks, and write down which pans the beans will go out in.

Three days before: toast the almonds. Buy lemons, garlic, and butter.

Two days before: trim, blanch, shock, dry, and refrigerate. This is a two-hour session for 25 pounds with two people and two pots.

The day before: make the garlic butter and refrigerate it in a jar. Label the bean containers with the batch and the pan they're going into.

The day of: the beans go into the oven about 20 minutes before the buffet opens, after the main is out and resting. If dinner is at 6:00, the sheet pans go in at 5:35, the first pan goes out at 5:55, and the second round goes in when the first pan is half gone.

Keeping them hot and green on the line

Green beans are at their best right after they come out of the oven. They're still very good after 30 minutes in a chafing dish and noticeably tired after an hour. So don't put out the whole batch at once. Serve them in half pans, keep the next pans ready in the kitchen, and refill when the pan is a third full. Keep everything you put out at 135°F or above, and don't top up a half-empty pan with fresh beans; swap the pan.

If the kitchen is in one house and the reception is in another yard, an insulated carrier holds a full hotel pan of hot food for the drive. Better still, transport the beans cold and reheat them on site if there's an oven. Our wedding buffet setup guide covers line order, pan sizes, and labels, and the backyard wedding power plan matters if you're adding a roaster or warming trays to a circuit that's already running a coffee urn.

Put the beans after the main and before the starch, so people see them before their plate is full of macaroni and cheese. A small card that says "lemon-garlic green beans with almonds" heads off the nut question at the front of the line.

Other green bean recipes for a crowd

Southern-style green beans. Canned or frozen beans simmered low for an hour or more with bacon or smoked turkey, onion, a little broth, and pepper. They're soft, deeply savory, and almost impossible to overcook, which makes them a good choice if the dish has to sit in a slow cooker on the buffet for two hours. Budget a pound of bacon for about 25 servings.

Green bean bundles. Five or six blanched beans wrapped with a half slice of bacon and baked with a brown sugar and butter glaze. They look charming and feel like a party, but they're fiddly: 100 guests means 100 bundles. Save them for a small wedding or a rehearsal dinner.

Green bean casserole. The Thanksgiving classic, built in 9x13 pans, about 12 to 15 servings each. It holds well and many guests love it, but it's heavy next to a rich main. If your menu is ham, pot roast, or anything creamy, the bright lemon version above is the better partner.

Roasted green beans. Raw beans tossed with oil and salt and roasted at 425°F until blistered. Delicious, but you can't do the work ahead, and you need a lot of oven space at the last minute, which is exactly what a wedding kitchen doesn't have.

What goes with them

Green beans go with almost every wedding main: glazed ham, roast chicken, pot roast, baked ziti, or a vegetarian pasta. What to skip

Skip crowding the pot; too many beans at once means the water stops boiling and the beans steam to gray. Skip the ice bath at your peril, because it's what keeps them bright. Skip adding lemon juice the night before. Skip holding one giant pan for the whole dinner. And skip a complicated garnish; toasted almonds and a little lemon zest look like you meant it.

Green beans for a crowd: quick answers

How many pounds of green beans do I need for 100 guests?

About 25 pounds of fresh, untrimmed green beans when they're one of two or more sides, and about 32 to 34 pounds when they're the only vegetable. That works out to a cooked portion of roughly 3 to 4 ounces per guest after trimming.

Can I make green beans ahead of time for a wedding?

Yes. Blanch them in boiling salted water for 3 to 4 minutes, chill them in ice water, dry them, and refrigerate up to 2 days. Reheat them in garlic butter in a hot oven or a wide pan just before serving.

How do I keep green beans hot on a buffet?

Hold them at 135°F or above in a chafing dish, serve them in smaller pans, and replace pans rather than topping them up. Add lemon juice at the last minute so the color holds.

A short green bean checklist

Buy about 1/4 pound per guest, or 1/3 pound if they're the only vegetable. Trim in bunches, not one at a time. Blanch in big pots in batches, shock in ice water, dry, and refrigerate up to two days. Reheat in garlic butter at 425°F, finish with lemon and toasted almonds, and send them out in smaller pans. Then watch the pan come back empty.
