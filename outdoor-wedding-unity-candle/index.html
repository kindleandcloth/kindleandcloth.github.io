A Unity Candle at an Outdoor Wedding: A Flame, a Shield, and a Backup

A unity candle at an outdoor wedding is a flame plan: a short cue, a weight or a hurricane, and an honest backup if the wind will not allow a wick to stay lit. It is not required, and it is not the only way to mark the same moment.

The short version. If you want a unity candle outdoors, put it on a small, steady table, in a hurricane or behind a real wind guard, and light a test in that yard, at the real hour. Keep the cue short. Have a way to put the flame out, and a backup if the wick will not stay lit — a single shared taper indoors later, or a different ritual you already have words for, such as a handfasting or a ring warming. Do not make a struggling flame the center of the ceremony.

A flame outdoors is a plan, not a symbol you can ignore the weather for

The useful purchases are the ones that keep a wick lit, or give you a different object to use. A unity candle set is only useful if you can shield it with a glass hurricane or set it in a weighted holder that will not walk. A small stand or a sturdy table keeps the moment off a folding tray. A long lighter is kinder than a short match in a breeze, and drip protectors help if you are using family tapers. A lantern-style hurricane is a sturdier shield than a thin glass alone. A sand set is an option if you would rather pour than manage a flame. That is the kit. None of it is a promise that the wind will cooperate.

A unity candle is a short ritual some couples like: two tapers light a larger candle, or a couple lights one candle together, as a way of marking that two households are making one. It is familiar, it photographs clearly, and it can be done in a minute or two with a sentence from the officiant. Outdoors, it is also a wick in moving air, sometimes on a lawn, sometimes on a porch, often at the exact hour when a breeze comes up. This post is about whether to use one in a yard, how to give the flame a chance, and what to do instead if the test says no. It is not a religious ruling, and it is not a claim that you must have a ritual of this kind at all.

The ceremony guide already asks you to keep the program short and to give it a clear shape. A unity moment is an optional shape in the middle, after the vows or just before them, not a second ceremony. If you add one, write a cue the officiant can actually say in a breeze, and practice the lighting once, with the real candles, so no one is learning to find a wick while everyone watches. The friend officiant guide is useful here if the person with the script is not a professional. Put the cue on paper, in the folder they already hold.

You do not have to use a candle because a parent expects one. You can say, kindly, that the yard will not hold a flame, and that you will do something else, or nothing, at that moment in the ceremony. You can also use the family tapers to light a candle at a dinner table later, or at a parent’s house the next morning, if the meaning matters more than the timing. What does not work is a long, awkward attempt to relight a wick six times while guests wait in the sun.

If the ceremony is indoors, even in a living room, the wind problem mostly goes away, and this post is less of a concern. If you are on a porch, under a tent, or on an open lawn, treat the flame as a weather object, the way you already treat an arch or a runner. The weather plan is the broader reading for wind and storms. This is the smaller, specific version: one wick, one table, one minute of the day.

A table, a hurricane, and a test in that yard

Put the candles on a small, steady table, not on the ground, not on a chair, and not on a surface a hem will sweep. The table should be close enough that the couple can reach it in a step or two, and far enough from a runner, a dry hedge, and a hanging branch that a flare or a tip does not become a fire. Have a way to put the flame out — a snuffer, a lid, a wet cloth in a bowl, something more deliberate than hope. A small fire extinguisher in the ordinary house kit is not a ceremony prop, and it is not a bad thing to know the location of on any day you have open flame in a yard.

A hurricane or a tall glass cylinder around the unity candle will do more than a decorative holder with no shield. Tapers used to light it are harder to shield; light them carefully, keep them in holders, and do not walk them through a crowd. Some couples light the family tapers at the seats and walk them forward. That can be lovely, and it is also two moving flames. Practice the path. If it feels fussy in a still room, it will feel worse in a breeze.

Test the whole setup outdoors, at the real hour, in that yard. Light the candles, say the cue, and see whether the wick stays lit for the length of the moment plus a little longer. A kitchen test the night before will not tell you about the wind at the edge of a lawn. If the candle will not stay lit with a hurricane on a typical day, it will not stay lit at the ceremony unless you move it to a porch, a tent wall, or inside. Believe the test. Do not assume the day will be still because you would like it to be.

Drip is a dress problem and a table problem. Protectors on tapers, a tray under the holder, and a cloth you do not care about on the table will save a hem and a painted surface. Keep hair, veils, and loose sleeves in mind when someone leans in to light. This is ordinary caution, the same way you would not lean over a stove in a long sleeve. Say it once in the rehearsal, not as a scold in the moment.

After the lighting, decide whether the candle stays lit for the rest of the ceremony or is put out. A flame left unattended on a lawn, near a dress and a row of guests, is a poor idea even if it looks pretty. Many couples light, pause, and snuff, and let the meaning live in the minute they already had. If you want it to burn longer, put it on a monitored table, in a real vessel, away from the recessional path, and assign a person to put it out before anyone moves the furniture.

A short cue, and what to do if the flame goes out

The cue should be a few sentences: an invitation to step forward, one line about what the lighting means to you, the lighting itself, and a close that leads back to the vows or to a kiss or to the next thing. It should be on paper. It should not be a poem so long that a breeze has time to win. If a parent is lighting a taper, tell them when, and whether they stay or sit. The rehearsal is the time to find out that three people cannot fit around a small table.

If the flame goes out during the ceremony, do not make a show of fixing it unless a single relight will obviously work. A backup is better: a second wick already prepared, a move to a more sheltered edge of the table, or a line the officiant already has, such as “we will light this at the table later,” and then they go on. Guests will follow a calm sentence. They will also follow a long, silent struggle, and that is the clip people will remember. Write the backup line in the same folder as the cue.

Photographers will want a clear view of hands and a wick. Give them a place to stand that is not in the family, and do not build a wall of people around the table. The moment is short. If it has to be perfect on camera, practice it, and put the table where the light already works, not in deep shade under a tree at dusk or in hard glare at ten in the morning.

Children and pets should not be the people next to an open flame unless you have a real plan for that, which is usually no. A unity moment can include a child in the words, or in a hand on a table that has already been snuffed, more safely than in the lighting itself. The dog guide is the longer reading if an animal is part of the day. A candle is not an invitation for a tail and a hem to meet a taper.

If you are using family candles with a history, say so in a sentence, and still put them in holders that will stand. Sentiment does not stabilize a thin taper. If a candle is too precious to drip or to fail in public, use it later, at a table, and use a simpler set for the yard.

Alternatives, and when to skip the flame

If the test fails, or if you simply do not want a flame in the grass, you already have good alternatives on this site. A handfasting is cords or ribbons, a short cue, and a wrap you have practiced in the wind. A ring warming is a walk of the rings through the seats, or through a small set of hands, with a sentence about what people are invited to do. Either one is a real moment, and neither one asks a wick to work. You can also plant something, pour sand, or simply hold a look and a sentence and sit down. The ceremony does not owe anyone a prop.

Sand is popular outdoors because it does not blow out. It can blow, in a different way, if the day is dry and gusty, so a tray and a careful pour still matter. Use a set you have practiced, on a steady table, and do not pour at the edge of a beige dress in a hard wind. If sand is not your language, do not use it just because it is easier than a candle. Easier is not the same as yours.

Skipping the extra ritual is allowed, and often wise, when the ceremony is already short, the yard is already doing a lot, or the meaning you wanted is already in the vows. A unity moment that is only on the program because a template included it will feel like a template. If you skip it, you do not have to replace it. You can go from vows to a kiss to a walk back down the path, and that is a complete ceremony.

If a parent is disappointed, tell them early, and offer the later lighting, or a place in the words, or a seat that matters. Do not surprise them in the yard with a missing candle they expected to light. Kindness is a sentence in a week-of conversation, not a debate at the table during the processional.

Whatever you choose, keep it in proportion. The vows are the work of the ceremony. A candle, a cord, or a pour is a short mark, if you want one, and then the day goes on. The yard will be more forgiving if you treat flame as flame.

A short unity-candle checklist

Decide whether you want a flame at all. If you do, test the actual candles in that yard, at the real hour, in a hurricane or a weighted holder, on a steady table. Write a short cue, and a backup line if the wick goes out. Practice the lighting once. Have a way to put the flame out, and move or snuff it before the recessional. If the test fails, choose an alternative you already have words for, or skip the extra ritual.

A unity candle can be a lovely, familiar minute in a backyard ceremony, and it can also be a small, public fight with the weather. The difference is a test, a shield, a short script, and a willingness to change the plan before the guests are seated. If you treat the flame as part of the yard, people will remember the sentence and the lighting. If you treat it as a symbol that should work because it worked indoors at a rehearsal, they will remember the relighting. That work is ordinary, and it is worth doing while you can still move the table or cut the ritual, instead of discovering the breeze when everyone is already standing.
