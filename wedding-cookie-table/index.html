A wedding cookie table is a dessert you can build in many kitchens, not a second bakery order. Decide how many kinds you actually need, bake or collect on a schedule, and put out a table people can walk up to without a server standing over it.

The short version. A cookie table works when it is a planned dessert, not a last-minute pile of mixed leftovers. Count two to four cookies per guest if this is the main sweet, fewer if you also have cake. Three to five kinds is plenty; one of them can be a lemon knot cookie you bake ahead and glaze the day of. Use sturdy platters, label anything with nuts or gluten, and keep a closed backup tray so the table does not look picked over. If you cannot bake, a cookie table can still be a collection from relatives, with one person in charge of the grid, not a dozen people arriving with unmarked tins. Pack leftovers in a way you can actually send home. Skip a table that is only for photographs.

What a cookie table is, and whether you need one

Keep the shopping list short, and let it follow the plan rather than a mood board. A cookie table is a spread of many cookies, often homemade, set out so guests can take a few after dinner or on the way out. In some Italian-American families it is the dessert, with pride attached. In a backyard wedding it can also be a practical way to serve something sweet, or to let relatives cook instead of mailing a check. It is not automatically required, and it is not the same as a dessert table with a cake and a row of extras.

You need one if someone will actually fill it, and if you want dessert to be finger food that can wait on a platter. You do not need one if the only baker is also the person getting married at two o'clock, or if the kitchen cannot hold the trays. A small cake, a tray of brownies, or coffee and one kind of cookie will not look like a failure. It will look like a wedding that did not take on a second production.

If a parent or a grandparent considers the cookie table part of the marriage, have that conversation early, and give them a real job. If no one in your family has ever made one, you can still do a modest version without pretending you inherited a method you did not. The lemon knot cookies below are in that spirit: a citrus cookie a home kitchen can bake in a crowd batch, not a recreation of anyone's exact family tin.

Think about weather and ants. A backyard table in July needs shade, lids or a mesh cover, and a plan to refresh platters rather than leave icing in the sun. In cooler months the table is easier, but you still need a surface that is not the same folding table as the drinks. Put the cookie table where people will naturally walk after dinner.

How many cookies, and how to fill the table

If the cookie table is the dessert, plan on two to four cookies per guest, plus a little extra for the people who take a box at the end. If you also have cake, you can come down toward one or two. A list of eighty with cake and cookies does not need four hundred cookies. A closed backup tray matters more than a fifth flavor.

Three to five kinds will look like a table. Ten kinds will look like a potluck that got out of hand, and they will not all be eaten equally. Include at least one cookie without nuts, and at least one that can sit out without a filling that spoils. If someone has a gluten-free need you know about, give that cookie its own plate and a label, not a corner of a mixed platter.

If relatives are contributing, appoint one coordinator. Send a short note: we would love cookies; here are the kinds we still need; please drop them off at this time, in a labeled tin we can return. A coordinator can also buy a kind if a promised tin does not arrive. That is how the table stays full.

Baking in a home kitchen is easier if you choose cookies that freeze or hold. Lemon knots, a simple butter cookie, a chocolate cookie without a perishable filling, and a nut cookie on its own plate will travel. Frosted cookies with soft buttercream are fussier in a warm yard. Use half-sheet pans for baking and staging, and cotton towels to cover cooling racks without trapping steam against icing. A long wooden board can hold one kind at the table. Sturdy crates can lift a platter if they are stable and not a trip hazard.

Labels can be small. A simple card holder and a short name — “lemon knots, no nuts,” “chocolate, contains nuts” — is enough. A plain linen runner will make a borrowed table look finished. Skip a sign that requires a paragraph of family history.

Lemon knot cookies for a crowd

Italian-American lemon knot cookies — sometimes called anginetti, and baked for holidays and for cookie tables — are a soft, glazed citrus cookie, tied in a loose knot, pale on top and only just gold at the bottom. They are a good wedding cookie because they keep, they do not need a filling, and they look like you meant to make them, even if the knots are a little uneven. This version is an oil-based dough, scented with lemon zest and juice, baked on sheet pans, and glazed with a thin lemon icing. It is not any one family's recipe. It is a crowd batch you can make in a home kitchen and write down for the next person who asks.

The dough should be soft but not sticky enough to glue itself to the counter. If it slumps, add a spoonful of flour. If it cracks when you roll a rope, knead in a teaspoon of milk or a few drops of oil. Roll ropes about the thickness of a finger, cut six-inch lengths, and tie a loose overhand knot, tucking the ends under a little so they do not burn. They will puff in the oven. Do not pull the knot tight.

Bake on parchment-lined half-sheet pans, in batches, until the bottoms are lightly colored and the tops are still pale. Overbaking makes them dry, and a cookie table rewards a soft bite. Cool until just warm, then dip the tops in a lemon glaze that runs slowly off a spoon. Set them back on a rack until the glaze is dry. If you want a little color, add a pinch of zest to the glaze, not a heavy sprinkle that will fall off the platter.

Make the dough a day ahead if you want, wrapped and refrigerated, then bring it back toward cool room temperature so it will roll. Baked, unglazed cookies freeze well; glaze them the day you need them. Glazed cookies keep in an airtight container at room temperature for a couple of days, with parchment between layers. Do not stack them while the icing is still tacky.

Lemon Knot Cookies for a Wedding Cookie Table

A soft, glazed lemon knot cookie baked on sheet pans. The batch makes about fifty cookies; double it for a hundred. Glaze when the cookies are just warm, and let the icing set before you pack them.

About 50 cookies (double for about 100) · Prep 45 minutes · Bake 12 to 15 minutes per tray · Total about 2 hours, including glazing and cooling

  1. Heat the oven to 350°F. Line half-sheet pans with parchment. Whisk the flour, baking powder, and salt in a bowl.
  2. In a large bowl, whisk the eggs and sugar until the mixture looks a little thick and pale. Whisk in the oil, lemon zest, 3 tablespoons lemon juice (6 for the larger batch), and vanilla.
  3. Add the dry ingredients and stir with a spatula, then with a clean hand, until you have a soft dough that holds together and is only slightly tacky. If it sticks badly, dust in a spoonful of flour. If it cracks, knead in a teaspoon of milk. Let it rest 10 minutes.
  4. Pinch off pieces of dough and roll into ropes about the thickness of a finger and about 6 inches long. Tie each rope in a loose overhand knot and tuck the ends underneath slightly. Set the knots a little apart on the pans.
  5. Bake one tray at a time, or two if your oven bakes evenly, for 12 to 15 minutes, until the bottoms are lightly golden and the tops are set and still pale. Cool on the pan for 5 minutes, then move to a rack.
  6. When the cookies are just warm, whisk the powdered sugar with enough lemon juice to make a glaze that slowly falls from a spoon. Dip the tops, or spoon a little glaze over each knot, and return them to the rack until the icing is dry. Store airtight, in layers separated by parchment, once the glaze has set.

This is one kind on a mixed cookie table, not the only dessert for a hundred people unless you bake more batches or add other cookies. The dough can be refrigerated overnight, well wrapped; let it soften slightly before you roll. Unglazed cookies freeze well for a few weeks. If you want a nut-free table, keep these on their own platters and wash the pans before any cookie that contains nuts.

How to set, label, and pack up

Set the table late enough that the sun and the flies have less time to work, and early enough that it is ready when dinner ends. Hold a third of the cookies back and refill, so the table still looks generous later.

Put small plates or napkins at both ends, and a box or bag if you want people to take cookies home. Some families consider the take-home box part of the tradition; some do not. If you offer it, have more containers than you think, and a marker. Do not use the same crate you used for welcome bags unless you have cleaned it and it will not smell like snacks.

Assign one person to watch the table the way you would watch a coffee urn: not to hover, but to refill, to pull a platter that has been in the sun, and to cover the table if the wind comes up. If children are around, put the sturdier cookies where a small hand can reach without pulling down a stand.

At the end of the night, pack what is left by kind, not in one mixed tub. Send tins home with the people who baked, and send a few plates with overnight guests. Cookies that have sat out for hours in heat should be judged honestly; if you would not eat one, do not pack it as a gift. Wash the platters before you go to bed if you can.

A short cookie-table checklist

A month out, decide whether you are baking, collecting, or skipping the table, and name a coordinator if other people will contribute. Two weekends out, bake anything that freezes. The day before, glaze, plate a first round, and clear the table you will use.

The day of, keep backup trays closed, set the table in the shade, and label nuts and gluten. Refill once or twice, then pack what is still good. A cookie table is a generous dessert when someone can finish it. If that person is you, keep the kinds few and the schedule early. If it is a parent who has been waiting to do this, give them the platters and your thanks.

Decide early whether dessert will be a table of cookies, and then fill that choice. A platter of lemon knots, two other kinds, and a backup tray is a complete dessert.

If you are also building a coffee bar, put the two stations near each other without making one person run both.
