There's a particular kind of evening you only get in a backyard: the lights coming on over the grass, the house glowing behind the tables, somebody laughing at the far end. That's the part everyone is picturing. Here's the thing — that easy feeling still takes a real plan, because there's no banquet manager on site. Parking, restrooms, power, neighbors, and rain are yours to sort, and they're all solvable if you put them on a list early.

We love a wedding at home. You probably do too. This is the checklist we want on your fridge, from six months out through the Monday after. It assumes a private home — yours, a parent's, or a friend's — and a guest list the yard can actually hold. Still deciding how small to go? Start with our small and micro wedding planning checklist.

Six months or more before

  • Walk the property at the time of day you want to say your vows and take photos
  • Measure the usable lawn, patio, and indoor backup space, and sketch a rough layout
  • Confirm the homeowner is fully on board, including cleanup, overnight guests, and insurance
  • Look up noise ordinances, parking rules, tent and assembly permits, and any HOA or lease restrictions
  • Set a hard guest-count ceiling based on seating, restrooms, and parking, not on who might be offended
  • Decide ceremony, dinner, and dancing locations (they can share a space if you plan a flip)
  • Get quotes for a tent, restroom trailer or luxury portable restrooms, tables, and chairs
  • Ask your homeowner's insurance about a one-day event liability policy
  • Book the photographer and officiant; small weekends go quietly
  • Look up marriage license rules, waiting periods, and expiration dates for the county

The walk-through is not optional. You're looking for slope, soft ground, sprinkler heads, low branches, the path from the street, and where the sun will be in your eyes. Note every outdoor outlet and which indoor circuit it shares. If the yard is tight, it is better to cut the guest list now than to discover, in September, that you can't fit dinner and a dance floor.

Permits catch people off guard. Many towns care about tents over a certain size, amplified music after a certain hour, and street parking. If you rent, the lease or HOA may forbid large gatherings altogether. Make the call or submit the form yourself; don't assume a parent “already checked.”

Four to five months before

  • Book the tent (or another firm weather plan) and ask for the decision date on sidewalls
  • Book tables, chairs, linens, and any dance floor or flooring
  • Decide how you'll feed people and book the caterer, restaurant drop-off, or food truck; see budget wedding food ideas
  • Plan restrooms: home bathrooms only for a tiny list, otherwise rent a restroom trailer
  • Sketch power needs: lights, music, catering equipment, coffee, fans or heaters
  • If house circuits can't handle the load, get a generator quote
  • Send save the dates if anyone is traveling, or set up a simple wedding website
  • Start a parking plan: street, a nearby lot, carpools, or a shuttle from a church or school lot
  • Ask a nearby neighbor, in person, about the date and the music end time

Restrooms are the item couples underestimate most. A single home bathroom is miserable for 60 people, and many hosts don't want guests wandering through the house. A restroom trailer costs money, but it protects the house and the guest experience. Book it when you book the tent; the good ones go early in peak season.

Power is the other quiet budget line. String lights, a speaker, chafing dishes, a coffee urn, and a soft-serve machine will happily trip a garage circuit at 7 p.m. Count watts, use outdoor-rated extension cords, and plug lights into GFCI-protected outlets. An outdoor timer or smart plug means nobody is crawling under a table at dusk. If the math is tight, rent a quiet generator and put it far from the ceremony with the cord run safely.

Two to three months before

  • Send invitations with a clear RSVP date; digital tracking helps on a moving family property
  • Share parking, weather, and shoe guidance on the invite or wedding website
  • Finalize the layout to scale, including buffer around tent stakes and guy lines
  • Order or rent the ceremony backdrop, aisle markers, and signage
  • Plan lighting in three layers: overhead, table, and paths; test a sample strand on site
  • Arrange trash, recycling, and (if you need it) a cigarette station so the lawn doesn't become a bin
  • Hire or officially appoint day-of help: a coordinator, a calm friend, or both
  • Confirm who is locking up pets and valuables, and where vendors may stage

Invitations for a backyard wedding should do more than name the date. Tell people if they will be on grass, if the ceremony is uncovered, and where to park. “Garden party, comfortable shoes” prevents a row of sinking heels. This is also the moment to decide what you'll rent rather than buy. Tables, chairs, linens, and an arch are almost always cheaper and kinder to your garage when they arrive and leave on a truck. If you want the styled layer (signs, backdrops, tabletop decor) without a post-wedding selling spree, look at a decor rental service such as RENT MY WEDDING.

One month before

  • Apply for the marriage license inside the valid window
  • Confirm final counts, arrival times, and access notes with every vendor
  • Share a simple one-page timeline with vendors and your day-of helper
  • Make a shot list of must-have photos, including the house and any family ritual
  • Buy or borrow the unglamorous kit: extension cords, gaffer tape, zip ties, a power strip, a basic tool kit, stain wipes, bunches of paper towels, and a small first-aid kit
  • Set up a guest comfort basket: bug spray, sunscreen, throw blankets if evenings cool off, and a basket of spare flip-flops or heel stops
  • Plan seating, even if it is just “family in the first two rows” and a kids’ table in the shade
  • Order or pick up a guest book or a framed print people can sign
  • Confirm the rain and heat plan in writing with the photographer and caterer

Write access notes as if the vendor has never been there: gate codes, which driveway to use, where to unload, which hose is potable, and who to text on arrival. A food truck that circles the block for twenty minutes will remember you for the wrong reason.

The week of the wedding

  • Mow two or three days out, then stay off the sprinklers so the lawn can dry
  • Mark sprinkler heads, holes, and tent stake positions with flags
  • Hang and test all lights at night; replace dead bulbs now
  • Set up any arch, signage, and path lights you are doing yourselves
  • Clear indoor spaces vendors or grandparents may need, and empty a hall closet for purses
  • Prep vendor payments and tips in labeled envelopes
  • Give the license and rings to one reliable person
  • Check the forecast twice a day and make the tent-sidewall or furniture-move call by the deadline you were given
  • Tell neighbors the start and end times once more, and offer them a parking plan if the street will fill
  • Stage trash and recycling where guests will actually use them, not behind the garage

Don't hang the lights on the morning of the wedding. Night testing is the only way to see dark corners, glare in someone’s eyes, and the one strand that died in the box. Pathway lighting to the restrooms is a safety issue, not just a pretty one. For more on lighting and tables, use our outdoor wedding decor ideas.

The day of

  • Eat breakfast and assign one person (not the couple) as the vendor contact
  • Walk the guest path from parking to ceremony to dinner and remove tripping hazards
  • Confirm the officiant and witnesses will sign the license correctly
  • Place water, sunscreen, and bug spray where people can help themselves
  • Do a restroom check before guests arrive
  • Keep pets secured and a plan for someone to let them out later
  • After dinner, have a helper quietly swap full trash bags so the yard stays pleasant
  • Collect cards, gifts, and the guest book before anyone starts packing cars

The Monday after

  • Return rented goods on time and photograph any damage disputes before the truck leaves
  • Walk the yard for lingering glass, florals, cable ties, and stake holes
  • Water the lawn if it took a beating, and thank the neighbors with a note or leftovers
  • Return the signed license if your officiant is not filing it
  • Start thank-you notes while the day is still fresh

A few decisions that deserve their own pause

How many people can this yard actually hold?

A working rule for a seated dinner is to leave generous room around every table and a clear path for servers. A 6-foot rectangular table wants more than its own footprint; guests need to push back their chairs. Add the tent’s stake and sidewall margin (often several feet beyond the canopy). If the number only fits on paper, cut the list. A slightly smaller wedding in a yard that can breathe will feel more expensive than a squeeze.

When do you need a tent even if the forecast is clear?

If you can't move everyone indoors comfortably, you need a reserved weather plan. A sunny morning doesn't help at 5 p.m. when a front moves through. Heat and hard sun are reasons to tent too: shade over dinner is a gift. Read the sizing and add-on math in our tent rental cost guide before you accept the first quote.

Do you need extra insurance?

Homeowner policies often exclude or cap “business-like” events, and they may not cover a rented tent that damages a buried line. Ask the carrier, in writing, whether you need a one-day event liability policy and whether vendors should name the homeowner as additional insured. Reputable tent and restroom companies carry their own insurance; still ask for a certificate. If you're using a friend’s property, put the agreement in a short note: who pays for turf repair, who is home for load-in, and when the last rental leaves.

Who is actually running the day?

Someone other than the couple should hold the vendor phone, the license, the timeline, and a printed contact list. That can be a hired day-of coordinator or a calm friend who wants this job and no other job. Give them authority to make the rain call, to tell the DJ the end time, and to move a table if the tent crew needs the path. A backyard wedding without a point person turns into a family meeting every time a truck arrives.

Quick recap you can print

  • Walk and measure the yard at ceremony time
  • Confirm permits, HOA or lease rules, and insurance
  • Set the guest cap from space, restrooms, and parking
  • Book tent, tables, chairs, and restrooms early
  • Plan power, including a generator if needed
  • Tell neighbors the date and the music end time
  • Put parking, shoes, and weather notes on the invitation
  • Test lights at night a week out
  • Give one person the vendor phone and the license
  • Schedule cleanup and rental returns before you leave for a honeymoon

The pretty part of a backyard wedding is real. It's just easier to feel when the unromantic work is already on a list. Start with the yard you actually have, book the big pieces early, and let the lights — and the people you love — do the rest.
