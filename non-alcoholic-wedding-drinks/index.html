Non-Alcoholic Wedding Drinks That Aren't an Afterthought

Non-alcoholic wedding drinks should be as considered as the wine, not a warm can of soda at the end of the table. Set a real station: water, something sparkling, and a make-ahead citrus-cranberry punch you can top with ginger ale when guests walk up.

The short version. Plan non-alcoholic drinks as a station, not as an afterthought. Everyone needs water. Many guests will want something that feels like a drink, not a child's juice. A citrus-cranberry punch base can be mixed the day before, kept cold, and finished at the table with ginger ale and ice so it stays lively. Put it in a labeled dispenser, keep a backup in a cooler, and offer a second option that is not the punch — sparkling water, a soft drink, or iced tea. Skip a single open bowl that sits in the sun, and skip making the punch so sweet that only children will drink it. If you have a bar, place the non-alcoholic drinks where they are as easy to reach as the wine.

Treat the non-alcoholic drinks as a real station

If you are going to buy anything for this, buy the tools the work actually uses. A beverage dispensers, which is the actual tool this part of the day uses, not a generic stand-in, a beverage cooler, a beverage dispenser glass setter, a drink dispenser spigot replacement, a punch bowl glass, only if you have a ladle and a way to keep the bowl out of the sun, a coffee urn 55 cup, a hot chocolate dispenser, and a non alcoholic drink dispenser, for water, tea, or a non-alcoholic drink that should not live in a jug with no ladle.

A surprising number of people at a wedding are not drinking, or not drinking much: designated drivers, pregnant guests, teenagers, guests who do not drink for any number of reasons, and people who simply want water in a warm yard. If the only option is a warm bottle at the end of the bar, you have made those guests wait in the same line and then given them less. Put the non-alcoholic drinks where they are obvious, and stock them as if they were the main bar for part of the room — because for part of the room, they are. The same care belongs at the toast: set a flute of sparkling water or punch on every tray so no one is empty-handed during the speeches. If you are also pouring wine, a batched sangria can sit in a second dispenser without turning the table into a cocktail list.

You do not need a list of twelve mocktails. Individual shaken drinks require a bartender and a pace you may not have. What you need is water that is cold, a sparkling option with no sugar, and one mixed drink that feels finished — a punch, a spritz you can pour from a dispenser, or iced tea with lemon. If you want a second mixed option, make it another batch, not a second menu on a chalkboard.

Place the station in the shade, on a stable table, with cups, a bin for empties, and a way to keep ice from melting into the punch. Glass dispensers with a spigot are easier than a ladle in a wide bowl, and they keep leaves and fruit from being scooped into a cup. A hard cooler behind the table can hold backup base, extra ginger ale, and ice. Sturdy glasses or a stack of clear cups both work; choose one and stay with it so you are not washing two systems. Small cards should say what is in the dispenser, including whether it has caffeine or citrus, in words a guest can read without asking.

If you also have wine and beer, do not hide the punch behind the bottles. Set it to one side, or on its own table nearby, so a guest does not have to interrupt a pour to ask for water. The same person can restock both sides if the list is small; on a larger list, give the non-alcoholic station its own helper for the first hour.

A make-ahead punch that can wait

The punch that survives a wedding is the one you do not have to build cup by cup. Mix a tart, cold base the day before — cranberry, orange, and lemon, sweetened only enough to be pleasant — and keep it refrigerated. At the station, you pour the base over ice and top it with ginger ale so the drink stays bright. If you mix the ginger ale in the night before, you will serve a flat, sweet juice in the afternoon.

This is an old punch structure, not a party shortcut of sherbet scooped into ginger ale, which can be fine at a shower and is messy and very sweet at a wedding. The point of a base is control: you can taste it, adjust the sugar, and chill it hard. Guests who want it lighter can add more ginger ale. Guests who want it less sweet can add a splash of sparkling water from the bottle beside the dispenser.

Keep fruit simple. A few orange and lemon slices in the dispenser look like hospitality; a dense raft of berries will clog the spigot and go soft. Add the slices when you set the station, not the night before. If you want rosemary or mint, put a sprig in some glasses, not a handful in the batch, where it can turn bitter.

Quantity is easier if you also serve water and a plain sparkling drink. For fifty, about two gallons of finished punch is a solid option, not the only liquid on the property. For a hundred, double it, and use two dispensers so a line does not form at one spigot. If the day is very hot, or the punch is clearly popular, you will be glad of the cooler of extra base.

Citrus-cranberry punch base for a crowd

The base is mostly juice, a little fresh lemon, and a modest simple syrup you can hold back if the cranberry is already sweet. Taste it cold, not warm, before you decide it needs more sugar. It should be tart enough that ginger ale makes it friendly rather than cloying. Mix the syrup from equal parts sugar and hot water, cooled, so you are not stirring granules in a cold jug.

Use 100% juices if you can, and avoid a cranberry cocktail that is already a punch on its own. Fresh lemon juice matters more than fresh orange juice; bottled orange is acceptable if it tastes like orange, not like concentrate. Strain the lemon juice. Combine everything in a clean pitcher or a food-safe container, chill it overnight, and stir it before you pour it into the dispenser.

At the table, fill the dispenser no more than two-thirds with cold base, add ice and a few citrus slices, and top with cold ginger ale in roughly equal volume, or a little less if you want a stronger fruit flavor. Stir once, gently. Refill from the cooler in the same ratio, rather than topping up with only ginger ale as the afternoon goes on. Keep spare ginger ale cold; warm soda will flatten the batch.

Citrus-Cranberry Punch Base for a Wedding

A tart cranberry, orange, and lemon base you mix the day before, then finish at the station with cold ginger ale and ice. The smaller batch makes about two gallons of punch, a solid non-alcoholic option for fifty when water is also on the table.

  1. Stir the cranberry juice, orange juice, and lemon juice together in a clean pitcher or container. Add 1/2 cup of the simple syrup (1 cup for the larger batch), taste the mixture cold, and add more syrup only if it is sharply tart. It should still be brighter than a finished punch.
  2. Cover and refrigerate overnight, or for at least four hours. Keep the ginger ale cold and unopened.
  3. When you set the station, stir the base and pour it into a dispenser until the vessel is about two-thirds full. Add ice and a few citrus slices. Pour in cold ginger ale to fill, starting with a roughly equal volume of soda to base, then taste. Stir once, gently.
  4. Refill from the cooler in the same ratio, rather than adding only soda as the level drops. Keep a pitcher of unsweetened sparkling water beside the dispenser for anyone who wants the punch lighter, and keep drinking water on the table as its own clearly labeled option.

This is a non-alcoholic station drink, not the only beverage you should offer. Do not add ginger ale until you are ready to serve, or the punch will go flat. If you want a less sweet finish, use club soda for part of the ginger ale. Keep the dispenser in the shade, and do not leave a dairied or sherbet-style punch sitting out in a warm yard.

Water, ice, and the rest of the table

Water should be as easy to pour as the punch. A second dispenser, or bottles in a tub of ice, will be used more than you expect, especially between the ceremony and dinner. Label it. People should not have to ask whether the clear dispenser is water or soda water. If you are also running a wine and beer bar, put water on both tables if they are far apart.

Ice is a plan, not a bag in a sink. You need ice for glasses, ice for the dispensers, and ice in the cooler. In a warm backyard, underestimate this once and you will spend the afternoon sending someone to a store. Keep drink ice and cooling ice separate if you can, so the cubes in a glass have not been sitting on a bag of raw food.

Cups, napkins, and a trash or tub for empties are part of the station. So is a towel for the puddle that will form under the spigot. A crate can corral extra cups and bottles under the table if it is stable and out of the path. A runner is optional; a level table is not.

Coffee, tea, and hot cocoa belong later, after dinner, unless you are hosting a morning wedding. There is already a guide to a wedding coffee bar; do not try to run a hot station and a punch station in the same two feet of table at four o'clock. If children are present, keep a small, clearly separate option for them — water, a juice you have actually chosen, not an open bowl of punch they have to lean over.

Taste the punch when you set it, and again an hour later. If it has gone weak from ice, stir in more cold base, not more syrup. If a dispenser runs out during a toast, wait until the toast is over, then refill. A station that is quietly stocked will be used. A station that is empty, unlabeled, or dripping onto a guest's shoes will be avoided, and those guests will go looking for a kitchen sink.

A short non-alcoholic drinks checklist

A week out, decide on punch plus water plus one sparkling option, count dispensers and cups, and buy more ice than feels reasonable. The day before, mix the base, chill the ginger ale, and make simple syrup if you still need it. The morning of, slice citrus, set the table in the shade, and label everything before the first guest is looking for a drink.

Assign someone to check the ice, the spigot, and the trash once or twice an hour in the first part of the reception. Close or move the station when you no longer want to keep a sweet drink out, and send leftover base home in a cold container. Leftover ginger ale can go back in the cooler.

The measure of this station is whether a guest who is not drinking alcohol can get a cold, pleasant drink without a speech or a special request. If they can, you have done the job. If they cannot, no amount of garnished cocktail photography will have made the table hospitable. Start with water, a tart punch you can finish with ginger ale, and a clear label. That is enough for a backyard wedding, and it is enough to feel like you meant to welcome everyone who came. Label the pitchers the way you would label a bar — an editable menu from our wedding templates is enough — and, in cooler months, let a hot cocoa bar do the same inclusive job with heat.

If you are also setting a cookie table later, keep the punch far enough away that crumbs and icing do not land in the spigot, and close enough that a person can carry a cookie and a cup in one trip. That small piece of geography is the difference between a station people use and a station they walk past.

Questions couples actually ask

What are good non-alcoholic wedding drinks besides soda?

A tart punch that is not too sweet, sparkling water with citrus, a pitcher of iced tea or agua fresca, and coffee later in the evening will cover almost every guest who is not drinking. The test is whether the drink feels like a drink, not like a child's juice cup. A big-batch citrus-cranberry punch is one reliable version; in cooler months a hot cocoa bar does the same job with heat.

How many non-alcoholic drinks should I plan per guest?

Everyone needs water, all afternoon and evening. On top of that, plan about one to two specialty non-alcoholic drinks per guest for a typical reception, more in heat or at a long outdoor party. For punch, a 2-gallon dispenser is roughly 20 to 25 servings; have a cold reserve so you are not mixing mid-event. Ice is its own line item, not an afterthought.

Should we have a separate mocktail bar?

You do not need a second bar with a long list of named mocktails. You need the non-alcoholic drinks to be as easy to reach as the wine, clearly labeled, and not hidden behind the bartender. One punch and one sparkling option, set on their own table or at the end of the main bar, is usually kinder than a performative menu nobody has time to mix. An editable bar menu from our wedding templates is enough paper for that table.

How do you make a wedding bar feel inclusive for guests who aren't drinking?

Put water first and in more than one place. Serve the punch in real glasses, not plastic cups reserved for the kids' table. Label ingredients, and have a non-alcoholic option at the champagne toast so no one is holding an empty hand during the speeches.

Can I batch wedding punch the day before?

Yes, and you should. Mix the tart base a day ahead and keep it cold, then add ice and any sparkling top-up at the station so it does not go flat. A dispenser with a spigot is easier than a ladle bowl when a hundred people are thirsty at once. You can use the same batching logic for wedding sangria if you are also pouring wine.
