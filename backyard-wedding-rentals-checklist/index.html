Borrow the back-of-house gear. Buy only what you'll keep. Measure the lawn, the gate, and the truck path before you sign a quote, and get delivery, pickup, and damage-waiver terms in writing.

A backyard looks simple until you start counting the things a venue would have rolled out of a closet: a hundred chairs that match, linens that aren't from the hall closet, glassware, a place to go to the bathroom that isn't the only powder room in the house. The yard is free. The infrastructure is a rental order.

This is the checklist we want in your notes app before you call a party-rental company. It sits beside our backyard wedding planning checklist and the numbers in our cost breakdown. If you do nothing else, measure twice and book the unglamorous pieces first.

Measure the site before you request a quote

A rental company can only bid honestly if you can tell them what the truck is driving into. Walk the property with a tape measure and a phone full of photos, and write down:

  • The usable lawn or patio for dinner, the ceremony, and any dance floor, with a rough sketch to scale
  • Gate width, fence height, and whether a truck can get within a reasonable carry of the setup
  • Slopes, soft spots, sprinkler heads, buried lines, and overhead wires
  • Where a tent would stake or sit on ballast, and what happens to the neighbor's view
  • Parking for the delivery truck and for guests, which are not the same problem
  • Power: outdoor outlets, breaker capacity, and whether a generator will need a home

If the carry from the curb is long, or the only access is a side gate and a flight of stairs, say so on the first call. Those conditions show up later as labor fees if you don't.

When to book, and what to book first

For a popular Saturday in peak season, start the rental conversation when you start the rest of the plan — often four to six months out, earlier for tents and restroom trailers in busy markets. Chairs and tables are usually easier, but not in a weekend when every lawn in town is hosting something.

Book in this order, once the guest cap is real: weather cover, restrooms, tables and chairs, then linens and tabletop, then extras (dance floor, lighting package, heaters, bars, a staging tent for catering). Changing a chair count is easier than moving a tent after the permit is filed.

If the guest list is still floating, give the company a range and a decision date, and ask what it costs to add a table late. Don't sign a 120-person order because you're afraid to have a hard conversation about the list. The rental bill is one of the places a fuzzy guest cap gets expensive, which we also talk about in the cost breakdown.

Delivery, setup, and the day-after pickup

Read the part of the quote people skip. Delivery windows on a residential street are not the same as a hotel loading dock. Ask:

  • What day and time will the truck arrive, and how long will setup take?
  • Is setup and strike included, or is this a drop-and-go?
  • Is there an after-hours, weekend, or holiday pickup fee?
  • Who is on site to show the crew where to place the tent and the restrooms?
  • What happens if the ground is soaked, or if a storm delays the strike?
  • Where do dirty linens and glassware go, and do they need to be rinsed?

Plan a point person who is not the couple for delivery and pickup. Someone has to be home, know the layout, and be willing to count chairs before the truck leaves on Sunday. Missing items and damage waivers are how a reasonable rental becomes a surprise invoice.

What to borrow, and what to skip

Borrow the unglamorous support: coolers, a folding table for gifts or the cake, wagons, extra extension cords, outdoor extension-cord covers, a spare pop-up for the grilling corner, and lawn games if they suit the day. Label everything with painter's tape so it finds its way home.

Skip, or at least pause on, the things that photograph well and then sit in a garage: a candy cart, a seating-chart easel you'll use once (unless you already have a simple easel you'll keep), specialty goblets, and a second lounge set "for the photos." If a styled piece is important, rent it for the weekend. If it isn't important, the money usually belongs in food, shade, or restrooms.

Linens are a judgment call. Renting them is often worth it for a seated dinner. A farm table with a runner and no cloth can look beautiful and cut a line item, as long as the tables themselves are in good shape. Don't mix those two ideas on the same lawn unless you mean to.

Questions worth asking every company

  • Can you bid the same date, guest count, and add-on list as the other quote I'm comparing?
  • What's included in delivery, setup, and pickup — and what isn't?
  • What's the damage waiver, the deposit, and the cancellation or weather policy?
  • Do you carry liability insurance, and can you send a certificate for the homeowner?
  • What's the latest I can change a chair or table count?
  • Who is my day-of contact, and what's the backup if a truck is late?
  • For tents: who pulls any permit, and what do you need from us for staking or ballast?

A low number with a long list of exclusions is not a bargain. Ask two companies to bid the same package, then read the exclusions side by side.

How many tables, chairs, and restrooms you actually need

Start from the guest cap, not from a hopeful list. For a seated dinner, plan a chair for every guest plus a couple of extras for vendors who sit down, a grandparent who needs a spare, and the inevitable plus-one who was never entered in the spreadsheet. A few extra chairs are cheap insurance; a last-minute Saturday delivery is not.

Table math depends on the shape. A standard 8-foot rectangle often seats 8 comfortably, 10 if you squeeze and the centerpiece stays low. Rounds of 60 inches usually seat 8. Leave room to get in and out — about 5 feet between long tables is a humane starting point, more if anyone will use a wheelchair or if servers need to pass with platters. Our seating guide goes deeper on chair types and grass.

Restrooms are the line item couples under-order. A house with one or two bathrooms can sometimes handle a very small dinner if guests are close to the door and someone is watching the queue. Past that, a restroom trailer or a pair of nicer portable units, stocked and lit, is kinder than sending people through the kitchen, past the dog, and up a hall of bedrooms. Ask the company how they service the unit, where the truck parks, and whether you need a water hookup or a long hose. Put a lantern and a small amenity basket there, and make the path obvious.

Linens follow the table count, plus a couple of spares for the bar, the gift table, and the spill that happens at 6:10. Glassware should match how you're actually serving: a water glass and a wine glass for everyone is a different order than a full bar with highballs. Don't rent a cocktail-glass package for a beer-and-wine evening because it was in the "wedding" bundle.

Insurance, the homeowner, and who is on the hook

A backyard wedding is still an event on someone's property. Talk to the homeowner's insurance agent about a one-day event liability policy, and ask every rental company for a certificate of insurance that can name the homeowner as interested. Tent companies sometimes need this to stake a structure; restroom companies sometimes need a clear path and a firm patch, and they'll want to know who pays if a truck marks a wet lawn.

Read the damage waiver. It is not always a blanket "we're covered." It may exclude weather, standing water, missing pieces, or burns in a linen. Photograph the delivery as it comes off the truck, and photograph the pickup. It's unromantic and it takes ten minutes, and it's how you don't spend Monday arguing about a cracked Chiavari.

If you're using a friend's or parent's yard, put the practical agreements in writing even if the conversation feels awkward: who is home for delivery, who pays for turf repair, what time the generator goes off, and how Sunday morning works. The rental order is easier when those answers already exist.

A rental timeline you can actually follow

  • Six or more months out: measure the site, set a guest cap, get tent and restroom quotes, check permits and HOA rules
  • Four to five months out: book tent, restrooms, tables, and chairs; reserve a generator if power is tight
  • Two to three months out: add linens, glassware, a dance floor or lighting package, and any decor rental
  • One month out: confirm counts, delivery windows, and a point person; share a simple site map with the company
  • The week of: mow, mark tent corners and truck access, clear the gate, confirm the weather call
  • The day after: walk the property with the pickup crew, count items, photograph anything damaged before the truck leaves

One more habit that saves money: refuse the pre-bundled "wedding package" until you've walked it line by line. You may not need the champagne flutes, the charger plates, or the lounge grouping that padded the quote. You may very much need an extra 6-foot table for the bar and a second water station. Build the order from the layout, not from the package name.

Rentals are not the romantic part. They're how the romantic part has a place to sit. Book the heavy pieces early, measure the yard you actually have, and leave the cute extras until the infrastructure is settled.
