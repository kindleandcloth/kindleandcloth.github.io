A Wedding Soup Bar That Stays Hot, With Roasted Tomato Soup for a Crowd

A soup bar for a wedding reception works when the pots are hot, the list of soups is short, and bread is part of the meal. Two good pots will feed a small list; a long line of lukewarm tureens will not.

The short version. Serve one or two soups that can be made ahead, reheated to 165°F, and held at or above 140°F in a Dutch oven, a slow cooker, or a chafing dish. Put bread, spoons, and bowls first, then the pots, then a short topping line — herbs, a little cream, croutons — not a second salad bar. Two large pots of the tomato soup below will feed about 24 as a main with bread; four pots will feed about 48. This is not Italian wedding soup, and it is not a repeat of the squash soup already on the winter-food guide. Label anything with dairy or gluten, and do not leave a blended soup on a warm porch.

A station, not a tasting flight

The tools that actually earn their keep here are specific. A soup bar is a practical wedding dinner when the weather is cold, the list is modest, and you want a meal people can hold in one hand or eat at a table without a carved roast. It fails when you treat it as a tasting of six flavors, each in a pot that cannot stay hot, with a dozen toppings and no bread. Two soups are plenty. One soup is enough if it is good and there is bread, a salad, and a little cheese or a second plate for anyone who needs more than a bowl.

Tell people what it is. “Dinner is a soup bar, with tomato soup, bread, and a salad” is enough, on the website or in a note, so a guest who expected a plated roast is not surprised. If you also want a chili or a stew, that is a different station, and the chili-bar guide is the place for it. Do not put six pots on one table and hope the line figures itself out.

Think about the room. You need a table long enough for bowls, the pots, and an exit, with a way to keep the soup hot and a place for bread that is not over a flame. A board for sliced loaves, ladles that stay in their own pots, and a towel under the spoons will do more than a chalkboard menu. If the party is in a yard, put the soup indoors or under a tent, not on a windy table where a lid will cool a pot in ten minutes.

Italian wedding soup, with meatballs and greens, is a different dish. If you love it, serve it as one labeled pot with its own ladle. This recipe is a roasted tomato soup from canned tomatoes, as useful in January as in August. The winter-food guide already has a squash soup; you do not need two orange pots unless you want them.

If soup is the meal, make enough of it, and put bread on the table. If it is only a starter, make less, and have the main already holding. The worst version is a pretty tureen that runs out while the roast is still in the oven.

How many pots, and how to hold them

Plan on about one and a half cups of soup per person as a main, plus bread, and a little extra. Two large pots of the recipe below — about five to six quarts each — will feed about 24. Four pots will feed about 48. If soup is only a starter, you can get more bowls from the same batch, but then you need another dinner. Write the plan down so you do not cook a starter quantity and call it a meal.

Make the soup a day ahead if you can. Tomato soup is often better after a night in the refrigerator, and the wedding day is a poor time to be blending gallons of hot liquid in a crowded kitchen. Cool it in shallow containers, refrigerate, and reheat until it is steaming and registers 165°F, then hold it at or above 140°F. Stir now and then, and loosen it with a little broth or water if it thickens on the hold. An instant-read thermometer is the simple way to know.

A Dutch oven on a low burner, a slow cooker on warm (checked with a thermometer — “warm” is not a temperature), or a chafing dish with a gentle flame will all hold soup if you watch them. Do not fill a cooker to the lid. Do not leave a pot on a porch “to stay out of the way.” Dairy, if you have added cream, and a blended soup both belong in a controlled hold, not in the sun.

Ladles stay with their pots. If one pot has cream and one does not, or one has a gluten crouton garnish in it, they should never share a utensil. Labels help: the name of the soup, and the thing a guest might need to know — cream, butter, a stock that is not vegetable. People should not have to ask while they are holding a hot bowl.

Bread is not a garnish. Buy more than you think, slice some of it, and keep a closed backup loaf so the board can be refreshed. Butter, olive oil, or a plain board with a knife is enough. A grilled cheese can sit beside this soup if you have a person to cook batches and a way to keep them hot for a short window; it is a lot of work, and it is optional. The soup should be complete without it.

The roasted tomato soup that can wait

This soup is built from canned tomatoes, onions, garlic, and broth, roasted or simmered until the tomatoes taste sweet, then blended until smooth. A little sugar can help a sharp can; a little cream at the end is optional and should be stirred in off the boil, or offered as a topping so the pot can stay dairy-free. It holds. It reheats. It does not depend on August tomatoes in a January kitchen.

Roasting the tomatoes and onions in a half-sheet pan or in the pots, if they are oven-safe, gives you a deeper flavor than a straight simmer, but a simmered pot will still be a good soup if the oven is already full of something else. The method in the card uses a hot oven for the vegetables and tomatoes, then a blend and a hold. Use a blender in batches, with a towel over the lid, or an immersion blender in the pot, and do not fill a blender with boiling liquid.

Taste for salt after reheating. Cold soup tastes duller than soup at 140°F. A few turns of pepper, a pinch of sugar, a squeeze of lemon if it is flat — those corrections belong to the person in the kitchen, not to a guest at the end of a line with a shaker.

If you want a second pot, choose a real contrast: a bean soup, a chicken broth with noodles, a clean vegetable soup, not a second tomato with a different garnish. Keep it in its own pot, with its own ladle and its own label. The tomato soup should still be enough, on its own, for someone who wants only that bowl.

Roasted Tomato Soup for a Wedding Soup Bar

A blended tomato soup made from canned tomatoes, onions, and garlic, roasted until sweet, then held hot. The smaller batch is two large pots for about 24 as a main with bread; the larger is four pots for about 48. Hold at or above 140°F; reheat leftovers to 165°F.

Two large pots (about 24) or four pots (about 48) · Prep 30 minutes · Cook about 1 hour · Total about 1 hour 30 minutes, plus holding

  1. Heat the oven to 425°F. Divide the tomatoes and their juice, the onions, and the garlic between two rimmed sheet pans (four pans for the larger batch), or use additional roasting pans. Drizzle with the olive oil, sprinkle with the salt, and toss. Roast until the onions are soft and the tomatoes have taken on some color, 35 to 45 minutes.
  2. Scrape the roasted vegetables and all of their juices into two wide pots (four for the larger batch). Add the broth and the thyme. Bring to a simmer and cook 15 to 20 minutes, until the flavors come together.
  3. Remove the thyme sprigs. Blend the soup until smooth, in batches in a blender with a towel over the lid, or with an immersion blender in the pot. Do not fill a blender with boiling liquid. Return the soup to the pots. Taste, and add salt, pepper, a little sugar if the tomatoes are sharp, and a squeeze of lemon if it needs brightness.
  4. If you are serving the cream in the pot, stir it in off the heat. If some guests will not want dairy, leave the pots plain and put cream in a small pitcher on the topping line.
  5. To hold, keep the soup at or above 140°F, stirring now and then, and loosen it with broth if it thickens. If the soup was made ahead and chilled, reheat it to 165°F, then hold it at or above 140°F. Reheat leftovers to 165°F.

Cool leftovers in shallow containers before you refrigerate them. A blender full of hot soup can splatter; work in batches and hold the lid. If you need a dairy-free pot, keep cream off that pot entirely, including the ladle.

The line, the toppings, and a table you can clear

Set the line in order: bowls and spoons, bread, the pots, then a short topping line, then napkins and a place to walk away. If people will sit, have the tables already set with water and a runner that leaves room for a bowl and a plate of bread. If people will stand, give them a place to put a bowl down. Soup is a poor food to eat while holding a coat and a conversation in a doorway.

Toppings should be few: chopped herbs, a pitcher of cream, croutons in a bowl, a small dish of chili flakes, maybe grated cheese. That is enough. Do not put a salad bar's worth of garnishes after a hot pot; the soup will cool while people decorate it. Allergies belong on labels, not in a speech. If the croutons contain gluten, say so, and keep a plain slice of bread available.

A green salad, dressed at the last minute, can sit off the main line. Dessert — a pie, a cake, cookies — can wait until the first wave has bowls. Coffee and water will be used; a dispenser of water is more useful than a cocktail you have to explain while someone holds a ladle.

When the rush is over, check the pots, refresh the bread, and stop holding soup that has fallen below a safe temperature. Send leftovers home only if they have been held safely and will be reheated to 165°F. A beautiful tureen that sat out for hours should not travel.

A short soup-bar checklist

A week out, decide whether soup is the main or a starter, count bowls and ladles, and choose one or two flavors. Buy bread and a backup loaf. Confirm you have pot space and a way to hold the soup hot.

The day before, make the soup, cool it, and refrigerate it. Slice some of the bread. Write labels. Assign one person who is not you to the line for the first twenty minutes.

The day of, reheat the soup to 165°F, start the hold at or above 140°F, set the line, and taste the pot before you open it. Keep a little extra broth to loosen it. When the wave passes, cover what is left and be honest about what should be eaten that night.

The measure of a wedding soup bar is a hot bowl, bread, and a clear label. Two pots, a thermometer, and a short line will do that work.
