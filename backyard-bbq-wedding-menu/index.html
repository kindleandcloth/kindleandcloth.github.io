A Backyard BBQ Wedding Menu That Holds Up Outdoors

The short version: A backyard BBQ wedding menu works when the food can wait, the meat is held hot, and someone who is not the two of you is responsible for the grill or the smoker. Choose cuts and sides that stay good in a pan, put sauce on the side, plan shade and a line, and be honest about whether you are hiring a pit cook, ordering a drop-off, or asking a trusted person to run a grill. Barbecue is generous food. It is also slow food, and a wedding does not wait for a brisket that is not ready.

Three ways to serve barbecue that still feel like a wedding dinner

Best for most lists

A restaurant or a local pit cook who will drop off sliced meat and sides, plus your own chafers, a helper, and a sauce-on-the-side setup. You get the flavor without spending the day on the smoker. Our budget food ideas treat barbecue as one drop-off cuisine; this guide is the menu, the heat, and the yard.

Best if you want a live grill

Hire someone — a caterer, a food-truck cook, or a trusted cook you pay — to run a grill during the dinner window. Burgers and dogs can work for a very informal list; a wedding dinner usually wants a smoked or grilled meat that can be sliced and held, with a grill only for finishing or for a second option.

Best to skip, most of the time

The two of you smoking a brisket overnight and grilling for seventy people on Saturday. It can be done for a tiny, skilled list. It is a poor job for a wedding morning. If a family pit master is the point, give them a helper, a timeline, and permission to finish early and hold the meat correctly.

Barbecue belongs at a backyard wedding. It is outdoor food, it feeds a crowd, and it doesn't ask guests to treat dinner like a tasting menu. A good pulled pork, a simple smoked chicken, a tray of sausage, and a few honest sides will make people happy in a way a fragile plated dish often doesn't, especially on a lawn.

The risk is not the cuisine. The risk is timing, heat, and the person standing over the fire. Meat that is "almost ready" at ceremony time will be late. A grill in the sun, with no holding plan, will dry out the first tray while the last guests are still in line. Flies, sauce drips, and a single-file table will do the rest. Plan for those, and the menu can stay simple.

Decide what kind of barbecue dinner this is

You do not need a specialty aisle of props. You need a short, honest list. A chafing dishes, to hold food at a safe serving temperature once it leaves the kitchen, a slow cooker, a beverage dispensers, a taco holder stand, a pizza stone, if you are actually baking pies at home rather than only ordering them, a chafing dish 8 quart stainless, a chafing dish frame full size, and a chafer fuel cans, which is the actual tool this part of the day uses, not a generic stand-in.

There are three honest versions. A drop-off barbecue is trays of meat and sides from a restaurant or a pit you already like, finished and held at the house. A staffed barbecue is a cook who stays, whether they bring a smoker, a grill, or just pans and a cutting board. A house barbecue is food you or your family make, with a named pit boss who is not also in the wedding party photographs.

All three can taste like a real dinner. The staffed version buys you timing and a line that isn't your problem. The drop-off version is usually the calmest for a mid-size list, if you have chafing dishes, shade, and a helper. The house version is a labor decision, not just a savings decision, and it belongs in a self-catering plan as much as it belongs here.

Tell guests it is a barbecue dinner. Most will be pleased. A few will want to know there is a non-meat plate, or that sauce is on the side, or that the meal isn't only ribs you eat with your hands in formal clothes. A short note on the website is enough. If the dress code is very formal and the food is very casual, make sure those two facts are both clear, so no one is surprised in a silk dress at a picnic table.

A menu that can wait, and sides that earn their place

Choose meat that can be cooked ahead and held. Pulled pork, sliced smoked turkey or chicken, sausage, and a well-wrapped brisket that is finished early and held in a pan with its juices are the usual backyard friends. Ribs can be wonderful and are messier, slower to serve, and harder to keep from cooling on a tray. If you serve ribs, have another filling for people who don't want to work that hard in wedding clothes, and have a lot of napkins.

Two meats is enough. One can work for a small list if the sides are serious. Three meats look abundant and multiply the pans, the fuel, and the carving. A vegetarian main has to be a dish: a smoked or grilled vegetable platter is a side unless it is substantial. Baked beans made without meat, a grilled cauliflower or squash, a hearty mac and cheese, or a chili-style pot can be the actual plate for someone who doesn't eat the meat. Label it. Make enough of it.

Sides should be few and good. A slaw that can sit (dress it in batches if it wilts), beans, a potato salad or a hot potato, cornbread or rolls, and a simple green or a watermelon if the weather wants it. That is a dinner. You do not need eight sides. You need enough volume, spoons that stay in the bowls, and a backup in the kitchen. Sauce belongs on the side, in more than one heat, so a dry-rubbed meat can stay that way for the people who want it, and a child isn't surprised by a hot sauce.

Dessert can be a pie table, a simple cake, or a cobbler you hold warm — our dessert-table guide covers volume and shade. Don't make the barbecue cook also bake the cake. Drinks should live at a separate table; our bar plan will keep the beer line from merging with the meat line. Iced tea or lemonade in glass dispensers is at home here, in the shade, with a refill plan.

How much to serve, without pretending it is a formula

Barbecue portions look smaller than they are once you slice them, and people come back. A useful starting point for a dinner is about a half pound of cooked meat per adult when you offer two meats, plus sides that could almost be a meal on their own, plus bread. Children eat less meat and more sides, until they don't. A cocktail-hour nibble is a different, smaller number; if barbecue is the meal, don't portion it like a tasting.

Ask a drop-off kitchen for their own yields and for meat that arrives hot, or that you can reheat correctly. "We'll bring it at 3 p.m. for a 6 p.m. dinner" only works if you can hold it safely. If you are cooking, finish earlier than you think, wrap or pan the meat, and hold it hot. Do not gamble the ceremony on a brisket stall.

Heat, shade, smoke, and the less charming parts of a yard

Hold sliced or pulled meat in covered pans, with enough moisture that the edges don't turn to bark you didn't want. Chafers with lids and enough fuel, or a slow cooker on warm for a pulled filling, will do that work for a drop-off or a house cook. Lift lids only to serve. A sheet pan of sliced brisket left uncovered on a picnic table will disappoint the back of the line.

The grill or smoker itself should sit downwind of the seating and the ceremony, on a stable surface, away from a tent wall, a dry hedge, and the play area. Agree on fuel, a fire extinguisher, and who is allowed to touch the fire. If you are hiring a cook who brings a pit, walk the access path: will the trailer fit, is the ground firm, is there a rule in your town about commercial smokers in a residential yard? Do that walk before you pay a deposit.

Shade is for the food and the people in line, not only for the guests at tables. Direct sun on mayonnaise-based salads and on dairy sides is a food-safety problem, not a styling one. Put those sides over ice or serve them in swapped bowls from a cold cooler. Flies will find the sauce. Mesh covers, a fan that isn't blowing ash, and a helper with a lid habit are more useful than a pretty herb bouquet in the potato salad.

Power matters if you are running warmers, a slow cooker, or a rented holding box. Don't daisy-chain kitchen cords across the lawn. Use outdoor-rated cords and read the power plan before you plug in. Water, a place to wash hands, and a clearly marked bin for bones and drippy plates will keep the yard from turning into the back of a restaurant.

The line, the plates, and a table that can take sauce

Set the buffet so people pick up a plate, then bread, then meat, then sides, then sauce and napkins, then leave. Give the table two sides if the list is more than about sixty, or split meats and sides onto two tables so a sauce decision doesn't stop the meat line. Leave room for two people at each pan. A runner will dress a folding table; crates can hold extra sauce and napkins behind the service line. For rental linens or serving pieces you don't want to own, browse RENT MY WEDDING. The pretty surface only holds what is in play.

Barbecue is messy. Provide real napkins in quantity, a wet-wipe basket if you are serving ribs, and a place for used plates. Compostable or rented plates both work; flimsy plates do not, once sauce and slaw are involved. Someone — a named helper — refills pans from the back, wipes drips, and keeps the sauce bottles from becoming a sticky cluster at the end of the table.

Labels should name the meat, mark heat, and mark the vegetarian dish and any hidden dairy or gluten in the sides. You do not need a story about the rub. You need a guest with an allergy, or a guest who doesn't eat pork, to know which pan is which without a conference.

Cost, quotes, and a timeline that doesn't steal the morning

A barbecue dinner can be a very good value, and it can also hide costs in delivery, trays you didn't know were extra, meat priced by the pound after trim, staffing, and the gear you still have to rent. Ask for a quote that includes meat, sides, bread, delivery or on-site time, tax, and how the food will arrive (hot, cold, sliced, whole). Then add your own numbers for chafers, fuel, plates, napkins, and a helper. City and season change the bid; treat any range you read in a roundup as a sense-check, and get a local quote.

If a family is cooking, write down the real cost of the meat and the sides, and the real hours. "It's just a pork shoulder" is how people spend a wedding week in a parking lot next to a smoker. Pay for the hog or the brisket early, assign the shop-and-chop to a day that is not Friday, and let the pit boss finish in time to change clothes and sit down.

The day before, confirm counts, set tables, test fuel or power, and make the cards. If you are dropping off, confirm arrival time and whether you need hotel pans that fit your chafers. The morning of should be holding, not cooking, unless a staffed cook is working a planned schedule away from the ceremony. About twenty minutes before service, uncover pans, put out bread and sauce, and walk the line once.

A pulled-pork recipe that can wait

Pulled pork is the backyard barbecue dish that most often survives a wedding timeline, because you can cook it until it shreds, rest it, and hold it in its juices. This version follows the usual American shoulder method — a simple rub, a long cook to about 195–205°F, and a vinegar-pepper sauce served on the side, so a dry-rubbed piece can stay that way. You can smoke it if you already know your smoker. The oven method below is the one that doesn't steal the morning of the wedding.

Recipe

Oven Pulled Pork with a Vinegar-Pepper Sauce

Three pork shoulders, a paprika-and-brown-sugar rub, and a sharp sauce you pass at the table. Cook it the day before. Hold it hot, covered, and don't drown it in a sweet bottled sauce unless that is actually what your list wants.

These amounts treat pulled pork as one of two meats, at about a quarter pound of cooked pork per adult, plus a second meat and sides. If pork is the only meat, use the 100-guest column for a list of about 50.

Steps

  1. The day before, or the morning before that, mix the salt, paprika, brown sugar, pepper, garlic powder, and cayenne. Pat the shoulders dry and rub them all over. If you have the time, refrigerate them uncovered or loosely covered overnight.
  2. Heat the oven to 275°F. Set the shoulders fat-side up in deep roasting pans. Pour a few tablespoons of cider vinegar and a splash of water around, not over, the meat. Cover the pans tightly with foil.
  3. Roast until a thermometer in the thickest part, not touching bone, reads about 195–205°F and a fork twists easily, usually 8 to 10 hours depending on the size of the shoulders. Uncover for the last 45 minutes if you want more bark. Rest, covered, at least 30 minutes, and up to an hour.
  4. Meanwhile, stir together the remaining cider vinegar, water, sugar, pepper flakes, salt, and black pepper until the sugar dissolves. Taste: it should be sharp, a little sweet, and a little hot. This is the table sauce. Keep it in jars, not poured over the whole pile.
  5. Pull the pork into pieces, discarding bones and big pockets of fat. Moisten it with some of the pan juices, and only a little of the vinegar sauce if it looks dry. Hold it covered in chafing dishes or in a slow cooker on Warm, and put the rest of the sauce in pitchers or bottles on the table, with a milder bottle and a hotter one if you can.

Make ahead

Cook and pull the pork the day before. Cool it quickly, refrigerate it in its juices, and reheat it gently, covered, until it is steaming hot all the way through before you move it to a chafer. Don't leave a pan of pork on a sunny picnic table through the toasts. If you would rather smoke the shoulders, use the same rub and the same finish temperature, and still hold the meat in a covered pan — the wedding does not need you standing at the pit at 4 p.m.

When the rush is over, collapse to fewer pans and get leftover meat into a hot hold or, if it has been out too long, into the bin. Meat that sat in the sun is not a favor to send home. What can go in a cold cooler, should; label it. The goal is a dinner that felt easy, which usually means the work happened earlier than the party, and the two of you were not the ones with the tongs. A taco bar uses the same logic — two proteins, a real vegetarian option, and pans that stay hot — if barbecue isn't the menu you want.
