Backyard Planning

Checklists, timelines, costs, weather, power, restrooms, tents, and the rest of the unglamorous work of a wedding in a yard or at home.

Most of a backyard wedding is not the arch or the menu. It is the walk through the yard, the permit or the neighbor conversation, the restroom plan, the power, the weather backup, and a day-of timeline someone other than the couple is holding. These guides are the unglamorous ones, written so you can make those decisions on a weekend and then stop thinking about them.

Start with the planning checklist or the just-engaged timeline if you are early. Use the cost and rentals posts when the numbers need to be real. Use the weather, power, restroom, and tent guides before you send a save-the-date that assumes a bare lawn will simply work. Spring and fall have their own idea guides here, because those months have their own weather and their own light.

  • Backyard Wedding Planning Checklist

    A backyard wedding planning checklist you'll actually use, from six months out through the Monday after: permits, restrooms, power, parking, tents, and a day-of plan.

  • Small & Micro Wedding Planning Checklist

    A month-by-month small and micro wedding planning checklist, plus how to handle the guest list, marriage license timing, and where small weddings can splurge.

The yard and the logistics

Weather, power, restrooms, a tent, rentals, a dance floor, and sound.

  • Backyard Wedding Weather Plan

    A backyard wedding weather plan with a time on it: what to do in rain, heat, and wind, when to make the call, and how to tell the tent company, the officiant, and the photographer.

  • A Backyard Wedding Power Plan

    How to power a backyard wedding: what a house can safely run, when to rent a generator, how to lay outdoor-rated cords, and a night test that happens before the day.

  • Backyard Wedding Permits and Insurance

    Start with the house and the neighbors, then ask the town and your insurance carrier about the day you are actually planning. This is not legal advice.

  • Backyard Wedding Restrooms

    A backyard wedding restroom plan: when the house is enough, when to rent a trailer, typical 2026 market ranges, and how to place and stock a unit guests will actually use.

  • Wedding Tent Rental Cost

    What wedding tent rental really costs in 2026 — how quotes are built, typical market ranges, the add-ons that change the number, and the questions to ask before you book.

  • Backyard Wedding Rentals Checklist

    A backyard wedding rentals checklist: what to book first, what to borrow, what to skip, and the site measurements and pickup details that keep a quote honest.

  • Outdoor Wedding Dance Floors

    An outdoor wedding dance floor that holds up: how to size it, where to put it, when to rent, how to light it, and when the patio you already have is enough.

  • Outdoor Wedding Sound System

    How to run outdoor wedding sound so the back row hears the vows: a real ceremony mic, speakers aimed at the guests, a power plan, and a person who is not in the wedding to watch the cues.

  • Backyard Wedding Layout

    Walk the yard at the hour you will use it, give ceremony and dinner their own zones, and keep the buffet, the bathroom, and the photographer from becoming bottlenecks.

  • Backyard Wedding Parking

    Count the cars, mark the overflow, light the walk back after dark, and send a short note to the neighbors before the week of the wedding.

  • Outdoor Wedding Shade

    Walk the yard at the real hour, count the chairs in actual shade, and put water and fans out before anyone sits. This is a seating plan, not a decorative sail.

  • A Backyard Wedding with a Pool

    Treat the pool as water, not decor: close it with a cover or a fence, or assign a sober adult, and light the edge after dusk.

  • Bugs at an Outdoor Wedding

    Empty standing water, be honest about citronella, cover the food, and set a labeled wipe station no one is required to use. This is a yard plan, not a decorating problem.

  • A Backyard Wedding Shuttle

    One pickup point, a sign, a driver who is not in the wedding party, and a last ride after dark. This is how guests get to a house without filling the street.

  • Backyard Wedding Cleanup

    Put lidded cans out, empty them during the event, name who stays after the last car, and leave the hosts a house. This is not leftover food, and it is not a hope that someone will start the dishwasher.

  • Kids at a Backyard Wedding

    Kids at a backyard wedding without the meltdown: a clear kids policy, a yard hazard walk, a kids' table, one activity per stretch, ear protection, a nap room, and a sitter.

  • A Zero-Waste Backyard Wedding

    How to plan a zero-waste (or honestly low-waste) backyard wedding: rent real dishes, use cloth, check what your town composts, label the bins, and pack leftovers before the last toast.

  • Wedding Program Fans

    Keep guests comfortable at a warm outdoor ceremony with paper or fabric fans on every chair, or print the program on the fan itself. Water and shade still come first; this is the thing in their hands during the vows.

The day itself

A ceremony, a timeline, getting ready, games, and a way to end the night.

  • Backyard Wedding Day-Of Timeline

    A backyard wedding day-of timeline you can actually follow: finish the heavy setup the day before, give someone else the clock, and pad the gaps that always run long.

  • Backyard Wedding Ceremony Ideas

    Backyard wedding ceremony ideas that feel intentional: how to pick the spot, set an aisle, choose a focal point (arch or not), and keep the order of events short and clear.

  • A Friend Officiant for a Backyard Wedding

    How to ask a friend to officiate, what they still have to file, a short ceremony on paper, and a rehearsal with a microphone. This is the job, not only a compliment.

  • An Unplugged Backyard Wedding

    Unplugged means a specific request: phones down during the ceremony, a photographer who is actually shooting, and a kind way to handle someone who forgets. It is a policy, not only a sign.

  • Getting Ready at Home

    A calm getting-ready morning at home: a room with light and a guest list, a schedule with slack, a place for the dress and the rings, and a leave-the-house checklist that actually gets used.

  • Backyard Wedding Lawn Games

    How to set out a couple of lawn games for cocktail hour without blocking the buffet, the aisle, or the dance floor — and when to skip them altogether.

  • Wedding Exit Ideas

    No-mess wedding exit ideas that still photograph: bubbles, ribbon wands, a short petal toss with a sweep plan, or a lit walk — and when to skip sparklers.

  • A Ring-Warming Ceremony

    A short cue for passing the rings through a small backyard list, with a plan that keeps them on a ribbon or in a dish, and a clear no when the list is too long for the grass.

  • Writing Wedding Vows

    How to write short vows you can say out loud, choose private or public or both, and give the officiant a copy in case you go blank.

  • A Sunset Backyard Wedding

    Look up sunset for your town and date, start the ceremony early enough for vows and photographs, and plan lights, a layer, and a meal that does not depend on the last glow.

  • A Handfasting Ceremony

    A short ritual with cords or ribbons you can hold in a breeze, a cue someone can actually say, and a clear sentence about what is — and is not — a legal marriage.

  • Dogs at a Backyard Wedding

    A handler who is not the couple, water and shade, a short ceremony job with a backup, and a clear policy on guest dogs — not a costume and a hope.

  • A Backyard Wedding Aisle

    Give the aisle a width, a clear start, and a runner only if you can keep it down. Grass is enough when it is even and dry; soft ground wants boards or a heavier cloth you can stake.

  • A Day-of Coordinator

    Give the day to one person who is not in the wedding party, with a printed timeline, a working kit, and the right to make small decisions without finding you.

  • A Morning Backyard Wedding

    Walk the yard at the real morning hour, dry the chairs, put the ceremony out of the sun, and tell guests when the day actually ends. This is a clock, not a brunch menu.

  • A Welcome Table

    Put one table at the actual entrance, with a readable sign, a card box that closes, and programs only if you need them. It is not a second reception, and it is not a welcome bag.

  • Backyard Wedding Photography

    Walk the yard at the real hour, pick two or three portrait spots, and give someone other than the couple a short family-photo list. This is a location plan, not a tutorial for photographers.

  • A Unity Candle Outdoors

    If you want a unity candle in a yard, test it at the real hour, in a hurricane or a weighted holder, and have a backup line if the wick will not stay lit. Or choose a ritual that does not need a flame.

  • A Wedding Card Box That Stays Put

    Keep wedding cards safe with a locking slot box, an AirTag inside, one person who has the key, and a plan to empty the box after cocktail hour. Never leave it with the venue overnight.

The weekend and the people

An itinerary, a rehearsal, a shower, an elopement, or a courthouse afternoon.

  • Wedding Weekend Itinerary

    How to write a clear wedding weekend itinerary: what belongs on the page, a template you can copy, welcome-bag copies, optional events that don't exhaust people, and when to send it.

  • Friday vs. Saturday Wedding

    A Friday wedding and a Saturday wedding are different days for work, travel, and a backyard at dusk. Choose for the list you have, not for a rule about which day is cheaper.

  • Rehearsal Dinner at Home

    A rehearsal dinner at home that still lets you sleep: a tight guest list, a menu you can finish before the doorbell, real seating, and a host who is not getting married the next morning.

  • Wedding Thank-You Notes

    Thank-you notes after a backyard wedding are a list, a stack of cards, and a few honest sentences. You do not need a matching suite, and you do not have to wait for the photographs.

  • A Farewell Brunch After the Wedding

    A farewell brunch is a shorter, smaller morning for people who stayed or traveled, not a second reception and not the wedding-morning breakfast.

  • A Wedding After-Party at Home

    A quieter second gathering after the official end: a smaller named list, lower music, simple food, and a second end time, with a plan for how people get home.

  • An Engagement Party at Home

    How to host an engagement party at home: a clear guest list, a simple food-and-drink plan, one toast, and a defined end time, without rehearsing the wedding.

  • A Backyard Bridal Shower

    How to host a bridal shower in a backyard or at home, including in the holiday months: a honest guest list, a short afternoon, and a house that can actually hold it.

  • Courthouse Wedding Reception Ideas

    Courthouse and civil wedding reception ideas: when to celebrate, backyard dinners, restaurant lunches, brunch, and how to word the invitation.

  • A Backyard Elopement

    How to plan a backyard elopement: the legal part, a short ceremony in the yard, who is there, and whether to host a party after — without turning it into a second wedding.

  • Backyard Wedding Dress Code

    One official sentence about grass, weather, and shoes, written on the invitation, so guests know what casual or garden actually means in a backyard.

  • A Post-Elopement Reception

    The legal day and the party can be different days. Name the gathering honestly, invite a clear list, and skip a second ceremony unless you actually want one.
