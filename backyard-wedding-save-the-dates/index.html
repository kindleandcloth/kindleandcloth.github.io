When to Send Save the Dates for a Backyard Wedding

Save the dates for a backyard wedding have one job: give people a date they can put on a calendar before the invitation arrives. Send them when the date is real, to the people you are actually inviting, with the facts they need and nothing you will have to unsay later.

The short version. Send save the dates once the date, the city, and the fact of a backyard or home wedding are firm — typically eight to twelve months ahead for a 2027 Saturday, and earlier if many guests will travel. Address them to the people who will get an invitation, not to a hopeful longer list. The card needs a date, your names, a city or neighborhood, a promise that an invitation will follow, and a way to get in touch. It does not need a full itinerary, a dress code, or a registry. Paper is still the clearest form for mixed-age lists; a digital note can be enough for a very small day, or as a follow-up, not as a quiet substitute that some relatives will never see.

Top picks

  • Best for most 2027 backyard dates: a simple card with a photo or a clean typographic face, mailed eight to twelve months ahead to the real invite list.
  • Best if many guests will travel: the same card, sent closer to twelve months, with the city and a line that a weekend plan will follow.
  • Best for a very small list: a short personal note or a digital message, as long as every person who needs the date actually receives it.

What a save-the-date is for (and when you can skip it)

A save-the-date is a hold, not an invitation. It tells people when and where, in general terms, so they can keep the day clear and start thinking about travel. The invitation, later, is the one that asks them to come, tells them what time, and includes a way to reply. Mixing those jobs is how you end up answering “what time is the ceremony?” in January for a wedding in September.

You can skip save the dates if the list is small, local, and already knows the date — a backyard day for thirty neighbors and family who have already been told in person. You should not skip them if people will book flights, if the date is a busy Saturday, or if you will not be ready to send invitations until closer to the event. A holiday engagement and a 2027 date, as in the just-engaged timeline, is a common reason to send them once the date is actually locked, not the week after the proposal.

What it is not: a teaser campaign, a second invitation, or a place to settle family arguments in print. If you are still negotiating the guest cap, wait. A card you have to retract is worse than a card that goes out a month later.

When to send them for a 2027 backyard date

For a local or regional backyard wedding, eight to twelve months ahead is a solid window. For a date that will draw out-of-town guests, lean toward the long end, or a little earlier, so people can look at travel before peak prices and family calendars fill. A mid-summer 2027 Saturday, planned after a holiday engagement, would typically go out in late summer or early fall 2026 — after the date is confirmed with the photographer or officiant, not before.

Do not send them the week you get engaged, even if you are excited and the Christmas card is already on the table. Combined holiday-card save-the-dates exist, and they can work if the date is already real and you were going to mail that card anyway. They do not work if the date is a wish. A Christmas card that says “we're getting married sometime next year” is an announcement, not a save-the-date.

Month-specific habits: summer and early-fall Saturdays deserve more lead time than a winter weekday dinner at home. If your day is a courthouse ceremony plus a later party, put the date people need to travel for on the card, and be clear if there are two dates. If you later send a weekend itinerary, that is a separate piece, for later, for the people who are coming.

Build in time for printing, envelopes, and a real addressing session. A rushed digital file is not the same as a card in someone's kitchen, and a card that sits in a box because you ran out of stamps is not sent. A kitchen-table assembly is entirely respectable if you give it a Saturday: print or collect the cards, stuff, address, stamp, and walk them to the post office in one run. Split the work if the list is long, but do not leave a half-addressed stack “for tomorrow” unless tomorrow is actually on the calendar.

Who gets one, and what it has to say

The list for save the dates should be the invitation list, as you know it now. It is unkind to send a hold to people you are not prepared to invite, and it is messy to invite people who never got a date. If a parent has a late add, decide it before you mail, or plan to send a small second batch — not to treat the first mailing as a draft.

Address people the way you will invite them. A card to “the Smith family” is a statement about children. A card to two names is a statement about a plus-one. If you are not ready to make those calls, you are not ready to mail. The invitation guide can wait until wording and rsvp are real; the save-the-date still has to be honest about who is being asked to hold the day.

The face of the card needs four things: your names, the date, the city or town (and “at home” or “a backyard wedding in [place]” if that helps people understand the trip), and a line that a formal invitation will follow. A wedding website is optional and useful if it exists; do not invent a half-built one just to print a URL you will change. A way to reach you — an email or a phone — is kinder than a site with no information on it yet.

Leave off: the registry, a full hour-by-hour, a dress code you have not thought through, and any wording that sounds like a demand. “Please save the date” is enough. If the yard is the venue, you can say so without giving a street address you don't want circulating a year early. The invitation can carry the address.

Paper, a photo, or a digital note

Paper still works best for mixed-age lists because it lands in a house and can be stuck to a fridge. A single card with a photo you both like, or a clean typographic design without a photo, is enough. You do not need a letterpress suite. You do need type people can read, a date that is unambiguous (spell the month), and an envelope that will actually be opened.

If you are assembling them at home, keep the kit small and the process boring. Kraft envelopes in a size that matches the card, fine pens that will not smear on the paper, and, if you want a little finish, a wax seal kit or a pack of wax-seal stickers for the flap are plenty. Jute twine can hold a card and a small map or a belly band, and it can also be a nuisance in a stack of mail if you overdo it. One modest detail is enough. Address in a sitting, not in five late nights, and weigh a finished piece before you buy stamps.

A photograph should look like you, in a place that feels true, not like an advertisement for a venue you are not using. An at-home or backyard engagement picture will often make more sense here than a formal studio set, especially if the wedding itself will be in a yard. Crop tightly enough that faces are clear when the card is held at arm's length.

Digital save-the-dates are real, and they are only as good as the list you send them to. An email or a well-made digital card can reach people quickly and cheaply. It will not reach the relative who does not check that inbox, and it is easier to miss or delete. If you go this way, follow up with anyone who does not confirm they saw it, and consider a small paper mailing for the people who would want one. A group text is not a save-the-date.

If you are using a printer or an online service, build in time for a proof and for a mistake. Check the date, the year, the spelling of every name, and the way the city is written — people travel on those words. If a photographer took the picture, confirm you have the right to print it on a card, and credit them if that is part of the agreement. A screenshot from a phone is fine for a casual digital note; it is a weak face for a card you want people to keep on a fridge.

Whatever you send, keep a record of who received it. You will need that list when invitations go out, and you will be glad you did not rely on memory.

International guests, or anyone who has to request time off far in advance, may deserve a personal message even earlier than the mailing, followed by the same card so the details match. If addresses are hard to get, start that hunt when the date is locked, not the week you planned to go to the post office. A card that cannot be delivered is not a hold.

Wording can stay plain. “Please save the date: [names], [day, month, year], a backyard wedding in [city]. Invitation to follow.” That is a complete card. If you have a website with real information on it, you can add it. If you do not, wait. “Color palette to come” and “dress code: magical evening” do not help anyone hold a Saturday.

A short save-the-date checklist

Before you design anything, lock the date with the people and permits that could force a change, write the guest list as it will be invited, and decide city wording and whether you will say “at home.” Order or print only after those are true. Address, stamp, and mail in one stretch. Note the date they went out, and only then update a website or a family group thread so the story you tell is the same as the card.

If a date changes after mailing — it happens — tell people promptly, in a real message, and send a new hold if the new date is still far enough away. Do not bury a change in a caption. If only a few people had the first date, a phone call may be enough; if a hundred cards went out, a second mailing or a clear email to everyone, plus a website update, is the minimum.

The whole point of this piece of paper is that someone trusted you with a Saturday. Treat that as the job, and the card will have done enough. The invitation can carry the rest — the time, the address, the reply, the dinner — when you are actually ready to ask people to come.

If you are tempted to design for a long time because the card is the first fun, visible piece, set a deadline. A plain card that goes out on time will do more than a beautiful one that misses the window. The invitation work can absorb more of that design energy later, when the details are real. A save-the-date is a short message that has to travel well. Everything else can wait for the invitation.
