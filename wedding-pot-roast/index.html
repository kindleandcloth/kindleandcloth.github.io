Pot Roast for a Wedding Crowd: Chuck, Gravy, and an Honest Weight of Meat

Pot roast for a wedding crowd is chuck, browned and braised with onions, carrots, and broth until it is fork-tender, with gravy from the juices. One 4-pound roast is about eight servings. Plan two roasts, about 8 pounds, for about 24 people, or four roasts for about 48 — and make it ahead, then reheat to 165°F.

The short version. Brown chuck in a Dutch oven, or in batches if you are cooking more than one roast, then braise it with onion, carrot, broth, and tomato paste until a fork slides through. Skim and thicken the juices. Cool, slice or pull, and reheat the meat in the gravy to 165°F. Two 4-pound roasts feed about 24; four feed about 48. This is not the one-line “mains that hold” mention in winter wedding food — that post’s recipe is soup — and it is not a family-style platter of something you have to finish at the last minute. A thermometer and a pot you can actually lift will do more than a single huge roast you hope will stretch.

Top picks

  • Best cut: chuck, in 4-pound roasts you can actually brown and braise, not one giant piece that will not fit the pot.
  • Best schedule: cook a day ahead, cool in the juices, and reheat to 165°F in the gravy.
  • Best scale: two roasts for about 24, four for about 48, with vegetables from the same pot.

One 4-pound roast is not dinner for fifty

The tools that actually earn their keep here are specific. A instant thermometer, so you are not guessing at 165°F or at a hold above 140°F, a lodge dutch oven, a chef knife, a cast iron roasting pan, a meat fork carving, because a pot roast or a turkey needs a board and a fork, not a dinner plate, a carving board with juice groove, a dutch oven 7 quart enameled, and a gravy boat stainless, if you are carving a roast or sending biscuits out with something to spoon.

Pot roast has a reputation as a generous, old-fashioned main, which is earned when the meat is actually tender and there is enough of it. It is not earned by buying one handsome chuck and hoping it will carve into fifty polite slices. A 4-pound roast, once it has braised and been trimmed, is about eight servings as a main, maybe ten if the sides are heavy and the slices are thin. For a wedding list of 24, I would cook two of those roasts, about 8 pounds of meat. For 48, four roasts, about 16 pounds. The math is unromantic and it is the whole first decision.

Chuck is the right cut: it has enough connective tissue to become tender in a long braise, and it makes a gravy worth keeping. Lean “pretty” roasts will dry before they soften. If you see a larger chuck, you can buy it, but I would still cut it into pieces that fit the pot and brown on all sides, rather than wrestling a seven-pound lump that steams in its own shadow.

This is a sit-down or buffet main for a cool-weather dinner, at home or in a yard that has a real indoor kitchen behind it. It holds better than a steak, and it is calmer than a last-minute roast chicken for a crowd. It is a natural partner to a dinner at home, and to mashed potatoes, egg noodles, or a simple loaf. It is not a cocktail-hour nibble, and it is not a summer patio food you want sitting in the sun.

Winter wedding food ideas already names pot roast, in passing, as a main that holds. That guide’s actual recipe is a butternut soup. This post is the roast itself: the browning, the braise, the gravy, and the honest weight of meat. If you want soup, use that guide. If you want a platter of beef you can make on Friday and serve on Saturday, stay here.

A vegetarian main still belongs on the table, in its own pot or pan, labeled, with its own spoon. The carrots from the beef pot are not a vegetarian dinner, even if they look abundant. Plan that second main as seriously as the roasts, and do not cook it in the beef fat if it needs to stay meatless.

Brown, braise, and make the gravy from the pot

Dry the meat, salt it well, and brown it in a little oil in a Dutch oven until the sides are actually dark, not beige. That fond is the gravy. If you are browning two or four roasts, work in batches, and do not crowd the pot or you will steam the meat and skip the color. A wide skillet can help with the browning; the braise can happen in the Dutch oven, or in a covered roasting pan if you have already overflowed the pot. A knife and a board are for trimming and, later, for slicing; they should be clean ones, not the raw-meat board, when you get to service.

After the meat is browned, cook the onions, then the tomato paste, until the paste darkens a little. Add carrots, a little celery if you like, garlic, broth, and maybe a splash of red wine or more broth, plus thyme and a bay leaf. The liquid should come about halfway up the roasts, not over the tops. Cover and braise in a 325°F oven until a fork slides through with almost no push, usually 3 to 3 1/2 hours for a 4-pound chuck. Check once or twice and add a little broth if the pot is drying out. This is a long, quiet cook. It does not like to be rushed, and it does not like a rolling boil on the stove, which will toughen the meat instead of melting it.

Lift the roasts out. Skim the fat from the juices, or chill the pot and lift the hardened fat later, which is easier if you are making this ahead. Taste the juices. Thicken them with a flour slurry or a little cornstarch mixed with cold water, simmering until the gravy coats a spoon, and season again. Strain if you want a smoother gravy; I often leave the soft onions in and keep the carrots as the vegetable, if they have not gone to mush. If they have, make or reheat a fresh tray of carrots or other vegetables, rather than serving orange jam.

For a wedding, the make-ahead path is the calmer one. Cool the meat in the juices, refrigerate overnight, lift the fat, slice or pull the beef, and reheat it gently in the gravy until the meat itself reaches 165°F. Slicing cold meat is easier and tidier than shredding a scalding roast in a hurry. If you prefer a pulled pot roast, pull it after the reheat, in the gravy, so it does not dry. Hold at or above 140°F, in a pot or a chafing dish that is actually hot, and stir now and then so the edges do not dry.

A lasagna is a different make-ahead main, in pans you can slice. Pot roast is looser, and it wants a deep platter or a pot, a ladle, and towels. A buffet line should still start with plates, then the meat, then the starch and the vegetarian pot, with an exit that does not back into the kitchen door.

Braised chuck pot roast for a crowd

The recipe below is for two 4-pound chuck roasts, about 24 servings, or four roasts if you double it. Use the larger batch if pot roast is the main for a longer dinner. Make it ahead if you can, and reheat to 165°F.

Braised Chuck Pot Roast for a Wedding Crowd

Chuck browned and braised with onion, carrot, broth, and tomato paste until fork-tender, with gravy made from the juices. Two 4-pound roasts feed about 24; four feed about 48. Make ahead, slice or pull, and reheat the meat in the gravy to 165°F.

  1. Heat the oven to 325°F. Pat the roasts dry and season them well with salt and pepper. In a large Dutch oven, heat the oil and brown the roasts on all sides, in batches, until they are deeply colored. Set the meat aside. In the same pot, cook the onions with a pinch of salt until they soften and take on some color. Stir in the tomato paste and cook a minute or two, until it darkens slightly.
  2. Add the carrots, celery if you are using it, and garlic. Return the roasts to the pot. Pour in the broth and the wine or extra broth; the liquid should come about halfway up the meat. Add the thyme and bay leaves. Bring the pot to a simmer on the stove, cover, and transfer to the oven. Braise until a fork slides through the thickest part with almost no resistance, 3 to 3 1/2 hours, checking once or twice and adding a little broth if needed.
  3. Lift out the roasts. Skim the fat from the juices, or cool the pot, refrigerate, and lift the fat when it is solid. Fish out the thyme stems and bay leaves. Stir the flour or cornstarch with enough cold water to make a smooth slurry, whisk it into the simmering juices, and cook until the gravy coats a spoon. Season again. For a make-ahead path, cool the meat in the juices, refrigerate overnight, then slice or pull the cold meat, return it to the gravy, and reheat gently until the meat reaches 165°F. Hold at or above 140°F. Serve with the carrots and plenty of gravy, on a deep platter or from the pot, with a ladle.

Make-ahead, reheating, and getting the platters to the table

A pot roast dinner is heavy, in the best sense, and it wants a starch and a green thing that did not come out of the beef pot. Mashed potatoes can be made ahead and reheated with a little milk. A green salad, or green beans, should be finished close to service so the plate is not only brown. Keep the vegetarian main on a separate path through the kitchen, with its own oven time if it needs it.

Reheat in the gravy, not in a dry pan, and use the thermometer on the meat, not only on the sauce. Hot gravy can hide a cool center in a thick slice. If you are using a chafing dish, heat the meat and gravy on the stove first; a tray of lukewarm beef will not catch up over a single can of fuel. Stir, and keep a little extra hot gravy in the kitchen to loosen the platter as it sits.

Two roasts, already sliced, will not look like much until they are sauced and spread. Use a deep board only if it has a well; a low platter or the pot itself is safer. Label “pot roast” in ordinary words, and put the gravy ladle where a guest can see it. Someone should be watching the hold, not only the cake.

A short pot-roast checklist

Buy enough chuck: two 4-pound roasts for about 24, four for about 48. Brown in batches, braise until fork-tender, and make gravy from the juices. Cool and refrigerate if you can, then slice and reheat to 165°F in the gravy. Hold hot, with a vegetarian main that is actually a main, and a starch that can take the sauce.

Do not stretch one roast across a large list, and do not serve a lukewarm platter because the photographs ran long. Pot roast for a wedding is a make-ahead braise, an honest weight of meat, and a pot of gravy you reheat with a thermometer. That is the method, and it is enough. A backyard dinner can feel generous with two well-cooked roasts and a pot of gravy, and it will not feel generous with one roast asked to do the work of four.
