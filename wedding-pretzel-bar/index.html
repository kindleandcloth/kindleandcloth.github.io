A Wedding Pretzel Bar with Soft Pretzels, Mustard, and One Cheese Dip

A wedding pretzel bar is a cocktail-hour or stand-up station: soft pretzels baked ahead, held briefly in a low oven, and served with mustard and one cheese dip. It is not a basket of snacks left to go hard on a back table.

The short version. Bake soft pretzels in batches, dip them in a baking-soda bath — not lye — and hold them in a single layer in a low oven for a short time. Put out mustard and one cheese dip, and stop there. About 24 pretzels, or 48 for a longer hour, will cover a stand-up list if there is other food. This is not a late-night snack mention and not a second appetizer spread. A sheet pan and a board will do the work.

Top picks

  • Best habit: bake close to the hour, and hold only briefly, uncovered, in a low oven.
  • Best dip line: a good mustard, and one cheese dip kept hot and labeled.
  • Best tools: sheet pans, a board, towels, and a pot or slow cooker for the dip.

A station that has to stay warm, not sit out

Soft pretzels are at their best in the first half hour after they leave the oven: brown, a little shiny, still soft inside. They do not improve in a covered pan, and they do not survive a long afternoon on a pretty stand. If you want a pretzel bar at a backyard wedding, you are signing up for a bake, a short hold, and a line that moves.

Place the station where people can stand with a napkin, not where they have to carry a dripping pretzel across a lawn to a seat. Cocktail hour is the natural time. A late stand-up corner can work if the rest of the food is already a meal and this is an extra. It is a poor main for a seated dinner, and it is a poor substitute for a fire-pit hour if you were hoping one basket would do both jobs.

Count by pretzels, not by a vague “enough for mingling.” One pretzel per person is a taste; two is a real snack if the hour is long or dinner is late. For about 24 guests as a snack, the 24-pretzel batch may be tight; the 48-pretzel batch is more comfortable, or you make 24 and also put out something else. For a smaller hour, 24 is plenty. Bake a test batch the week before so you know how many your size of pretzel actually yields. Write the count on your shopping list, including extra flour for the board and enough parchment for every pan you will actually use, not the number you hope will fit in one oven wave.

Lye is the traditional bath for a deep brown, blistered crust. It is also a chemical you should not improvise with in a home kitchen full of helpers. A baking-soda bath in boiling water is the usual home method, and it is the one in the recipe below. The pretzels will be brown and taste like pretzels. They will not be a professional bakery pretzel, and that is fine.

Salt, or a mix of salted and unsalted, should be decided before you bake, not at the table with a shaker over a wet dip line. If children or people who need less salt are coming, bake a clearly marked tray with little or no coarse salt, and keep those at one end of the board.

Dough, a baking-soda bath, and a short hold

The dough is a simple yeast dough: flour, water, a little butter, salt, and a bit of brown sugar or malt if you have it. It needs time to rise once, then a short rest after shaping. You can mix it in the morning, or the day before and refrigerate the dough, then shape and bake closer to the hour. Cold dough is a little slower to shape and should come back toward cool room temperature so it is not fighting you.

Shape into ropes and twist the familiar knot, or make simpler rods if your helpers are not interested in knots. Consistent size matters more than a perfect shape, because you want an even bake. Work on a lightly floured counter, and line sheet pans with parchment.

The bath is boiling water with a generous amount of baking soda. Dip each pretzel briefly, on a slotted spoon, and return it to the pan. Do not leave them in the water to soak, and do not use a shallow lukewarm pan and hope. Egg-wash, coarse salt if you are using it, and a hot oven until they are a deep brown, not merely blond. Pale pretzels taste like rolls.

Hold them on a rack over a pan in a 200°F oven, in a single layer, for up to about 20 minutes. Do not bag them, do not wrap them in a towel on a counter for an hour, and do not stack them in a deep basket where the bottom ones steam. If the hour runs long, bake a second wave rather than stretching the first one. Towels are for the table and for hands, not for wrapping hot bread until it is wet.

A cheese dip belongs in a pot, a skillet set over very gentle heat, a slow cooker on warm, or a double boiler, kept hot and stirred. It should not sit in a bowl in the sun. Mustard can be cool. If the dip contains beer or dairy, label it. One dip is enough; three dips is a second station you have to staff and clean.

Soft pretzels for a wedding pretzel bar

The recipe below makes about 24 pretzels, or about 48 if you double it. Use the larger batch if pretzels are the main snack for a longer cocktail hour. Hold them only briefly after they bake.

Soft Pretzels for a Wedding Pretzel Bar

A yeast dough shaped into soft pretzels, dipped in a baking-soda bath, and baked until deep brown. Hold in a single layer in a low oven for a short time. Serve with mustard and one cheese dip. About 24 or 48 pretzels.

About 24 or 48 pretzels · Prep 30 minutes plus rise · Bake 12 to 16 minutes per pan

  1. Stir the yeast and brown sugar into the warm water and let it stand until creamy, about 5 to 10 minutes. Stir in the melted butter and the kosher salt, then add 5 1/2 cups of flour for the smaller batch (11 cups for the larger), mixing until a dough forms. Knead, adding flour a little at a time if it is very sticky, until the dough is smooth and elastic, about 8 minutes. Place it in a lightly oiled bowl, cover, and let it rise until doubled, about 1 hour. If you refrigerated the dough overnight, let it sit out until it is workable.
  2. Heat the oven to 450°F. Line sheet pans with parchment. Punch the dough down and divide it into 24 or 48 pieces. Roll each piece into a rope about 18 to 20 inches long, shape a pretzel (or a rod), and set it on the pans.
  3. Bring the bath water to a boil and add the baking soda carefully; it will foam. Dip each pretzel for 20 to 30 seconds, lift with a slotted spoon, and return it to the pan. Brush with egg wash and sprinkle with coarse salt, leaving some unsalted if you planned a marked tray.
  4. Bake until deep brown, 12 to 16 minutes, rotating the pans if needed. Transfer to a rack set over a pan in a 200°F oven, in a single layer, and hold up to 20 minutes. Serve warm with mustard and one cheese dip, kept hot and labeled. Bake a second wave if the hour runs long rather than holding the first pretzels until they harden or steam.

Do not use lye in a home wedding kitchen. A baking-soda bath is the method here. For a cheese dip, melt shredded sharp cheddar with a little milk and mustard in a pot or a slow cooker, stir so it stays smooth, and keep it hot (140°F or above). Label it if it contains beer or dairy. Mustard can be served cool, in a small jar with a spoon.

The line, the dips, and a table that can take salt

Set the board first, then napkins, then mustard, then the dip with a ladle or a short stack of small cups if you do not want people dipping a bitten pretzel. The pretzels should be the last thing to land, so they are still warm. A fondue pot is a different station; do not merge the two unless you have the space and the staff to keep both safe and hot.

Put a towel under the dip and a plate under the mustard. Coarse salt will travel. Keep a small trash bowl for used napkins so they do not blow into a yard. If you are outdoors, keep the cheese dip out of direct sun, even in a cooler month, and do not leave it unattended for a long gap in the hour.

Allergies deserve a sentence on a card: wheat, egg wash, dairy in the dip, mustard. If you made an unsalted tray, say so. People can enjoy a pretzel bar without a speech, but they cannot guess a dip that has beer in it.

Cleanup is part of the plan. A baked-on soda bath pan and a cheese pot will not wait politely until Monday. Assign a person, or accept that you will soak the pots before you change for dinner. The station should close when the hour closes, not linger into the toasts as a forgotten, cooling tray.

A short pretzel-bar checklist

A week ahead, bake a test batch and time your oven and your shaping. Decide on 24 or 48, and on whether you need an unsalted tray. Shop for mustard and for the dip ingredients.

The day of, mix or finish the dough with enough rise time, shape, dip, and bake close to the hour. Set the board, the napkins, the mustard, and the hot dip. Hold pretzels only briefly, in a single layer.

When the hour ends, close the station, get leftover dip into the refrigerator, and soak the pots. Send leftover pretzels home only if they are still good; they do not improve in a bag on a warm dashboard.

A pretzel bar is a generous cocktail-hour idea when you treat it as a short bake-and-hold, not as a basket you can set and forget. Mustard, one dip, and pretzels that are still warm will do the whole job. That is enough for a stand-up hour in a yard, and it is better than a pretty stand of pretzels that have already gone hard.

If you are already cooking a seated dinner, do not also make yourself the pretzel baker unless a helper owns the station from mix to cleanup. The bar looks simple in a photograph. In a real kitchen, it is a rise, a bath, a hot oven, and a dip that cannot be forgotten. Give it a person, or choose a station that can sit. A helper with a timer, a rack, and permission to bake a second wave will save the hour. A couple already in the bedroom getting dressed will not. If no one can own the bake, put mustard out with something that can wait, and save pretzels for a quieter weekend at home, when you can bake one pan without a clock.
