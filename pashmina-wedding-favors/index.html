Pashmina Wedding Favors: The Basket Guests Will Actually Take Home

Most wedding favors stay on the table. A basket of pashmina shawls, with a sign that says the wrap is theirs to keep, is the rare exception: guests take them because they are cold, and they take them home because they are useful. For a cool evening or an outdoor reception, the comfort basket is the favor.

The short version. Set out a basket or a blanket ladder of pashmina shawls near the entrance to the reception, just as the air starts to drop. Add a small sign that says "please take one — it's yours." Buy one for about two-thirds of your guests, in a color that matches the tables, and skip a second trinket at the place setting. Keep a few throws on the back of chairs for anyone who wants something heavier.

Top picks

  • Best for most receptions: a 10-pack of beige pashmina shawls, repeated until you have enough, in one color.
  • Best if you want the thank-you built in: pashminas packed with thank-you tags, so you are not tying cards at midnight.
  • Best way to display them: a large seagrass basket or a wooden blanket ladder at the entrance, with a take-one sign.

The favor guests will actually take home

There is a particular moment at an outdoor reception, usually just after dinner, when the light is beautiful and the temperature drops two sizes. People start wrapping their arms around themselves. Someone goes back to the car for a jacket. Someone else sits through the speeches and looks a little miserable, and you will see it later in the photos.

A basket of shawls fixes that, and it does it more gracefully than a note on the invitation that says "bring a sweater." Some guests will bring one. Many will not, especially if they are traveling, or if the afternoon was warm. The basket is for them. And because the sign says the wrap is a gift, they do not have to wonder whether they are borrowing the host's scarf.

Wedding-forum threads about favors come back to the same honesty: candles, matches, and little bags of candy get left on the table. Blankets, pashminas, and anything edible people can use on the way home get taken. If you have been trying to invent a clever favor, you can stop. This is the one.

How many to buy, and in what color

You do not need one for every guest. Not everyone will be cold, and some people will share or will have brought a jacket. A reliable starting point is enough shawls for about two-thirds of the adults, and a few extra if your reception runs late or sits on an open lawn. For 80 adults, that is roughly 50 to 55 shawls, which is five or six 10-packs. For a 40-person dinner, three packs will probably be plenty.

Buy one color, or two at most. Beige, ivory, dusty rose, or a soft sage will sit quietly with almost any tablescape. A rainbow of colors looks like a gift shop. If you want a tag, a simple kraft thank-you tag tied with twine is enough; or buy a set that already includes the cards and skip the crafting evening. A small shawl pin is a nice extra for a winter wedding and is not required in May.

Open one pack when it arrives and feel the fabric. "Pashmina" on a product page often means a soft, lightweight acrylic or viscose wrap, not cashmere, and that is fine for a favor. You want something large enough to go over the shoulders, not a skinny scarf. If a pack feels scratchy or smaller than you expected, you still have time to order a different one.

A basket, a ladder, and a sign that says it's theirs

Display matters because people need permission. A large floor basket of folded shawls at the entrance to the tent or the patio, with a please-take-one sign, is the whole setup. A wooden blanket ladder is prettier in photos and holds fewer; use it if your guest count is small, or use it as the overflow display and keep the rest in the basket.

Place the basket where people walk in from the ceremony, or just outside the tent as the evening cools, not at the far end of the dance floor. If the ceremony and reception are in the same yard, bring the basket out after dinner, when it will actually be used, so it does not sit in the sun all afternoon.

The wording on the sign should be plain. "Please take a wrap — it's yours to keep" is enough. "Favors" makes some guests hesitate. "Borrow a shawl" means they will try to return it at midnight, and you will spend the end of the night running a coat check. Say it is a gift, and then let it be a gift.

When a shawl is the wrong tool

A lightweight pashmina is perfect for a cool evening in late spring, early fall, or a mild winter in a tent with sides. It is not enough for a January reception in an unheated barn. For that, you want real heat first — our guides to outdoor wedding heaters and a winter backyard wedding cover the unglamorous part — and then a few heavier throws on chairs, not a thin wrap as the only layer.

If your whole wedding is indoors and warm, a pashmina favor is just another object. Pick a different useful favor, or skip favors entirely. Our eco-friendly wedding favors guide is honest about what is worth making or buying, and what becomes trash on Sunday. A shawl guests wore and took home is the version that does not.

For a daytime ceremony that will be hot, look at program fans instead, and save the shawls for after sunset if the reception stays outside. You can do both. You should not do both at 2 p.m.

Packing, leftover shawls, and a kind way to handle the extras

When the packs arrive, unfold each shawl once, refold it in a way you like, and keep them in a clean bin or a second basket, out of the sun, until the day. A light mist of unscented wrinkle spray, or a night hanging on the blanket ladder, takes care of the packing creases. Do not iron a synthetic wrap on a hot setting; you will melt a hole in a favor.

At the end of the night, the leftovers are not a failure. Some guests were warm. Some left early. Fold what remains and take it home. Extra shawls become hostess gifts, emergency wraps for the next cool dinner, or a care package for a friend who is traveling. They should not go in the trash with the leftover paper cones.

If you want to be especially tidy, put a small basket or bag near the exit for anyone who picked up a wrap, got warm on the dance floor, and does not want to carry it. Label it "shawls to go home" so it does not look like a return pile. Most people will keep what they took. A few will be glad of a place to set one down without feeling rude.

Traveling guests, in particular, tend to love this favor. A wrap is easier to pack than a candle and more welcome on a morning flight than a jar of jam. If a lot of your people are flying, this is the one to choose, and a note on the website ("we will have wraps at the reception") means they can leave the extra sweater out of the suitcase.

What to skip

Skip a second favor at the place setting. The shawl is the favor. Skip five colors of wrap, monogrammed shawls for every guest, and a "shawl attendant" unless you are already staffing a coat check. Skip setting them out in the morning sun, where they will be too warm to touch and faded by dinner. And skip wording that makes guests think they have to bring the wrap back. If you wanted a coat check, you would have a coat check.

A short pashmina-favor checklist

Buy enough shawls for about two-thirds of the adults, in one color, and open a pack when it arrives. Fold them into a basket or hang them on a ladder at the entrance, with a sign that says the wrap is theirs to keep. Bring the basket out when the air actually drops. Add a few heavier throws if the night will be truly cold, and let heat do the rest of the work. Then let people walk out wearing their favor.
