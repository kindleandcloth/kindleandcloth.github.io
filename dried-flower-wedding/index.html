Dried Flowers for a Wedding: Bouquets, Centerpieces, and How to Keep Them From Shedding

Dried flowers are the wedding flowers you can finish weeks ahead: no buckets, no wilting in a warm room, and a bouquet you can keep on a shelf for years. Buy a few mixed bundles or dry your own, keep the palette tight, mix in a little fresh green if you like, and plan for the one real downside, which is shedding.

The short version. Choose three to five stems and repeat them: a grass (pampas or bunny tails), a filler (baby's breath or lavender), a structure stem (wheat or eucalyptus), and one or two focal flowers. Build the bouquet in your hand, bind it with tape, and wrap it in ribbon. For tables, a few stems in bud vases or a low jar go further than big arrangements. Keep everything out of direct sun and humidity, give pampas a light mist of unscented hairspray to calm the shedding, and box it all until the morning of.

Top picks

  • Best starter bundle: a 200-stem mixed dried bundle of pampas and grasses, enough to build a bouquet and several bud vases.
  • Best filler: dried baby's breath, which makes everything look fuller and costs little.
  • Best finishing touch: frayed silk chiffon ribbon for wrapping the bouquet stems.

Why dried flowers work for a wedding

Picture a bouquet of cream bunny tails, soft pampas, a few stems of lavender, and a dusty rose or two, tied with a long, frayed ribbon that moves when you walk. It looks a little wild and a little vintage, and it'll look exactly the same at the last dance as it did at the first look. That's the appeal.

For a wedding at home or in a barn, dried flowers solve real problems. They don't need water, so there are no buckets in the bathtub the night before. They don't wilt in a warm tent or a heated room. You can make them weeks ahead, on a slow Sunday, instead of at midnight. And afterward they become a keepsake, not compost on Monday.

Here's the honest part. Dried flowers are brittle, some of them shed, and a room full of nothing but beige pampas can feel a little 2021. The most beautiful dried arrangements right now are restrained: a short list of stems, one warm accent color, and a bit of texture rather than a mountain of fluff. That's easier to make, and it costs less too.

Buy dried, or dry your own

Buying is the easy path. Mixed bundles give you a little of everything in one box, which is the cheapest way to try a look. A large mixed bundle of pampas and grasses plus a bundle with bunny tails and reed grass covers the textured base for a bouquet and several tables. Add single-stem bundles for the things you'll use most, like dried lavender or natural wheat stalks. Order early and open the boxes right away; dried stems arrive compressed and need a day or two to relax.

Drying your own is slower and very satisfying. Pick or buy stems at their peak, strip the lower leaves, gather them in small bunches with a rubber band, and hang them upside down somewhere dark, dry, and airy, such as a closet, an attic, or a spare room, for about two to three weeks. Darkness keeps the color; air keeps the mold away. A pair of sharp floral snips makes clean cuts that dry neatly.

How many stems to buy

Dried stems are sold by the bundle, and bundle sizes vary a lot, so plan by finished piece, not by box. Count how many of each piece you need, add the stems up, and then buy about 20% extra. Dried stems snap in shipping and in your hands, and you'll want spares for the practice bouquet and for a quick repair on the day.

For a small wedding with one bouquet, two bridesmaids, four boutonnieres, and six tables of bud vases, that's roughly 150 to 200 stems before the spares. Fill the rest of the order with soft grasses and baby's breath, which are cheap and fill a lot of space. A few dyed or focal stems go further than you'd expect.

Pick the palette before you shop. Natural cream and tan with one accent works almost everywhere: terracotta and rust for fall, dusty rose for spring, deep red and evergreen for winter. Hold the accent to a few stems per piece so it reads as a choice, not a mix.

A dried bouquet you can make in an afternoon

Lay out your stems by type. You want something tall and soft (pampas, bunny tails, grasses), something to fill (baby's breath, lavender), something for structure (wheat, a stem of dried eucalyptus), and one or two focal flowers if you're using them.

Start with two or three structure stems in your hand. Add stems one at a time, turning the bouquet a quarter turn after each, so the stems spiral. Keep the soft, fluffy pieces toward the outside and a little higher, and tuck the filler into the gaps. Step back to a mirror every few minutes. Dried bouquets look best a little loose and uneven, so stop sooner than you think.

When it feels right, bind the stems tightly just below your hand with floral tape or a rubber band, trim the stems to an even length, and wrap the binding with ribbon. A long, frayed silk chiffon ribbon is the classic finish; leave the tails long so they move. Make the bridesmaids' bouquets smaller versions of the same recipe, and save a few stems for boutonnieres.

Make it once as a practice run a month ahead. It costs a little in stems and saves the morning of the wedding from guesswork.

Centerpieces that last all weekend

For tables, small and repeated beats big and singular. Three bud vases per table, each with a few stems of the same recipe, is quick to make, cheap, and easy to carry. A low jar or bowl with a loose cluster of grasses and baby's breath works well on round tables. On long tables, lay a runner of dried eucalyptus and wheat bundles down the center, tied with twine, with candles in glass between them.

Keep everything low enough that people can see across the table. And keep dried stems well away from open flames; they burn very easily. Glass hurricanes or LED candles are the safe choice, and a hand's width between any flame and a dried stem is the minimum. Our winter wedding centerpieces guide covers candle safety in more detail.

A nice trick for an eco-minded wedding: make centerpieces that double as favors. Small bundles of lavender or wheat tied with ribbon at each place setting are a decoration during dinner and a gift on the way out. Our eco-friendly wedding decor post has more ideas that don't end up in the trash.

Pampas, arches, and the big moments

Pampas is the showpiece of dried flowers: tall, soft, and big enough to fill a corner. Clusters of it on the corners of an arch, at the end of the aisle, or in tall vases on either side of the ceremony spot do the work of a floral installation for a fraction of the cost. Secure it well; pampas catches the wind like a sail, so wire it to a sturdy frame or weight the vases. Our DIY wedding arch post covers building and anchoring an arch outdoors.

Mixing dried and fresh

You don't have to choose. Some of the loveliest wedding flowers right now are mixed: fresh roses or ranunculus with dried grasses and wheat, or fresh greenery with a few dried accents. The trick is that fresh stems need water and dried stems hate it.

For a mixed bouquet, make the dried part ahead, then add a few fresh stems the morning of, wrapped together at the base. For centerpieces, put fresh stems in a small water tube or a hidden jar and tuck the dried ones around it, out of the water. Fresh flowers will wilt by the end of the night; the dried ones will still look the same on Sunday.

In winter, a mixed approach is especially pretty: fresh evergreen or cedar with dried wheat, dried orange slices, and a few deep-red stems. See our winter wedding flowers guide for fresh stems that hold up in the cold.

Shedding, sun, and storage

Dried flowers have three enemies: shedding, sunlight, and moisture.

Shedding. Pampas and some grasses shed fluff, especially when they're new and when they're handled. Give them a good shake outdoors, then a light mist of unscented hairspray from about a foot away, and let them dry. It won't stop shedding completely, but it calms it a lot. Keep heavily shedding stems away from dark clothing, the cake, and the food.

Sun. Direct sun fades dried flowers quickly, so store them in a dark place and set them out the day of.

Moisture. Humidity makes dried stems droop and can cause mold. Store finished arrangements in a box, loosely covered, in a dry room. Don't keep them in a garage or a damp basement.

A word on allergies and sourcing: some dried flowers are dyed or bleached, and some guests are sensitive to grasses and pollen. If that matters to you, buy natural, undyed stems, or ask a local flower farm that dries its own.

After the wedding

This is where dried flowers really shine. The bouquet goes straight onto a shelf or into a vase at home. Bud vases go home with the people who helped you. Wheat and lavender bundles become favors. Anything you don't keep can go into the compost if it's undyed. Dried flowers last for years kept out of the sun, and a few shakes now and then keep them dusted.

What to skip

Skip a dozen different stems; three to five, repeated, looks better. Skip a room full of beige fluff with no accent color. Skip dried stems near open flames. Skip storing them in a damp garage. Skip loose pampas near the cake. And skip leaving the bouquet to the morning of; make it weeks ahead and enjoy the quiet.

A short dried flower checklist

Pick three to five stems and one accent color. Buy mixed bundles or dry your own for two to three weeks. Practice the bouquet a month ahead. Make bud vases and low arrangements, and keep them away from flames. Mist pampas to calm the shedding. Store everything dry and dark until the day. Then take it all home and keep it.
