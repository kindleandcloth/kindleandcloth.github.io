A cold salad, not a sandwich station

The useful purchases are cook-and-keep-cold tools. A stockpot will poach a lot of chicken without a fight. A knife or shredders make quick work of cooled meat. A large mixing bowl keeps the dressing and the chopping from taking over a small kitchen. A lidded bowl belongs in the refrigerator; an ice-nested serving bowl belongs on the table. A scoop keeps servings even, and an insulated carrier is for a cold ride, not for a warm hour in a car.

Chicken salad is a wedding and shower workhorse because it can be made ahead, it scales, and it can sit on a lunch or brunch buffet as a salad or as a filling. It fails when the chicken is still warm when you dress it and the mayonnaise thins, when the bowl sits out in the sun, or when you guess at the amount and run out in the first twenty minutes. It also fails when you treat it as if it were a tray of crackers and forget that it is a mayonnaise salad of cooked poultry, which needs a cold hold as seriously as potato salad does.

It is not a sandwich bar. A sandwich bar is a station of fillings, breads, and a vegetable slab people walk through. This post is one bowl, dressed and chilled, which you may also offer with rolls or croissants if you want. The distinction matters because the work is different: one salad, scaled and kept cold, is a finished dish. A station is a layout and a line. If you already have a sandwich bar on the menu, you may not also need a large bowl of chicken salad unless it is one of the fillings, already counted in that plan.

About 4 pounds of cooked chicken will serve about twenty-four as a generous side or as a filling for a modest sandwich. About 8 pounds will serve about forty-eight. If chicken salad is the main protein at a lunch, people will take more; consider the larger amount, or add a second protein. If it is one of several sides at a brunch or a buffet, the 4-pound scale for twenty-four is a solid starting point. Keep a reserve in the refrigerator, and do not put the entire batch out at once.

Use boneless, skinless chicken breasts or a mix of breast and thigh, or a combination you already like. Poaching is gentle and keeps the meat moist if you do not boil it hard. Baking a large pan of seasoned chicken and cooling it also works, and it frees the stovetop. Rotisserie chickens are allowed if you can get enough of them, pull them when they are still safe to handle, cool them, and count the actual meat, not the weight of the birds as they sat in the shop. Do not invent a raw-chicken shortcut the morning of.

Cook it, cool it, dress it, and keep it cold

Poach the chicken in water or light broth that is barely simmering, with a little salt, until it is cooked through. An instant-read thermometer, if you use one, should read 165°F in the thickest part. Take it out, let it sit until you can handle it, then cool it completely before you chop. Warm chicken in mayonnaise is how the dressing thins and the salad sits in a temperature range you do not want. Spread the meat in a shallow pan to cool, or cut it into large pieces and refrigerate it until it is cold, then chop it for the salad.

Chop the chicken into small, even pieces, or shred it if that is the salad you like. A fine shred can go pasty once it is dressed; a small dice usually holds better in a bowl on a buffet. Celery should be diced small enough that a person does not get a long string. Scallions or a little minced onion should be modest, enough to notice, not enough to become the dish. Grapes or apple, if you use them, should be dry and cut just before they go in, or they will leak into the dressing.

Dress the salad when the chicken is cold. Start with the mayonnaise, lemon, and salt, toss, and add a little more mayonnaise only if it looks dry. It will loosen slightly as it sits. Taste it cold, then taste it again after an hour in the refrigerator, because a salad that seemed perfectly seasoned in the mixing bowl can read quieter once it has chilled. Do not thin a whole batch with a lot of extra lemon at the last minute if you have not tasted it; add a spoon, toss, and taste again.

The hold is the job as much as the recipe is. Keep the bowl in the refrigerator, at or below 40°F, until service. On the table, put out a modest bowl nested over ice, and refill from the cold reserve. Do not leave a mayonnaise chicken salad on a sunny backyard table for the length of a reception. If the day is hot, shorten the service window, keep the reserve inside, and treat this as seriously as you would a picnic in the same yard. The leftover guide is the packing version of the same rule.

If you are offering rolls or croissants, keep the bread in a separate basket, and scoop the salad to order, or set a scoop in the bowl so people are not digging with a serving spoon that has already been on a roll. Label the salad and the bread. If there is a vegetarian main, it should not be an afterthought on the same unlabeled platter.

Wedding chicken salad for a crowd

The recipe below is for about twenty-four generous servings from 4 pounds of cooked chicken, or about forty-eight if you double it. Cook the chicken ahead, cool it completely, dress it, and chill it. Keep it cold at service.

Wedding Chicken Salad

Cooked chicken, cooled and chopped, dressed with mayonnaise, lemon, celery, and a little scallion, then thoroughly chilled. About 4 pounds of cooked chicken serve 24 as a generous side or filling; about 8 pounds, 48. Keep the bowl cold.

About 24 or 48 servings · Prep 30 minutes plus cooling and chilling · Cook 20 to 30 minutes

Directions

  1. Cook the chicken until it is done, then cool it completely. For poaching, cover boneless pieces with lightly salted water, keep the pot at a bare simmer, and cook until the thickest part reaches 165°F, about 15 to 20 minutes depending on thickness. For baking, roast seasoned pieces at 375°F until they reach 165°F. Cool in a shallow pan in the refrigerator.
  2. Chop the cold chicken into small, even pieces. Dice the celery, and slice the scallions or mince the onion.
  3. In a large bowl, toss the chicken with the mayonnaise, lemon juice, celery, scallions, salt, pepper, and mustard if you are using it. Add a little more mayonnaise only if it looks dry. Fold in the grapes or apple last, if you are using them.
  4. Cover and refrigerate until thoroughly cold, at least 2 hours or overnight. Taste again when it is cold, and adjust the salt or lemon.
  5. Serve from a bowl nested over ice, and refill from the refrigerator. Do not leave the salad out in the sun.

Double every amount for 48 servings. The raw-weight note is an estimate; weigh the cooked meat if you can. If you use rotisserie chickens, count the picked meat, not the weight of the birds. Label the bowl if you added grapes, apple, or nuts, and keep the salad at or below 40°F.

A cold hold, a label, and rolls if you want them

Keep the reserve in the refrigerator, and put out a modest bowl. A helper should know to refill it and to take the serving bowl back in if the ice has given out. On a mild day, under cover, a nested bowl will last a service window. On a hot afternoon in a backyard, shorten that window, or serve the salad earlier, at lunch, and do not leave it beside the late cake. This is the same caution you already use in home catering for anything with mayonnaise.

Label it in ordinary words: chicken salad, with mayonnaise and celery. If there are grapes, apple, eggs, or nuts, say so. If you made a version without mayonnaise, label that one even more clearly, and give it its own spoon. People who need to know will not always ask.

If you are traveling with it, pack the bowl already cold, in an insulated carrier with ice packs, and get it back into a refrigerator as soon as you arrive. A long, warm ride is not a plan. Do not dress the salad in the car. Do not set a bowl on a sunny porch “for a minute” while you unload the rest of the car.

Leftovers should be refrigerated promptly and eaten in the next day or two, or discarded if you are not sure how long the bowl sat out. The leftover guide is the packing and labeling version. Be slightly ruthless in the morning. A mayonnaise salad is not a leftover you hope your way through.

If this is part of a shower, a farewell brunch, or a lunch after a morning ceremony, it will feel more at home than it will as the only dinner main at a dusk reception. You can still serve it later in the day if you keep it cold and give people something else that is hot or substantial. It is a cold plate, and it should be planned as one, not as a substitute for a dinner you did not want to cook.

A short chicken-salad checklist

Cook the chicken, cool it completely, and chop it. Dress it with mayonnaise, lemon, and celery, and chill it thoroughly. About 4 pounds of cooked chicken serve twenty-four; about 8 pounds, forty-eight. Keep the reserve in the refrigerator, serve a modest bowl over ice, and label it. Do not leave it in the sun.

Chicken salad is a quiet, familiar generous dish: a cold bowl, a clean celery crunch, a count you can finish the day before, and a hold you can actually defend. If you treat the cooling and the ice as part of the recipe, it will taste like you meant it. If you dress warm chicken and leave a deep bowl on a table, it will taste like a hurry, and it will not be a good idea to keep. That work is ordinary, and it is worth doing while the refrigerator still has a shelf and the morning is still unhurried, instead of discovering a warm bowl when the first guests are already in the yard and the only thing left to do is hope a bag of ice will undo what the last hour already did.
