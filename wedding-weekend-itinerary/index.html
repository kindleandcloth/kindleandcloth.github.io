A Wedding Weekend Itinerary Guests Can Actually Follow

The short version: Guests need one clear page for the weekend: where to be, when, what is optional, what to wear, where to park, and who to text if they're late. Send it with the travel note, and put a copy in the welcome bag. Keep the wedding day's minute-by-minute plan on a different document — that's for you and the helpers, not for the group chat.

A wedding weekend itinerary is a hospitality document. It answers the questions your guests will otherwise text you while you're in a fitting or a rehearsal: what time is dinner, is the welcome drink optional, can I wear boots, where do I park, is there a plan if it rains. It is not the day-of timeline you use to run the wedding, and it is not a novel about the town. One page, maybe two, in a type people can read on a phone, is the job.

Small and backyard weddings need this as much as destination weeks do. Even a Saturday-only guest list benefits from a clear Saturday. A weekend list — rehearsal dinner, welcome drinks, ceremony, farewell brunch — falls apart without times and addresses written down in one place. Put it on the website if you have one, send it as a PDF or a clean email, and print a few copies for the bags and the house.

What belongs on a weekend itinerary (and what doesn't)

Keep the shopping list short, and let it follow the plan rather than a mood board. A guest book, if you want names, or skip it and use a better alternative you will actually keep, a hand warmers, a welcome bag totes, a polaroid photo guest book, a polaroid guest book album, if you want names, or skip it and use a better alternative you will actually keep, a easel seating chart 5 foot, a picture frame, and a sleep mask cotton, in a welcome bag, if guests are staying over.

Belong on the page: the name of each event, the date, the start time, the end time if it matters, the address, a parking or rideshare note, the dress code in plain language, whether the event is optional, and whether kids are invited. Add a weather line for outdoor pieces, a rain plan in one sentence, and one contact name with a number that will actually be answered — usually a sibling, a friend, or an planner, not the couple at 3 p.m. on Saturday.

Also useful, in a short "good to know" section: hotel or rental addresses, a map link, a note about gifts (and whether you'd rather people didn't bring them to the house), allergies only if you're feeding people at a welcome event, and a request about photos or phones if you have a real one. Two restaurant or coffee suggestions near the rooms will spare you a dozen "where should we eat on Friday" texts. You do not need a ten-stop tourist itinerary unless you are hosting those stops.

Leave off: hair and makeup call times, florist delivery windows, the photographer's shot list, private family portraits, and anything that would only confuse a guest who is trying to decide when to leave the hotel. Those belong on your internal timeline and in the planning checklist, not on the document you mail to cousins. If a moment is just for the wedding party, put it on a party-only note, not on the all-guest page.

Be honest about optional events. "Welcome drinks at our house, 6 to 8, come as you are — totally optional if you're tired from traveling" is a complete thought. A vague "Friday night hanging out" is how half the list arrives at 9 and the other half wonders if they missed a reservation.

A template you can copy

You can decorate this later. First, fill in the facts. Something as plain as the following is better than a pretty card with a missing address.

Header: Our wedding weekend — names, city, dates. One line of thanks.

A contact: "If you're running late or lost, text [Name] at [number]. We'll be light on our phones Saturday afternoon."

Friday (or Thursday): Rehearsal dinner or welcome drinks — who is invited, time, address, parking, dress, whether it's a meal or just a drink. If only the wedding party is invited to dinner and everyone else is invited for dessert at 8, say both things clearly.

Saturday: Ceremony at [time] at [address]. Seating or arrival note ("please arrive by 3:15; we're starting promptly at 3:30"). Outdoor or indoor, and the rain plan. Reception to follow at [place], with a meal time if guests should not show up hungry at 8. End time if the house or park has one. Dress in real words: "garden party, comfortable shoes for grass," not only "semi-formal."

Sunday: Farewell brunch or coffee — time, place, optional or not. "Stop by between 9 and 11 if you'd like; no guilt if you've got a flight."

Good to know: Lodging block or house addresses. Parking. Weather. A note for guests who are driving in same-day. One or two places to eat. What to do with gifts. A line about kids, dogs, or smoking if those will otherwise become a question in the yard.

If you have a backyard ceremony and a house reception, write both addresses even if they're the same property — "ceremony in the back garden; dinner in the tent on the south lawn; entrance is the side gate, not the front door" will save a usher a dozen conversations. Link or attach a simple map pin.

Welcome bags, hotels, and the information people actually need

Welcome bags are optional. The itinerary is not. If you do bags, the printed itinerary is the most useful thing in them, along with water, a snack, and maybe something local and small. Everything else is extra. A bag that is only snacks and no schedule is a missed chance; a bag that is only swag and no water is a heavier one.

For hotel stays, confirm the block name, the cutoff date, and the address on the itinerary even if you already emailed them once. People lose emails. If guests are staying at your house or a rental, put the door code or the host's number in a place that isn't also posted on a public website. A paper slip in the bag or a private note is enough.

Parking deserves its own line at backyard weddings. Where to put the car, where not to put the car, whether the street is permit-only after 6, whether a field will be muddy, whether rideshare can actually find the side gate. The backyard planning checklist treats this as a logistics item; the itinerary is how guests hear the answer.

Weather belongs in one or two sentences, not a paragraph of forecasts. "The ceremony is outdoors and short; we'll have throws, and dinner is under the tent. If we move inside, [contact] will text the number you RSVP'd with." Then actually collect numbers you can text. A rain plan that lives only in your head is not a plan your guests can follow.

Optional events that don't exhaust everyone

The kindest weekend is a little under-programmed. A welcome drink on Friday, the wedding on Saturday, a loose brunch on Sunday is a full weekend. Adding a hike, a museum morning, a couple's choice activity, and a late karaoke night is how older guests and parents of young children quietly disappear, and how you arrive at your own ceremony already tired.

If you want a group activity, make it optional, put it at a humane hour, and give a start and a finish. "Saturday 10 a.m. coffee walk at [place], back by 11:30, optional" is a plan. "We'll see what people want to do" is not. Don't schedule anything that requires the couple the morning of the wedding, or late the night before, unless that something is sleep.

Welcome parties should end. Put an end time so people can leave without being rude and so you can finish setting up or go to bed. If the party is at your house, give yourself an hour of quiet after it. The rehearsal dinner has its own rules — see how to host it at home — and keep the dinner guest list honest on the itinerary so no one arrives expecting a seat they were not offered.

Out-of-town guests often appreciate one suggested "on your own" option: a park, a bakery, a short walk, a bookstore. One is enough. You are not a concierge service, and you should not spend Saturday morning routing people's brunch.

Send the first version once the important times are firm — typically two to three weeks before the wedding, sooner if many people are booking flights. Send a short update only when something actually changes: a rain move, a time shift, a parking reassignment. Don't train your list to ignore you with daily "so excited" messages that don't contain information.

Design-wise, readable beats precious. If you make a card, leave the addresses as real type that can be copied, not as a script overlay on a photo. Include a plain-text version in the email even if you also attach a pretty PDF. Check the phone number. Check the map pin. Have one other person read it as if they had never been to your house.

The couple should not be the emergency contact during portraits or the ceremony. Put a deputy on the page, give that person a charged phone, and tell them what they are allowed to decide (parking overflow, a late uncle, the rain text). That is part of the same hospitality as the food and the chairs.

A local Saturday wedding and a destination weekend use the same skeleton, with different density. Local guests may only need Saturday's times and a parking line. People flying in need the Friday and Sunday events, the hotel facts, and a clearer rain plan. You can make one document and mark sections "for guests traveling in" rather than sending two competing versions. What you should not do is leave travelers to assemble the weekend from five old emails and a group chat.

If several households are hosting pieces of the weekend — a parent giving the rehearsal dinner, a friend opening their house for brunch — put the host's name on that line, and confirm their address and end time with them before you publish. The itinerary is a promise. A brunch that the host thought was "around 10, maybe" will not match a card that says 9:30 sharp.

Welcome-bag packing, if you do it, can follow the same document: print the itinerary first, then water, a simple snack, and only then any small local item. A compact first-aid kit in the host house, a basket of throws or hand warmers if nights are cold, and a guest book at the first gathering will do more than a bag of extras nobody asked for. Skip perishable food that will sit in a warm lobby, and skip anything glass if bags will be stacked. A friend can drop bags at the hotel desk with a name list; you should not be in a lobby with a car full of tote bags the morning of the wedding.

If you do all of this, the itinerary disappears into the weekend, which is the point. People show up on time, in shoes that can handle the lawn, at the right gate. You get fewer texts. The day-of timeline can stay a working document, and the guests can have a page that treats them like adults who would like to know what to do with their Friday night.
