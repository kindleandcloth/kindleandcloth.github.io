Winter Wedding Food Ideas That Stay Warm

The short version: Winter wedding food has to do a job a summer menu can dodge: it has to be warm, holdable, and ready in an early dark. Choose a short menu of dishes that can wait in a pot or a chafer — a soup, a stew or a roast, a starch, a vegetable — and plan the holds before you plan the garnish. Serve earlier than you would in June. Keep a hot drink on its own table, and don't make guests stand in a cold yard to get dinner.

Three winter menus that can actually wait

Best for most indoor or tented receptions

A soup or a stew as the first pot, a roast or a baked pasta as the main, a mash or a grain, a green vegetable, and bread. Everything can be made ahead and held. Our budget food ideas and buffet setup still apply; this guide is what changes when the light goes at 4:30.

Best for a holiday-weekend list

A sit-down or family-style meal in the house, with one rich main, a serious vegetarian pot, and a dessert that isn't only peppermint. A Christmas wedding at home can use this menu thinking without turning dinner into a theme. Keep the cocoa or cider for a later station, as in our cocoa-bar guide, not as the only warm thing on offer.

Best to skip, most of the time

A long outdoor cocktail hour of cold passed bites, a raw bar, or a grilled menu that keeps a cook and a line in the wind after dark. Pretty in a magazine. Hard on guests, and hard on the food. If you want a short outdoor moment, keep it brief, give people a hot cup, and move dinner inside.

A winter wedding can be a wonderful time to eat. People want a bowl, a warm plate, something that smells like a house in January. The failure is usually not the cuisine. It is a summer menu served in a cold room, or a pretty grazing table that has been sitting since noon, or a dinner that starts at 8 when guests have been chilly since the 4 p.m. ceremony.

Plan the food around the clock and the building. If the ceremony is outdoors, it should be short, and there should be a warm room or a heated tent waiting — our winter backyard ideas and heater guide cover that part. Dinner belongs in the warm place, ready sooner than you think.

What winter wedding food has to do that a summer menu doesn't

The tools that actually earn their keep here are specific. A chafing dishes, to hold food at a safe serving temperature once it leaves the kitchen, a slow cooker, a soup crock, a chafer fuel cans, a fine mesh cocoa sifter, if you are dusting a tiramisu or finishing a dessert at the last minute, a chafing dish 8 quart stainless, a chafing dish frame full size, and a dutch oven 7 quart enameled, for a stew, a pot roast, or a sauce that has to sit over real heat.

It has to arrive hot, and stay hot, in a season when doors open and close and the light leaves early. Cold food is not forbidden — a citrus salad, a crisp pickle, a clean cheese can be a relief next to a rich stew — but it cannot be the whole welcome. The first thing a guest should be able to do after a ceremony in the air is hold something warm.

It also has to wait. Winter ceremonies run over, coats have to be parked, photographs take longer in low light. Choose dishes that can sit in a slow cooker or a chafer without collapsing: soups, braises, baked pastas, roasted vegetables that can be rewarmed, a mash you can loosen. Leave the last-minute searing and the fragile emulsion to a staffed kitchen, if you have one.

And it has to respect the house. A winter wedding often shares the kitchen with getting-ready, coats, and a dishwasher that will run all night. Finish the cooking the day before when you can. If you are using the oven for food and the bathroom for hair, write down the order of operations, and name a person who is not in the photographs to run the holds. Our DIY catering notes on cap the list still apply in January.

A short, warm menu that can wait

Build a plate, not a tasting. A useful winter shape is: a soup or a stew; a main protein and a vegetarian main that is a real dish; one starch; one or two vegetables; bread. Dessert can be a sturdy cake, a crisp, or a chocolate pot that can sit in the cold room. You do not need a five-station holiday market in the living room.

Mains that hold: a pot roast or a short rib, a roasted chicken, a lasagne or a baked ziti, a stuffed squash, a dal or a chickpea stew, a baked fish only if you can hold it safely and not too long. Mains that fight you: a steak cooked to order, a thin cutlet, anything fried that should be eaten at once. Sides that hold: mashed potatoes, a gratin finished early and rewarmed, roasted roots, braised greens, a grain pilaf. A big green salad is fine if you dress it close to service; it should not be the only vegetable.

Seasoning can feel wintry without becoming a novelty menu. Sage, citrus, brown butter, mustard, a pickle, a sharp cheese — these do more than a tray of peppermint bark at dinner. If the wedding is near a holiday, you can nod at it. You do not have to serve a themed course.

Stations, holds, and a house that is also the kitchen

Use the gear you would use for any buffet, and then be stricter about lids and timing. Light chafers early. Hold soup in slow cookers or an urn on a low setting, and stir so a skin doesn't form. Keep backup pans in a low oven. Don't put the entire roast out at 5 if people will still be eating at 7; slice and refresh.

If dinner is in a tent, the food still has to travel from a warm kitchen without going lukewarm in the yard. Cover the pans, use insulated carriers if you have them, and don't stage a buffet in a draft at the tent door. If dinner is in the house, decide which rooms are for coats, which are for the table, and which oven is actually available after 3 p.m. A winter wedding that also tries to roast a tray of vegetables during family photographs is a winter wedding that will run late.

Label everything, especially soups and creams, and keep a clearly vegetarian, clearly vegan main if you have guests who need one. A winter menu leans on butter and stock; don't hide that in a “vegetable pot” that was finished with cream.

Drinks, dessert, and the things that go cold

A hot drink station is a kindness, not a substitute for dinner. Cocoa, cider, tea, or coffee should sit on their own table, with cups, and with a person who can watch a pot — the cocoa-bar guide covers volume and holds. Water still matters; heated rooms and winter coats make people thirsty. A small wine-and-beer table, or a batched winter cocktail, can live nearby, as in our bar plan. Don't make the only warm liquid a cocktail.

Dessert can be simple and make-ahead: a sheet cake, a fruit crisp that can be rewarmed, cookies, a chocolate pot. Ice cream is a project unless you have a freezer plan and a place to scoop that isn't the walkway. Late-night food, if you want it, should be truly warm — a slider tray or a small soup leftover, not a cheese board that has been out since cocktail hour.

Passed cold bites outdoors are the usual winter mistake. If you want a short outdoor toast, give people a hot cup and a passed warm thing, keep it brief, and go inside. Gloves and a pretty skewer are not a meal.

A timeline for a dark, early evening

Work backward from dusk, not from a summer 6:30 dinner. If the ceremony is at 3 or 4, cocktail hour — such as it is — should be short and indoors or in a heated tent, and dinner should follow while people still have some afternoon in them. An 8 p.m. meal after a 4 p.m. winter ceremony is a long, hungry wait.

The day before, cook what can be cooked: the soup, the braise, the dessert, the potatoes that will be reheated. The morning of should be assembly and holds, not a from-scratch project, especially if the house is also a getting-ready space. About twenty minutes before you want people to eat, put out the soup cups, bring the first hot pans, and walk the room as a guest who just took off a coat.

Name a captain. Winter service has more moving parts — lids, fuel, a door that keeps being opened, a pot that needs stirring — than a midsummer salad lunch. The two of you should not be the ones in an apron when the light is going. The soup below is one first pot you can roast and blend the day before, hold in a slow cooker, and serve in cups as people come in from the air.

A roasted butternut squash soup you can hold

A winter reception needs something warm in people's hands before the main even starts. A roasted butternut squash soup is the usual pot: sweet from the oven, sharpened with a little acid, easy to blend, and happy in a slow cooker or an urn for an hour or two. Serve it in cups or small bowls at a short, separate line so it doesn't stall the buffet. The amounts below are for about 50 servings of 8 ounces.

Recipe

Roasted Butternut Squash Soup for a Winter Wedding

Squash and onion roasted until browned, simmered with stock, blended until smooth, and brightened with lemon or vinegar. Optional cream, added at the end. It is meant to be made the day before and held hot.

  • Yield: about 50 cups (8 ounces)
  • Prep: 40 minutes
  • Cook: about 1 hour, plus a gentle reheat

This should yield a little over 3 gallons, enough for fifty 8-ounce cups with a small backup. If your cups are smaller, you will have extra, which is better than running out at the door.

Steps

  1. Heat the oven to 425°F. Toss the squash, onions, garlic, and apples if you are using them with the oil, a generous amount of salt, and a little pepper. Spread them on sheet pans in a single layer — use as many pans as you need — and roast until the squash is tender and browned at the edges, 35 to 45 minutes, stirring once.
  2. Tip the roasted vegetables into large pots. Add the sage or thyme and enough stock to cover. Bring to a simmer and cook 10 to 15 minutes so the flavors meet. Remove the herb stems if you used sprigs.
  3. Blend until smooth, in batches in a blender with the lid vented, or with a stick blender in the pot. Add more stock if the soup is thicker than a cup-able puree. Stir in the cream or coconut milk if you are using it, and bring it back to a gentle steam, not a hard boil. Taste for salt, then add lemon or vinegar until it tastes bright, not heavy.
  4. Hold hot in slow cookers on Warm or in an urn, stirring now and then so a skin doesn't form. Serve in cups or small bowls with a napkin, and put a small card on the pot: whether it contains dairy, and whether the stock is vegetable or chicken. A few toasted pumpkin seeds or a drop of cream in the cup is optional; a line that moves is not.

Make ahead

The soup is better the next day. Cool it, refrigerate it, and reheat it gently until it is steaming before you pour it into the holding pots — an eight-quart slow cooker or a chafer will keep it there. Set a board of bread beside the urn, a basket of throws near the door, and keep water or cider in dispensers so the soup line isn't also the drink line. It also freezes well without the cream; add the cream when you reheat if you want it. If you need a dairy-free pot, use coconut milk or skip the enrichment, and give that urn its own ladle. Keep water and a non-soup option on the table for anyone who wants to skip a cup.
