How to Plan a Backyard Wedding in Three Months

A backyard wedding in twelve weeks is a compressed plan, not a panic. You decide the list, the legal work, and the house first, and you let a lot of nice ideas wait.

The short version. Three months is enough for a small backyard wedding if you treat it as a short calendar, not a shorter version of an eighteen-month one. Book the legal work and tell the people who have to travel. Decide who will eat where, and what you can cook, loan, or rent without a long lead time. Write the invitations once you have the date and the rain plan, and stop adding projects in the last month. A notebook, a plain checklist, and a list you will actually keep are more useful than a new system. This is not the same as a just-engaged first stretch, which can take the year in a longer order.

Twelve weeks is a different kind of plan

The right objects are the ones you will still use after the weekend is over, or the ones that quietly do a job on the day. A first aid kit, in a place a helper can find without asking the couple, a kraft envelopes, a micron pens, a moleskine notebook, a seed paper invitations, only if you will also include a word about how to plant it, a envelope moistener, a calligraphy pens for invitations, and a save the date postcard, which is the actual tool this part of the day uses, not a generic stand-in.

A year-long plan can afford a month of indecision about typography. A twelve-week plan cannot. The work is the same in kind — a date, a list, a place to stand, a meal, a way to be legal — but the order is stricter, and the decorations that require a special order have to earn their place or leave.

Start by writing what is already true. Who is getting married, where you think the ceremony will be, how many people the house or the yard can actually hold in bad weather, and whether a relative’s calendar or a religious calendar is already spoken for. If the yard is not yours, get a yes from the person whose house it is before you tell a wider list. A borrowed backyard with a vague maybe will not survive week ten.

Tell the people who have to travel or take time off as soon as the date is real. A text or a call can go out before a printed card. The card can follow when the wording is ready. People who live nearby can hear a little later, but do not wait for a complete stationery suite to mention a Saturday that is already close.

What you should not do, in week one, is open a new Pinterest board and a new color story and a new seating-chart app. You may already have boards. Leave them. The three-month version of this work is a sequence of decisions that close: date, place, list size, legal path, food path, rain path. Pretty things come after those are written down, and some of them do not come at all.

If you have already been engaged for a year and are only now compressing the date, the job is the same, with one extra courtesy: tell people why the date moved, briefly, if they already had a later one in mind. You do not owe a long explanation. You do owe a clear new date.

Weeks twelve to eight: the legal work, the list, and the place

In the first month of a twelve-week calendar, do the things that other people’s offices control. Find out how your county issues a license, how long it is valid, whether you need an appointment, and who may officiate. If a friend will officiate, find out what that friend has to file, and when. Holiday weeks and Friday afternoons are poor times to discover a clerk’s office is closed. Write the actual steps down, including identification and waiting periods, rather than trusting a memory of what a cousin did in another state.

At the same time, write the list. A backyard list is a capacity question before it is an etiquette question. How many people can sit or stand if it rains and everyone comes inside. How many can park without blocking a neighbor. How many you can feed without a second kitchen. If the honest number is thirty and the wish list is sixty, the honest number wins. You can be kind in the wording and still be firm about the size.

Walk the yard and the house as they are, not as you might wish them. Note outlets, the path to a bathroom, where a car can turn around, and where a tent would actually go if you need one. If you need a tent, a restroom, or a serious rental, ask about dates now. Some weekends go early, even in a quieter month. If you cannot get the rental, change the plan — a shorter outdoor ceremony and a house reception — rather than hoping a cancellation appears in week three.

Save-the-dates, if you are sending them at all on this timeline, should go out in this window or not at all. A formal save-the-date that arrives four weeks before the invitation is mostly a delay. A simple message with the date, the town, and a note that the details will follow is enough. A later save-the-date guide still applies if you have a little more time; in twelve weeks, clarity matters more than a matching suite.

Money should be sketched, not perfected. Write the costs you already know — the license, a rental if you need it, food for the list you just wrote, and anything you have already promised. Leave a line for the things you will forget. You do not need a full cost breakdown on day two, but you do need to know whether the plan you just described is in the realm of what you can spend.

Weeks eight to four: food, loans, and what people wear

By week eight you should know how people will eat. That might be a tray dinner you can make ahead, a potluck with a clear list of what is already covered, a restaurant drop-off, or a relative who has offered to cook and who means it. What does not work is “we will figure out the food later,” because later is a grocery store on a Thursday night and a missing serving spoon. Write the menu in the level of detail a helper could shop from.

Ask for loans early enough that people can say no. Chairs, tables, coolers, a folding table for gifts, extra wineglasses, a pitcher, a coat rack. Write down who offered what, and when it will be picked up or dropped off. A borrowed backyard often comes with borrowed things; thank people in advance, and plan a return on the morning after, not “sometime next week.”

Music, photographs, and flowers can be simple on this calendar. A phone on a tripod and a friend with a list of shots is a real plan if you do not have time to book a photographer; a professional is still a gift if one is free. Flowers can be grocery bunches, a pot by the door, or a small order from a shop that can do a short lead time. Do not start a complicated floral design in week seven that needs a wholesaler account you do not have.

If you are mailing invitations, this is the window. You need the date, the time, the address, a rain line or a phone number, and a way to reply that you will actually check. Envelopes and a pen you can address with are enough. A wax seal is optional and slow. If the list is local and small, a handwritten note or a careful evite can be the invitation; just keep a record of replies in one place.

Tell people what to wear in ordinary language: a backyard, a lawn that may be soft, a house that may be warm, a ceremony that may be short and outdoors. You do not need a dress code name if a sentence will do. If the month is cold, say so. If the ceremony is at dusk, say so. Guests would rather be told than be stylish and miserable.

This is also the time to stop adding new projects. A photo booth, a favor, a special cocktail, a late-night snack, a welcome bag — any one of these can be lovely if it is already in motion. A new one invented in week six will steal time from the meal and the chairs. If a project does not have a person, a budget, and a finish date, it is not in the plan.

The last month, and the week of

In the last four weeks, the work is confirmation, not invention. Count the replies. Confirm the officiant, the license steps that are still ahead, the rental windows, and the people who offered to cook or set up. Write a day-of timeline that a helpful person could follow if you are getting dressed. Include when the yard is off-limits to guests, when food should come out, and who is the person to ask when something is missing.

Shop for the meal on a schedule that matches the food, not in one heroic trip the morning of. If you are cooking trays, do the work that can be done ahead, and label what is in the freezer. If someone else is cooking, send the head count and the dietary notes in writing, once, and then stop tinkering with the menu.

Pick one person — not a parent who is already carrying too much, if you can help it — to hold the day-of list, a first-aid kit, and the extra extension cord. If the yard needs power, the plan belongs in writing now, including which outdoor cords you already have and which outlets are actually free. Do not discover a dead string of lights at dusk with no spare.

The week of, walk the house again. Clear a table for gifts or cards. Make room in a closet for coats. Put a sign on a bathroom if the house is easy to get lost in. Confirm parking in one sentence you can text to the group. Then stop. A short night of sleep will do more for the day than a last banner. If something is still unbought on Wednesday and it is not food, chairs, or a legal document, it can be skipped. Guests will remember the ceremony and the meal, not the missing sign. You can write the missing idea down for a later dinner, if you still like it on Monday.

After the day, return what you borrowed, take the trash and recycling out, and write the first thank-you notes while you still remember the gifts. The twelve-week plan does not end at dessert. It ends when the yard is a yard again and the people who helped have been thanked.

A short twelve-week checklist

In week twelve, write the date, the place, the list size, and the legal path. Tell the people who have to travel. Ask the owner of the yard, if it is not you, for a real yes. Sketch the money.

By week eight, know how you will feed people, what you will rent or loan, and how you will invite. Mail or send the invitations. Write the rain plan in a form a guest can use.

By week four, count replies, write the day-of list, and stop adding projects. Confirm the officiant and any rental. Shop and cook on a schedule the food can stand.

In the week of, set what you can set early, sleep, and let the day be the size you planned. Three months is not a scramble if you close decisions in order. It is a short, honest calendar, and that is enough for a backyard that is already yours.
