Lasagna for a Wedding Crowd: Make-Ahead 9x13 Pans, Including a Vegetarian One

Lasagna for a wedding crowd is a make-ahead 9x13 pan that can be baked and then rest, not a last-minute layering project in a hot kitchen. Keep a vegetarian pan clearly labeled, and keep meat sauce in its own pans.

The short version. Assemble lasagna a day ahead in 9x13 pans, refrigerate, and bake until the center is steaming hot. Two pans will feed about 24 as a main; four pans, about 48. If you need a vegetarian pan, make it separately, with its own sauce and its own knife, and label it. This is not baked ziti, which is tossed in a tray, and it is not a pasta bar. It is a layered pan you can slice. A thermometer will tell you when the middle is ready; a pretty top will not.

A layered pan, not a tossed tray

Keep the shopping list short, and let it follow the plan rather than a mood board. A instant thermometer, so you are not guessing at 165°F or at a hold above 140°F, a pyrex 9x13, a chef knife, a cake and pie server, a macaroni and cheese pan deep, which is the actual tool this part of the day uses, not a generic stand-in, a lasagna pan deep ceramic, a icing spatula, and a hotel pan pans, if you want a pan that fits a chafer and does not slide around.

Lasagna earns its place at a backyard wedding because it can be made ahead, it slices, and it feels like a dinner. It also fails in predictable ways: a watery middle, a cold center, a vegetarian guest served from a meat pan, or a tray that is cut too soon and slides into a heap. The work is to treat each pan as a complete, labeled thing, not as one giant lasagna you will somehow keep hot for two hours.

Count by pans, not by a hopeful number of “servings.” A 9x13 pan, cut into twelve pieces, is a generous main for about twelve people, or a slightly smaller piece for more if you have a serious salad and bread. Two pans are the right start for a list of about 24 who are sitting down to lasagna as the main. Four pans will cover about 48. If lasagna is one of two mains, you can make less, but then you must actually have the second main.

This is a different dish from family-style platters you pass, and from a vegetarian dinner that is a whole menu. Lasagna can be the vegetarian dinner if every pan is vegetarian. If only some of the list needs that, make one vegetarian pan and keep it honest: its own sauce, its own spoons, a sign.

A backyard kitchen should not be assembling twelve layers while guests are parking. Assembly belongs to the day before, or at least the morning, with time to chill. Baking belongs to a schedule that gets the pans out, rests them, and still leaves you a moment to change clothes. If you do not have oven space for every pan at once, bake in waves and hold the finished, rested pans, loosely covered, in a warm oven or a chafing setup that keeps the food hot, not in a cold sunroom.

Do not stack a second menu on top of this one unless someone else owns it. Lasagna plus a second cooked-to-order station is how a small kitchen loses the day. Salad, bread, and a simple dessert are enough beside it.

Sauce, filling, and a vegetarian pan that is actually separate

A meat sauce should be cooked long enough that it tastes like dinner, not like a can opened over noodles. Brown the meat, cook the onion and garlic in the same pot, add tomatoes, and let the sauce thicken so it will not flood the pan. Cool it before you layer if you are assembling ahead; a boiling sauce on ricotta is a watery lasagna by morning.

Keep meat in its own pots and its own pans. If you are also making a vegetarian pan, do not dip a meat spoon into the mushroom sauce, and do not cut both pans with the same knife at the table. Use a knife for each, or wash and dry thoroughly between them, and put a card in front of the vegetarian pan that a guest can see without asking.

A vegetarian pan can use the same ricotta, mozzarella, and noodles, with a sauce built on browned mushrooms, maybe a handful of well-drained spinach, and the same tomatoes. The mushrooms need their own time in a pan so they give up water before they go into the sauce. If you try to hide a thin vegetable layer inside a meat lasagna and call it a mixed pan, you have not made a vegetarian option. You have made a problem.

Ricotta should be seasoned and, in this style of pan lasagna, bound with egg so it slices instead of spilling. You do not need a complicated filling. Salt, pepper, a little Parmesan, chopped parsley if you have it, and eggs are enough. If someone cannot eat egg, make their pan without it and accept a slightly softer slice, or choose a different main for that guest and say so.

Noodles can be no-boil or regular. If you use regular noodles, boil them shy of tender so they finish in the oven, and do not let them clump in a colander. If you use no-boil, be generous with sauce, especially at the edges and corners, or those edges will stay tough. Either path works if you follow it all the way through the pan, not halfway.

Make-ahead lasagna in 9x13 pans

The recipe below is for two pans or four, of a classic layered lasagna: a tomato meat sauce, a ricotta filling, mozzarella, and noodles. For a vegetarian pan, make a mushroom sauce in a separate pot and assemble that pan on its own board, then reduce the meat quantities for the remaining pans. Two pans are about 24 as a main; four pans, about 48.

Make-Ahead Lasagna in 9x13 Pans

A layered, make-ahead lasagna in 9x13 pans, with a meat sauce, ricotta, and mozzarella. Assemble a day ahead if you can. For a vegetarian pan, use a mushroom sauce in its own dish and label it. Two pans feed about 24 as a main; four pans, about 48.

2 or 4 pans (about 24 or 48 as a main) · Prep about 1 hour · Bake 45 to 60 minutes, plus rest

  1. In a wide pot, heat the oil and cook the onions with a pinch of salt until soft. Add the garlic for a minute, then the meat, breaking it up until it is browned. Stir in the crushed tomatoes, tomato sauce, tomato paste, and Italian seasoning. Simmer, stirring now and then, until the sauce is thick enough to mound on a spoon, 20 to 30 minutes. Season with salt and pepper. Cool until it is no longer steaming if you are assembling ahead. For a vegetarian pan, brown two pounds of sliced mushrooms for each 9x13 (four pounds for two vegetarian pans) in a separate pot until their liquid cooks off, then build the same tomato sauce in that pot, without meat.
  2. In a bowl, stir the ricotta with the eggs, a cup of the Parmesan for two pans (two cups for four), the parsley if you are using it, and salt and pepper. If you are using regular noodles, boil them until just shy of tender, drain, and lay them out so they do not stick. No-boil noodles can go in dry if the sauce is plentiful.
  3. Spread a thin layer of sauce in each 9x13 pan. Add a layer of noodles, a layer of ricotta, a layer of mozzarella, and more sauce. Repeat, ending with sauce, mozzarella, and the remaining Parmesan. Cover tightly. Refrigerate up to a day, or bake the same day once assembled.
  4. Heat the oven to 375°F. If the pans are cold from the refrigerator, give them 15 or 20 minutes on the counter while the oven heats. Bake, covered, 30 minutes, then uncovered until the sauce is bubbling and a thermometer in the center reads at least 165°F, usually 15 to 30 minutes more. Rest 15 minutes before cutting, so the layers set. Hold leftovers and any waiting pans hot (140°F or above) or refrigerate; do not leave a dairy pan out on a warm table for a long service.

Label every vegetarian pan, and give it its own serving spatula. If you are baking in waves, rest each pan before it is cut, even if a later pan is still in the oven. For a list of 24 as a main, make two pans; for 48, make four. A salad and bread beside the pans are enough.

Baking, holding, and getting the pans to the table

A cold pan needs more time than a room-temperature one. Start checking the center with a thermometer rather than trusting the top cheese. When the middle is hot and the edges bubble, take the pan out and let it rest. Cutting too soon is how a good lasagna becomes a ladle of filling.

If you must hold a baked pan, keep it loosely covered in a low oven or over gentle chafing heat after it has already rested and been cut, or rest it and then return it, covered, to a warm oven for a short time. Do not hold an uncut, raw, refrigerated pan in a chafing dish and hope it finishes. The oven does that work.

Carry pans on a sheet tray, not in bare hands, and set them on a trivet or a folded towel on a runner you do not mind heating. Put the signs down before people line up. A dinner at home can be plated in the kitchen; a slightly larger list can come to the pans, one pan at a time, with someone cutting modest squares so the first guests do not take a quarter of a tray.

Think about oven math early. Four pans may not fit at once. Two and two, with a rest on the first pair while the second bakes, is a real plan. Write the times down. A backyard wedding that also needs the oven for bread or a dessert has to choose; lasagna usually wins the oven, and the dessert should be something that does not compete.

A short lasagna checklist

A day ahead, make the sauces and the ricotta filling, cook or prepare the noodles, and assemble the pans. Cool the meat sauce first. Label the vegetarian pan on the foil as well as on a card, so it does not get lost in the refrigerator.

The day of, bake until the center is hot, rest, and cut. Set out salad, bread, serving spatulas, and signs. Keep leftover pans hot or get them into the refrigerator.

After, send people home with labeled containers only if they want them and can keep them cold, and wash the pans before they sit overnight with leftover cheese in the corners.

Lasagna is a generous backyard main when you treat it as a pan with a schedule, not as a last-minute comfort-food improvisation. Two pans or four, a labeled vegetarian option if you need one, and a rest before the first slice will do the whole job. That is enough dinner for a list the house can actually hold. If you are tempted to add a second baked main, give that work to a different cook, or skip it. One kind of pan, done on time and served hot, will be remembered more kindly than two pans that are both late. Write the oven times on a card, and let the rest of the table stay simple. Salad, bread, and a hot, rested pan are the whole dinner.
