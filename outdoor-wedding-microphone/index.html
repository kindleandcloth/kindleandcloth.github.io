Outdoor Wedding Microphone: How Guests Actually Hear the Vows

The most common regret after a short outdoor ceremony is not the weather. It is that nobody heard the vows. Even for forty guests, and even when the ceremony is brief, a little wind is enough to carry the words away. A dual lavalier kit, a small speaker, and a test at the back row, at the real hour, will fix it.

The short version. Yes, you want a microphone. Rent a small system, or buy a dual wireless lavalier kit and a portable battery-powered speaker, and put a lav on the officiant and one on whoever is saying vows, or one on each of you. Add a windscreen, raise the speaker on a stand off to the side, and do a sound check from the last row at the same time of day as the ceremony. Skipping the mic to "keep it intimate" is how you get a quiet, beautiful ceremony that only the first two rows can describe later.

Top picks

  • The ceremony kit: a dual UHF bodypack lavalier system, so two people can be heard without handing a microphone back and forth.
  • The speaker: a battery-powered portable PA on a tripod stand, just off the front row, aimed at the guests, not at the couple.
  • The small extras that matter outdoors: fur windscreens, fresh rechargeable batteries, and a roll of gaffer tape.

Yes, even for a short ceremony and 40 guests

This is the question couples ask, usually while looking at a small backyard and a guest list that does not feel like "an event." Do we really need a microphone if the ceremony is ten minutes and everyone will be close? The people who skipped it are the ones who write, afterward, that they could see their parents smiling and could also see that their parents had not heard a word.

Wind is the main reason. A voice that is perfectly clear in a living room disappears on a lawn, especially if you are facing each other and not the guests, or if there is a road, a fountain, or a neighbor's mower two yards over. Forty people is already two or three rows and a back row that is farther away than it looks in a sketch. A hundred people, without a mic, is a pantomime.

Keeping it intimate is a real wish. A good small system does not ruin that. A pack of battery-powered speakers stacked like a concert will. One modest speaker, placed carefully, and two small microphones that disappear into a jacket or a dress, will let people lean in for the feeling and still hear the words. Our longer outdoor wedding sound system guide covers music, dancing, and neighbors. This post is the ceremony: how the vows get heard.

A simple ceremony system, and where each piece goes

A dual lavalier system is the straightforward setup: two small microphones, two bodypacks, one receiver, plugged into a speaker. Clip a lav on the officiant, who does most of the talking, and one on you, or one on each of you if you are both speaking and the officiant can share or use a handheld. A handheld wireless mic is a useful backup for a reading, a toast later, or the moment a lav pack fails.

The speaker should be a battery-powered PA, so you are not running a long cable across the aisle. Put it on a tripod stand, just off the front row, slightly in front of the couple, aimed at the guests. Do not put it behind the couple, aimed at their backs, and do not hide it in a bush that will muffle it. Raise it above sitting-head height. A speaker on the ground plays to shins.

If you need a little more length than the pack's cable, a long aux cable is cheaper than a second system. Tape down anything a foot or a dress could catch, with gaffer tape, not office tape that leaves a residue on a rented deck.

Wind, clothing, and a test at the real hour

A bare lavalier outdoors will pop and rumble in even a light breeze. Put a fur windscreen on every lav you plan to use, and do it before the wedding day, so you are not fighting a tiny snap on a silk dress at 3:55. Clip the mic a hand's width below the collarbone, not on a necklace that will bang against it, and not under three layers of fabric that will make you sound like you are speaking through a coat closet.

Batteries fail at inconvenient times. Charge everything the night before, put fresh rechargeable batteries in anything that takes AA or AAA, and have a spare set in the emergency kit. Turn the system on early enough that you are not doing this as guests sit down.

Then do a real test. Not in the living room the week before, or at least not only there. Stand in your actual spots, at the actual time of day, and have someone you trust stand in the back row and tell you, honestly, whether they can hear the softest voice. Wind is different at 5 p.m. than at noon. A neighbor's yard is louder on a Saturday. If the back row cannot hear, move the speaker, raise it, or turn it up a little, and test again. This is also the moment to find out that a pack will not clip to a particular dress, while you can still invent a plan.

Unplugged ceremonies, neighbors, and the rest of the day

An unplugged ceremony — guests without phones in the air — is compatible with a microphone. In fact, it is kinder: if people cannot film, they should at least hear. A line in the program about cameras is enough; our unplugged backyard wedding post has wording that does not scold. The photographer will still want a clean view, so keep the speaker off to the side and the packs hidden as well as you can.

If you are writing your own vows, a mic is how those sentences survive. Practice them once with the system on. Some people get quieter when they get emotional, and a lav that was "fine" at rehearsal may need to be an inch closer, or a speaker that was polite at noon may need a nudge. Keep a printed copy of the vows with the officiant. Our guide to writing wedding vows is about the words; the microphone is how the back row gets them.

Be a good neighbor about volume. A ceremony system should be clear, not loud. If you are also playing processional music through the same speaker, test that too, and keep it below the "the next yard can hear the lyrics" level. Save the bigger system, if you have one, for later, and look at local quiet hours before you plan a late last dance.

If you already have a DJ or a band, ask them to handle the ceremony mics. That is often the easiest path, and it is worth a line in the contract: two lavs, a speaker at the altar, a sound check at the site. If you do not have a DJ, the small kit in this post is enough. You do not need to hire a full production to be heard in a backyard.

What to skip

Skip "we'll just speak up." Skip a single handheld passed around like a toast microphone during the vows. Skip hiding a Bluetooth speaker in a flower arrangement and hoping. Skip discovering, as you walk out, that the pack is dead or the dress has nowhere to clip it. And skip going unplugged and unamplified at the same time in the wind. People will be present. They just will not know what you said.

A short ceremony-mic checklist

Get a dual lav system, a battery-powered speaker, a stand, windscreens, and spare batteries. Decide who wears which mic, and have a handheld as a backup. Clip, tape, and test from the last row at the real hour, in the real clothes if you can. Charge everything the night before. Then say the vows you wrote, and let the people you invited actually hear them.
