A cool tray dessert, not a cookie table

The useful purchases are pan-and-cut tools, not a cake stand. A metal 9x13 pan or a glass baker, lined with parchment, will give you a slab you can lift. A juicer and a zester get the lemons done without a fight. A bench scraper or a long knife, wiped between cuts, makes even squares. A fine sieve or a shaker is for the sugar at the end. That is the kit this post is actually about.

Lemon bars are a wedding workhorse for a reason you can defend in a house kitchen. They can be baked the day before, they cut into a count you can actually plan, they do not need a freezer the way ice cream does, and they sit on a dessert table without asking for a server with a torch. They fail when the crust is raw, when the filling is overbaked and tight, or when you cut a warm slab and watch it smear. They also fail when the sugar is dusted at dawn and has vanished into a damp top by the time guests arrive. The recipe is simple. The chill and the cut are the job.

They are not a cookie table, which is a station of cookies people take by the handful. They are not a pie bar, and they are not a peach cobbler you serve warm with a spoon. They are a cool, set square, somewhere between a bar cookie and a tray tart. If you already have a soaked cake like tiramisu or tres leches, lemon bars are a good second dessert: a different temperature of feeling, and a different plate. If the dessert table is already three creamy things, this is the bright one.

Two 9x13 pans, cut into twelve generous squares each, will serve about twenty-four. Four pans will serve about forty-eight. If you want a smaller square on a mixed dessert table, you can cut each pan into eighteen and stretch the same bake a little. This post scales the recipe to the generous count, because people will take a full bar when it is the only lemon thing in the room. Keep a reserve in the refrigerator, and do not put every pan out at once on a warm afternoon.

A shortbread crust, a lemon filling, and a real chill

The crust is a shortbread you press into the pan, not a rolled pie dough. Softened butter, flour, powdered sugar, and a little salt, mixed until it looks like damp crumbs that hold when you pinch them, then pressed in an even layer, including the corners. Parchment with an overhang will let you lift the whole slab out later, which makes the cut kinder. Bake the crust until it is just turning color at the edges, not pale and raw, and not dark. A glass pan may need a few more minutes than a metal one; watch the crust, not only the clock.

The filling is eggs, sugar, fresh lemon juice, zest, and a little flour so it sets. Juice the lemons with a press, and zest them first with a zester, while they are still whole. Bottled juice will make a filling that tastes like bottled juice. Pour the filling onto the hot or warm crust — that is the usual method, and it helps the two layers like each other — and bake until the center is just set and no longer liquid when you nudge the pan. It should not brown much. If it souffles and then collapses, you have gone a little far; it will still taste like lemon bars, and it will still slice if you chill it.

Cool the pans on a rack until they are no longer hot, then refrigerate them until they are truly cold. A warm bar will drag and tear. Overnight is ideal. A few hours will do if the refrigerator is actually cold and the pans are not stacked tight against a pot of soup. Cover them once they are cool, so they do not pick up onion from the rest of the refrigerator.

For the cut, lift the slab out on the parchment if you can, or cut it in the pan with a long knife you wipe between passes. A bench scraper helps you keep the lines straight. Trim a very thin edge if you want a tidy tray, and eat the trimmings in the kitchen. Dust powdered sugar after the cut, close to service, with a sieve or a shaker. If you sugar them and then cover them for hours, the top will go damp and the white will fade. A second light dusting at the table is allowed, and looks like you meant it.

Wedding lemon bars for a crowd

The recipe below is for two 9x13 pans, about twenty-four generous squares, or four pans if you double it. Bake it ahead, chill it thoroughly, and cut it cold. Keep the reserve cool, and sugar the top when people are actually about to eat.

Wedding Lemon Bars

A press-in shortbread crust and a lemon filling of eggs, sugar, juice, and zest, baked in 9x13 pans, chilled thoroughly, and cut into squares. Dust powdered sugar close to service. Two pans serve about 24 generous squares; four pans, about 48. Keep the trays cool.

About 24 or 48 squares · Prep 25 minutes plus chilling · Bake 45 to 55 minutes

Directions

  1. Heat the oven to 350°F. Line two 9x13 pans with parchment, leaving an overhang. (For the larger yield, use four pans, or bake in two waves if your oven will not hold four fairly.)
  2. Mix the softened butter, 4 cups flour, 1 cup powdered sugar, and 1 teaspoon salt until the mixture holds when pinched. Divide it between the pans and press it into an even layer, including the corners. Bake until the crust is just colored at the edges, about 18 to 22 minutes.
  3. While the crust bakes, whisk the eggs, granulated sugar, lemon juice, zest, and 1/2 cup flour until smooth. Pour the filling over the warm crust and return the pans to the oven. Bake until the filling is just set in the center, about 22 to 28 minutes more. Do not wait for it to brown.
  4. Cool the pans on a rack, then refrigerate until thoroughly cold, at least 3 hours or overnight. Cover once cool.
  5. Lift the slabs out, or cut them in the pan, into 12 generous squares per pan (or 18 smaller). Wipe the knife between cuts. Dust with powdered sugar close to service, and keep the reserve in the refrigerator.

Double every crust and filling amount for four pans. A glass pan may need a few extra minutes on the crust; watch the color. Do not dust the sugar hours ahead under a lid. Keep the bars cool, especially on a warm afternoon.

A cool hold, a clean cut, and a label

Keep the cut bars in the refrigerator, or on a tray nested over ice if the table is outdoors and the day is warm. A lemon bar can sit at a pleasant room temperature for a service window; it should not sit in a sunny backyard for the length of a reception. Put out a modest tray and refill from the cold. The same habit you already use for a leftover plan starts here: a reserve, a lid, and a person who will not leave the last pan in the sun.

Label them in ordinary words: lemon bars, with eggs, butter, and wheat. People who need to know will not always ask. If you add a raspberry or a different citrus, say so. A small card is enough. Tongs or a small spatula will keep the tray from becoming a pile of broken corners; fingers will not, once the sugar is on top.

If you must travel with them, take the slab cold and whole, and cut at the house, or cut at home and pack the squares in a single layer, with parchment between layers, in a shallow lidded pan. A deep, warm stack is how the edges glue together. Powdered sugar still waits until you are there.

Buy more lemons than the juice line suggests. Zest needs a firm fruit, and some lemons are stingy, and you do not want to be short in the middle of a whisking. Juice them the same day you bake, and strain out the seeds. If you want a slightly sharper bar, you can add a spoon of juice to a pan and taste the filling before it goes in the oven — raw filling with raw eggs is not a tasting plan for a crowd of helpers, so leave that to the cook, or trust the amounts and adjust next time. Do not invent a bottled-juice shortcut in a wedding batch unless that is honestly how you always make them, and you already like the result.

One oven may not hold four pans fairly. Bake in waves, and let the first wave begin its chill while the second bakes. A crowded oven will brown one corner and leave another liquid. Rotate pans if your oven has a hot side, and give the second wave a fresh look, not a copied timer from the first. The refrigerator is the bottleneck as often as the oven is. Clear a shelf before you turn the oven on.

A short lemon-bar checklist

Bake the crust until it is just colored, pour on the filling, and bake until the center is just set. Cool, then chill thoroughly. Cut cold, twelve generous squares to a pan, and dust the sugar close to service. Two pans serve about twenty-four; four, about forty-eight. Keep the reserve cool, and label the tray.

Lemon bars are a quiet kind of generous: a familiar square, a sharp filling, a crumb that holds, and a count you can finish the day before. If you treat the chill and the cut as part of the recipe, they will look like you meant them. If you treat them as a cake you slice warm on the way out the door, they will look like a hurry. That work is ordinary, and it is worth doing early, while the refrigerator still has a shelf, instead of discovering a soft slab when the coffee is already poured and the only thing left to do is hope a knife will tidy what time in the cold was meant to do.
