A Wedding Pancake Bar of Sheet-Pan Buttermilk Pancakes, Cut into Squares

A wedding pancake bar should be baked on a sheet pan and cut into squares, not flipped one by one for a hundred people. A buttermilk batter, a short topping line, and a brief hold will do more than a second waffle iron.

The short version. Mix a buttermilk pancake batter, spread it on greased half-sheet pans, bake until set, and cut into squares. Two half-sheets will feed about 24 as a brunch main; four will feed about 48. Hold the squares briefly, in a single layer, and top at the plate. This is not a waffle bar, which needs irons and a different batter. It can sit at a brunch reception or, for a smaller morning, beside coffee. Butter, warm maple syrup, and fruit are enough.

Not a waffle bar, and not a griddle for a hundred

You do not need a specialty aisle of props. You need a short, honest list. A half sheet pan, because almost every crowd recipe in this house starts on a half-sheet, a chef knife, a electric griddle 20 inch, a pancake dispenser bottle, a icing spatula, so icing goes on close to service, not in a clump from a dinner spoon, a maple syrup dispenser, a pie server, and a cake and pie server, so slices come off cleanly, instead of with the same dinner knife you just used on a nut pie.

Pancakes and waffles get confused in planning conversations because both are batter and both like maple syrup. They are not the same station. A waffle has to be cooked in an iron, in batches, and held with care so it does not steam. A pancake, at wedding scale, should not be flipped to order unless you have a short list and a person who wants that job. A sheet pan can bake a whole layer at once, and you can cut it into squares that people can pick up or plate.

This is a brunch or late-morning idea, not a dinner main, and not the getting-ready breakfast you eat in a robe. It can be the main at a farewell brunch only if the list is the right size and someone who is not exhausted owns the oven. For a reception brunch, plan the larger batch if pancakes are the main, or the smaller one if you also have eggs or a savory tray.

Two half-sheet pans, cut into modest squares, will feed about 24 as a main. Four pans will feed about 48. If you cut huge pieces, you will run short. If pancakes are a side, you can make less, but then you need the other food to be real. Count plates, not a hopeful “people will only take one.”

The oven is the limit. Four pans may not fit at once, and a single oven that is also doing bacon or a casserole will not do all of it well. Write the waves down. Pancakes bake quickly, which is a gift, and they wait poorly, which is the constraint. A 200°F hold, in a single layer on a rack, will keep them decent for a short time. A covered stack will turn them into a steamed sponge.

Do not promise a special mix-in — blueberries in every square, a chocolate chip pan, a gluten-free batter — unless you have already baked that version successfully in the same pans. A plain buttermilk pan, with fruit on the side, is a complete bar. A burnt blueberry corner in a pan you have never tested is not.

If the morning is a farewell for a small list, two pans may already be generous. If the morning is the reception itself, look at the rest of the menu before you double everything. Pancakes plus a full egg casserole plus a pastry table is three breakfasts. Pick the one you can cook well, and let the others be fruit and coffee. Guests would rather have hot squares and a real cup than a long table of lukewarm ideas.

Batter, pans, and a short topping line

The batter is a standard buttermilk pancake batter: flour, buttermilk, eggs, melted butter, baking powder, a little baking soda, sugar, and salt. Mix it gently. A few lumps are fine. You can mix it shortly before baking; a long rest in the refrigerator will thicken it, and you may need a splash more buttermilk to spread it, not a vigorous beating that knocks the air out.

Grease the half-sheets well, including the corners, or line them with parchment and grease the parchment. Pour the batter in an even layer, not a puddle at one end. The layer should be modest, like a thick pancake, not a cake. Bake in a hot oven until the center is set and the edges are browned. If the middle still wetly jiggles, give it a few more minutes rather than cutting into batter.

Cut with a knife into squares you would actually like to eat, and move them to a rack or a board. A runner and towels will take the syrup. Top at the plate: butter, warm maple syrup in a small pitcher, and a bowl of berries or cut fruit. Warm the syrup; do not put an open saucepan of hot syrup at elbow height on a buffet.

If you want one savory add-on, keep it separate and labeled: a pan of bacon, a bowl of herbed yogurt, a warmed berry compote in a small pot. Do not scatter raw fruit or a crumbly topping over the sheet before you bake unless you have tested it. Wet fruit can leave a gummy well in the middle of the pan.

Coffee belongs next to this station, not in another room. People will stand with a plate and a cup if you let them. A long line that crosses a doorway will stall both the pancakes and the rest of the brunch. Set plates and forks first, then the board, then toppings, then an exit.

Buttermilk is worth buying for this batter. Ordinary milk plus a squeeze of lemon can work in a pinch at home; for a wedding tray, real buttermilk gives you a more predictable rise and a tang that stands up to syrup. If you must use a substitute, test that version in the same pan, because the batter’s thickness is how you get an even sheet instead of a cake in the middle and a cracker at the edge.

Keep a little extra buttermilk on the counter when you mix, in case the batter is too thick to spread. Add it by the splash, not by the cup, and stop when the batter evens out under a spatula. A batter that pours like soup will bake into a thin, sad sheet. A batter you have to shove into corners will bake unevenly and may still be wet in the middle when the edges are already dark.

Sheet-pan buttermilk pancakes

The recipe below is for two half-sheet pans or four. Two pans are about 24 as a brunch main; four pans, about 48. Bake close to service, and hold only briefly.

Sheet-Pan Buttermilk Pancakes

A buttermilk pancake batter baked on half-sheet pans and cut into squares. Two pans feed about 24 as a brunch main; four pans, about 48. Hold the squares in a single layer in a low oven for a short time, and top at the plate.

2 or 4 half-sheets (about 24 or 48 as a main) · Prep 15 minutes · Bake 12 to 18 minutes per wave

ItemTwo half-sheets (about 24)Four half-sheets (about 48)
All-purpose flour4 cups (about 500 grams)8 cups (about 1 kilogram)
Granulated sugar1/4 cup1/2 cup
Baking powder2 tablespoons1/4 cup
Baking soda2 teaspoons4 teaspoons
Kosher salt2 teaspoons4 teaspoons
Buttermilk4 cups8 cups
Large eggs48
Unsalted butter, melted and slightly cooled, plus more for the pans1/2 cup (1 stick)1 cup (2 sticks)
  1. Heat the oven to 425°F. Grease two or four half-sheet pans well, or line them with parchment and grease the parchment, including the corners.
  2. In a large bowl, or in two bowls for the larger batch, whisk the flour, sugar, baking powder, baking soda, and salt. In another bowl, whisk the buttermilk, eggs, and melted butter until combined. Pour the wet ingredients into the dry and stir until just combined. A few small lumps are fine. Do not beat the batter smooth.
  3. Divide the batter evenly among the pans and spread it into a modest, even layer. Bake until the center is set and the edges are browned, 12 to 18 minutes. If you are baking in waves, keep the remaining batter cool and stir it gently before the next pans go in; do not add more baking powder.
  4. Cut into squares and transfer to a rack set over a pan in a 200°F oven, in a single layer, for up to 15 or 20 minutes. Do not stack them or cover them tightly. Serve with butter, warm maple syrup, and fruit, topped at the plate.

Test one pan the week before in your own oven and your own half-sheets. For a list of 24 as a main, use two pans, or plan other food and bake less. Do not hold syrup-soaked squares in the oven. If you add fruit to the batter itself, test that version; wet fruit can leave a gummy middle.

Service, mess, and what not to add

Syrup will travel farther than you think. Put a tray under the pitcher, and keep a towel in reach. Forks matter; these squares are not always finger food if they are soft and loaded. If you want people to eat standing in a yard, cut smaller and go easier on the syrup at the station, with more syrup on a second table that has plates.

A coffee bar next to pancakes is a natural pairing, as long as the two lines do not block each other. Keep milk and mugs off the pancake board. Keep unsweetened butter away from a dairy-free guest’s fruit bowl if that is a real need on your list, and say so on a card.

What not to add: a chocolate fountain, a half-dozen flavored syrups, a made-to-order egg station run by the same person, or a promise of hot pancakes for latecomers an hour after the pans came out. Latecomers can have fruit, coffee, and whatever is still decent on the rack. You can bake a second wave if a helper is free and the oven is free. You should not keep a first wave under a lid until it is sad.

Cleanup should start before the syrup sets into a permanent glaze on the pans. Soak the sheets, get leftover batter into the refrigerator or into the oven as a last pan, and take the fruit off a hot table. The station closes when brunch closes.

If you have leftover squares that are still decent, they can go into a napkin-lined basket for people who are leaving, or into the refrigerator for the household. They are not as good later, and that is all right. What you should not do is leave a sticky board in the yard while speeches start, or try to refresh the first wave with a new drizzle of syrup in the oven. Syrup in a hot oven is a mess, and it will not revive a pancake that has already waited too long.

A short pancake-bar checklist

A week ahead, test one half-sheet in your oven and decide on two pans or four. Shop for buttermilk, butter, syrup, and fruit. Confirm oven space against anything else that has to bake that morning.

The day of, mix the batter shortly before baking, grease the pans, and bake close to service. Set plates, butter, warm syrup, and fruit. Hold squares in a single layer for a short time only.

When brunch ends, soak the pans, refrigerate what is left, and stop cooking. A sheet-pan pancake bar is a way to serve pancakes to a wedding list without flipping a hundred rounds. Two pans or four, a short topping line, and a brief hold will do the whole job, and they are enough. A backyard brunch does not need a diner’s worth of choices. It needs hot food, a place to stand, and a person who can take the last pan out of the oven on time. Give someone that job, then let the syrup and the fruit do the rest. That is a pancake bar a wedding list can actually eat, and it is the whole method. Keep it at that size, and the morning will stay kind.
