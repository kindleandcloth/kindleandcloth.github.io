A Wedding Pie Table You Can Actually Slice and Serve

A wedding pie table works when you know how many slices you need, who is baking or buying, and how you will cut and serve without turning the dessert into a second job. One homemade apple crumb slab pie on a half-sheet can sit beside bakery pies and actually feed a list.

The short version. If pie is the dessert, plan on one modest slice per guest, plus a little extra, and three or four kinds at most. A nine-inch pie usually gives you six to eight slices; a slab pie on a half-sheet pan gives you about 24 small squares. Mix bakery pies with one homemade pan if you want a project that is actually finishable. Keep whipped cream cold, not as a melting sundae bar, and label nuts. The dessert-table guide still holds for shade, height, and leftovers; this post is the pie version, the way the cookie table is the cookie version. Do not also build a full cake you do not need.

Top picks

  • Best for most backyard lists: one homemade slab pie plus a few bakery pies, sliced to order or just ahead of service, with cream on the side.
  • Best if you have helpers who bake: a short list of kinds, one coordinator, and a backup pie or a slab already in the house.
  • Useful kit: half sheet pan, pyrex pie plates, pie server, cake and pie server, and tiered pie stand — the tools this post is actually about, not a generic stand-in.

How many pies, and who bakes them

The right objects are the ones you will still use after the weekend is over, or the ones that quietly do a job on the day. A half sheet pan, because almost every crowd recipe in this house starts on a half-sheet, a pyrex pie plates, a pie server, a cake and pie server, a tiered pie stand, so a tall pie is not sitting in a puddle of its own juices, a pie bird ceramic, a pie crust shield, and a pie carrier insulated, if anyone is driving a homemade pie across town.

Start with the headcount, not with a flavor list. If pie is the dessert, plan on one slice per person and a small surplus — about 10 to 20 percent — because some people will take a sliver of a second kind, and because a pie that looks abundant at six o’clock should not look scraped at seven. A standard nine-inch pie, cut honestly, gives you six to eight wedding slices, not twelve restaurant slivers. For 50 guests, that is on the order of seven to nine nine-inch pies, or a slab plus four or five rounds, not a photogenic stack you cannot portion.

Three or four kinds are enough. Apple, something chocolate or cream if you can keep it cold, and one other fruit or a pecan if you can label the nuts, will cover a table. A fifth kind is a chance to be short on the ones people actually want. You do not need a savory pie at dessert unless that is a joke you are sure of. You do not need mini pies for everyone; they are charming and they multiply the work.

Decide who is responsible. If family is baking, write the kinds, the sizes, the delivery time, and who is bringing a serving tool. One person should be the coordinator, the way a cookie table needs a coordinator, so you do not end up with eight apple pies and no plates. Bakery pies are not a failure. They are how a lot of good pie tables actually get filled. Taste a shop once, order in time, and keep the receipt and the pickup window in the same notebook you are using for the rest of the day.

The homemade contribution, if you want one, should be a pan you can finish: the apple crumb slab below, baked on a half-sheet, cooled, and cut into squares. It travels, it slices more cleanly than a stack of lattice rounds in a hurry, and it looks like a decision rather than a leftover from a test bake. If you also like a traditional round, bake one or two in a glass pie plate and let the slab do the volume.

Setting, slicing, and holding

Fruit pies can sit at a cool room temperature for the length of a reception if they are not in the sun. Cream pies, custard pies, and anything with whipped topping belong on ice or in a refrigerator until service, and should not be treated like an apple pie that can wait on a porch. The dessert-table guide’s advice about shade, height, and bugs still applies. A backyard in July is a different table from a house in February. In winter, you are protecting pies from a radiator and from a cold wind, not from melting chocolate, but you are still protecting them.

Slice just ahead of service, or slice to order if you have a person who can do that without slowing the line to a halt. A slab pie can be cut into a grid: five by five is 25 small squares from one half-sheet, which is about right. Put the pies at different heights if you want the table to look finished, but leave room to cut. A board, a rimmed tray, and a sharp knife you will actually wash between a nut pie and a plain fruit pie are more useful than a stand you cannot work on.

Whipped cream, or a pint of vanilla ice cream if you have a freezer you can reach, should be a side, not a carnival. Keep cream cold, offer a spoon, and do not build a sundae bar you have to staff. Ice cream in July, outdoors, is a promise you may not be able to keep. In a winter house, it is easier, and still optional. A pitcher of coffee or tea nearby will be used. Labels should name the flavor and the nuts. “Aunt’s pie” is a nice second line, not a substitute for “pecan.”

Apple Crumb Slab Pie for a Wedding Table

A press-in crust on a half-sheet pan, a thick apple filling, and a butter crumb top, cut into squares. One pan serves about 24 modest pieces; two pans serve about 48. Cool until the filling has set before you slice.

One half-sheet (about 24 squares) or two half-sheets (about 48) · Prep 40 minutes · Bake 40 to 50 minutes · Total about 1 hour 30 minutes, plus cooling

  1. Heat the oven to 375°F. For the crust, stir the flour, sugar, and salt in a bowl. Cut in the cold butter until the mixture looks like coarse crumbs with a few larger pieces. Sprinkle in the ice water a tablespoon at a time, stirring with a fork, until the dough holds together when you pinch it. Press it evenly into the bottom and up the sides of an ungreased half-sheet pan (about 18 by 13 inches). If the kitchen is warm, chill the crust for 15 minutes.
  2. Toss the apples with the filling sugars, flour, lemon juice, cinnamon, and salt until every slice is coated. Spread the apples in an even layer in the crust. They will mound a little; they should not spill over the rim.
  3. For the crumb, mix the flour, brown sugar, cinnamon, and salt. Cut in the cold butter until the mixture forms clumps the size of peas and gravel. Squeeze a few larger clumps with your fingers, then scatter the crumb evenly over the apples.
  4. Bake until the filling is bubbling in the center and the crumb and crust are a deep gold, 40 to 50 minutes. If the crumb browns early, tent the pan loosely with foil for the last stretch. Cool the pan on a rack until the filling has set, at least 45 minutes, before you cut it into a grid of about 24 squares. For the larger batch, bake two pans, rotating them once if your oven has hot spots. Serve at room temperature, or barely warm, with cold cream on the side if you like.

Use apples that hold their shape — Granny Smith, Honeycrisp, or a mix — not a sauce apple that will collapse into soup. The pie should be cut only after the filling has stopped being molten. Leftover squares keep, covered, at cool room temperature for a day, or refrigerated if your kitchen is warm.

The rest of the dessert, and a table that can be cleared

If pie is the dessert, let it be the dessert. A small bowl of fruit, or coffee, is a companion, not a second concept. A cake in addition is only worth it if someone specifically wanted to cut a cake, and then the cake can be modest. A cookie table and a pie table together is usually too much, unless your families are both doing a tradition on purpose and you have the people to staff both.

Plates, forks, and napkins should be at the beginning of the line, not in a basket people have to hunt for with a slice already in hand. Someone should be assigned to cut, to wipe the knife, and to retire an empty tin so the table does not look scavenged. Leftover fruit pie can go home in a labeled tin; leftover cream pie should go home cold, or not at all, if it has been sitting out. Be more honest than generous with anything dairy that has been on a warm table.

In a small house, the pie table can be the sideboard, cleared of gifts, or the kitchen counter if that is the only surface that works. Outdoors, use a table in the shade, with a way to cover the pies when the line pauses. A thermometer is not required for a fruit slab, but it is useful if you are also holding a hot main in the same kitchen and you are already in the habit of using one. Keep the pie work out of the way of the dinner work: bake the slab the morning of, or the day before, and do not start a crust while guests are arriving.

A short pie-table checklist

A week or more ahead, decide the kinds, the source, and the coordinator. Order bakery pies, or write the family list, with sizes and a delivery time. Shop for pans, boxes, and the apples if you are baking the slab. The day before, bake the slab, or bake it in the morning, and let it cool on a rack. Chill the cream. Set the board, the knife, the plates, and the labels.

At dessert, cut what you need, refill from a closed backup, and do not decorate the table while people are waiting for a slice. At the end, pack fruit pies and tins with the name of the kind on them, and be conservative with anything that needed to stay cold. A wedding pie table is not a bakery display. It is a way to give people a slice of something they recognize, in a quantity you can actually serve, and then to put the knife down and go quietly back into the main room again. Coffee, or a cookie table as a second sweet, is enough beside pie. The dessert-table guide is the place if you want a mixed spread instead, and a coffee bar will be used more than a third pastry.
