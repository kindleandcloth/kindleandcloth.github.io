How to Host a Rehearsal Dinner at Home

The short version: A rehearsal dinner at home works when you treat it like a generous dinner party, not like a second wedding. Keep the list tighter than tomorrow's guest list when you can, seat people at real tables, and cook a menu you can finish before the doorbell. Someone who is not getting married the next morning should be the host-on-the-ground. End early enough that everyone can sleep.

The rehearsal dinner is a thank-you to the people who will stand with you, plus, often, the out-of-towners who have already traveled. It is not a preview of tomorrow's floral program, and it does not have to match the wedding. Hosting it at home is a reasonable, common choice: you already have a kitchen, you can cook food people actually like, and you can end the night without a venue cutoff you don't control. It is also a real amount of work the night before a wedding. The goal is a warm table, not a second event you have to recover from.

If the meal you're hosting at home is the wedding dinner itself, that's a different plan — see a small wedding dinner at home. This post is the night before: shorter, smaller, and designed so you can still get up and get married.

Who you invite, and what this dinner is for

You do not need a specialty aisle of props. You need a short, honest list. A slow cooker, for a hold, not for starting a raw batch at the last hour, a serving board, a sandwich platter tray, a macaroni and cheese pan deep, a cocktail napkin dispenser, for water, tea, or a non-alcoholic drink that should not live in a jug with no ladle, a pie carrier insulated, a lasagna pan deep ceramic, and a hurricane candle holders, which is the actual tool this part of the day uses, not a generic stand-in.

Traditionally the rehearsal dinner follows the rehearsal and includes the wedding party, their partners, both families, and the officiant. Many couples also add out-of-town guests so those people have something to do the night they arrive. That last choice is kind, and it is how a twelve-person dinner becomes thirty. Decide which dinner you are giving before you text a single invitation.

A tight list — party, partners, parents, officiant — is easier to seat in a house and easier to cook for. If you want to include out-of-town guests, consider a short open-house hour after the seated dinner, or a simple dessert and coffee later, so the actual meal can stay at a size your table can hold. You do not owe a plated dinner to everyone on tomorrow's list. You do owe a clear invitation that says who is invited, where to park, what time dinner starts, and when you hope people will head out.

Who pays is a separate conversation, and there is no single right answer anymore. What matters for a house dinner is that the person doing the shopping, cooking, and cleaning is not surprised by the size of the list, and that the couple is not footing a hidden second wedding in their own kitchen unless they chose that. Put the budget on one page: food, drinks, flowers if any, rental chairs if you need them, and a cleaner the next morning if you can swing it.

The house, the table, and where people actually sit

Walk the rooms the way a guest will. Coats need a bed or a rack, not a mountain on a dining chair. Bags and gifts need a landing spot that is not the dinner table. Bathrooms need a hand towel, soap, and a small wastebasket that isn't already full. If twenty people will share one powder room, that is information; it isn't a deal-breaker, but it is why you don't also schedule a long cocktail hour in the same tight hall.

Seat people at real tables if you can. A dining table plus a second folding table with a cloth is better than a vague "we'll just stand." Borrow or rent chairs rather than asking grandparents to take the floor. A plain runner, a few small vases, and candles in steady hurricanes or flameless votives if the table is crowded will do more than a centerpiece you have to move every time a platter comes down. Leave room for water glasses and serving dishes. This is a dinner, not a styling test.

If the house can't hold the list, change the list or change the plan — a backyard table, a garage with the cars out, a neighbor's dining room, or a private room at a restaurant. Stretching a two-bedroom into a seated forty is how people eat on stairs. A tight, comfortable twenty is a better night than a famous-looking forty.

Kids, dogs, and neighbors deserve a mention in the invitation if they will be part of the evening. If the dog will be crated, say so to guests who are afraid of dogs. If children are welcome, plan a place for them to eat and something quiet to do that is not your wedding seating chart.

A menu you can finish before the doorbell

The menu should be food you have cooked for a group before, or food a friend or a caterer is bringing in already finished. The night before your wedding is not the night to test a five-course tasting. Choose one main that can be assembled earlier in the day or the day before — lasagna, a baked pasta, a braise, a roasted chicken situation you can rest and slice, a big pot of chili or a curry that holds in a slow cooker. Add a green salad you can dress at the last minute, bread, and something simple for people who don't eat the main. That's a dinner.

Family-style platters at the table beat a buffet in a doorway. If you need to keep a main warm, use the oven on low, a slow cooker, or a chafing dish you already know how to light — the same kind of thinking as a small DIY catering setup, just for fewer people. Don't start frying when the first car pulls up.

Ask about allergies and serious restrictions when you invite people, and actually cook to the answers. A clearly marked vegetarian main is kinder than a side salad billed as a meal. Label dishes if you are doing a spread: nuts, dairy, gluten, shellfish. You do not need printed menus with a poem.

Drinks can be as simple as wine, beer, a pitcher of something non-alcoholic, and plenty of water. A glass dispenser of water with citrus or a big iced tea saves you from opening bottles all night. You do not need a full bar the night before a wedding. You do need a plan for who will pour, and a firm end to the pour so the morning stays intact. Coffee or tea at the end of the meal is a gift to the people who have to drive, and to you.

Dessert can be a bought cake, a tray of cookies, a pie you slice, or fruit and cheese on a board. If someone offers to bring a dessert, say yes and give them a time to drop it off. Save hero baking for a different weekend.

Timing the rehearsal, the dinner, and the next morning

Work backward from sleep. If the wedding starts at 3, and you want people home by 10, the rehearsal dinner cannot start at 8:30. A common, humane shape is: rehearsal in the late afternoon, a little buffer for travel and a change of clothes, dinner at a set time, a short toast, and a clear goodbye. Put the times on the invitation and on the day-of timeline so tomorrow's crew already knows tonight ends.

The rehearsal itself should be short and specific. Run the processional, the cues, and the recessional once or twice, not until everyone is hungry and annoyed. Then go eat. If some guests are only coming to dinner, they should not be asked to stand in the yard for a forty-minute practice.

Toasts belong here more naturally than they do at a rushed wedding reception, but they still need a lid. One or two people, a time limit you say out loud, and a host who can stand up and invite everyone to keep eating. Tomorrow already has speeches. Tonight is a table.

Out-of-town guests need parking notes, a weather note, and a sense of dress — "come as you are after the rehearsal" or "casual dinner at our place" is enough. If you are also sending a weekend plan, the dinner should appear as one line with an address and a time, not as a second puzzle.

  • Lock the guest list and dietary notes a week out, then stop adding seats
  • Assign a host-on-the-ground who is not the couple: greet, pour, run the oven, start the coffee
  • Set the table in the morning or the night before, including glasses, water, and serving utensils
  • Finish cooking, or finish reheating, before the first guest is due; ice and drinks ready at the same time
  • Plan coats, parking, and the restroom, not only the menu
  • Put a start time and a hoped-for end on the invitation
  • If you can, book a morning cleaner or a friend to do dishes after you leave the room
  • Get people out the door kindly, and go to bed

Flowers can be a few grocery stems. Playlists can be a phone in a bowl, at a volume that lets people talk. Favors are unnecessary. A guest book is unnecessary. Photographs can be a friend with a phone and the lights on. If you want the house to look a little dressed, do it the morning of, not at 5 p.m. with a sauce reducing.

Think about the neighbors the way you would for the wedding itself, just earlier. Let the adjacent houses know you'll have cars and conversation that evening, and give them a time you plan to quiet down. Keep music inside or low in the yard. A rehearsal dinner that runs loud until midnight in a residential street is a poor preview of a backyard wedding the next day, and you may need those neighbors' patience for parking tomorrow.

Leftovers are not a second dinner project at 11. Pack a clearly labeled plate for anyone who asked, put the rest in the fridge, and stop. If you want lunch for the getting-ready room tomorrow, make that decision before the dinner, and set aside a tray before guests arrive so you aren't assembling sandwiches in formalwear-adjacent chaos at midnight. Ice should go in a cooler or a clean sink, not in a bag that leaks on the grout until morning.

The morning after matters as much as the dinner. Overnight guests need sheets and a clear time when the house has to shift into wedding mode. Dishes should not be the couple's 7 a.m. project. Trash should go out before you sleep if the truck comes early. The rehearsal dinner was a success if people felt looked after, the food was enough, and you can still get married on a full night's rest. That is a better story than a perfect napkin fold at midnight.
