A Cheese Fondue Station for a Small Wedding, Not a Hundred-Person Buffet

Cheese fondue can be a lovely wedding station if you keep it small, keep the cheese moving, and treat each pot as a table of eight or ten, not a buffet for a hundred. It is a shared pot, and it has to be run like one.

The short version. Plan by the pot, not by a crowd-size fantasy. One fondue pot will serve about eight to ten people who are actually sitting or standing with it. Two pots will serve about sixteen to twenty; four pots, about thirty-two to forty. Make a classic Gruyère-and-Emmental fondue, keep it over a gentle flame, and put out bread, a few dippers, and enough forks that no one has to share a spear. This is not a grazing table and not a standing cocktail bite you can walk away from. Assign a person to watch the flame and stir. If your list is larger than the pots you can honestly run, choose a different station.

A small station, not a hundred-person buffet

Buy the few things you will actually use, not a themed set you will store once. Fondue is a shared pot of melted cheese, kept just hot enough to stay smooth, with bread and a few other dippers on the table. It is convivial, and it is also fussy. The cheese will break if it boils hard, seize if it cools, and sit in a skin if no one stirs it. A wedding can absorb one or two well-run pots. It cannot absorb a buffet line of lukewarm cauldrons, or a single pot that forty people are asked to visit in a row.

Tell people what it is, and how it will work. “There will be cheese fondue at two tables, during cocktail hour” or “dinner is fondue, at seated tables of eight” is enough. Guests need to know they will be standing or sitting with a pot, not walking the lawn with a skewer. If you wanted a roaming bite, the cocktail-hour appetizer guide is a better place to start. If you wanted a spread of cheeses that do not have to be kept molten, use a grazing table.

Do not invent a scale the pots cannot support. One pot is a table of eight to ten. Two pots are sixteen to twenty. Four pots are thirty-two to forty, and that is already a lot of flames, a lot of cheese to grate, and a lot of watching. A hundred-person fondue buffet is a different kind of catering problem, with different equipment, and this post will not pretend a home kitchen can do it. If the list is larger, fondue can still appear as one small table among other food — a soup bar, a chili bar, a platter already holding — not as the only dinner for a number you cannot seat at the pots.

Alcohol in the pot is traditional and small: wine, and sometimes a little kirsch. Say so on a label, and offer bread and dippers to people who do not drink, knowing a little wine remains in the cheese. You can make a wine-free pot with a nonalcoholic white grape or a broth-based method you have already tested; do not invent that method on the wedding morning if you have never made it.

Children and open flames need a rule. The pot sits in the center of a stable table, not at the edge, not on a trailing tablecloth, not where a toddler can pull a fork. Stemless glasses help. A grown-up stays with the station.

How many pots, and how to keep the cheese moving

Borrow pots if you need a second or a fourth, and test every one in the week before, with a half-batch of the same cheese and wine. A wedding morning is a poor time to learn that a burner will not light, or that a ceramic pot runs hotter than you thought. Keep an extra box of fuel or a charged burner, according to the pot you own, and a plan for a kitchen rescue: a heavy saucepan on a very low burner, transferred back to the table pot if you must.

Grate the cheese ahead. A knife and a board will do for bread and apples; a grater, or a food processor if you already trust it, will do for the cheese. Toss the grated cheese with cornstarch so it is ready. Cut the bread into cubes that have a crust on at least one side, and keep a backup loaf. Apples can be cut closer to service so they do not brown; a little lemon on the cut face helps.

The hold is a gentle flame and a stirring hand, not a lid and a hope. Fondue should stay smooth and fluid, not boiling, and not cool enough to seize. If it thickens, a spoonful of warm wine can loosen it. If it breaks, a little lemon juice or a small cornstarch slurry, whisked in off the highest heat, will sometimes bring it back. If it will not come back, start a fresh pot in the kitchen rather than serving a greasy split. That is why a tested recipe and a little extra cheese in the refrigerator are part of the plan.

An instant-read thermometer is less central here than it is for a soup or a chili, but it can still tell you whether a pot has gone cold on a porch. Fondue does not belong outdoors in a wind, or on a table you cannot watch. Indoors, or on a still, sheltered porch with a person assigned to it, is the honest version.

The Gruyère and Emmental fondue

This is a classic Swiss-style cheese fondue: Gruyère and Emmental, a dry white wine, cornstarch, a cut garlic clove rubbed on the pot, a little lemon, nutmeg, and black pepper. Kirsch is optional. The smaller batch is two pots, enough for about sixteen to twenty people who are actually at the pots. The larger is four pots, about thirty-two to forty. Do not try to stretch one pot across a larger list by thinning the cheese.

Use cheese you can taste and grate. Pre-shredded bags are often coated and can make a grainy pot; grate it yourself if you can. A good dry white wine — something you would drink, not a cooking wine — is the liquid. The cornstarch helps the cheese stay together. Rubbing the pot with garlic is enough garlic for most tables; minced garlic in the cheese can be harsh if it sits.

Make the fondue shortly before you serve it, in a heavy pot on the stove, then transfer it to the table burner. It does not sit overnight the way a chili does. You can grate, cube, and set the table ahead. The melting is a last-hour job, and it should be done by someone who has already made this pot once.

Gruyère and Emmental Fondue for a Small Wedding

A classic cheese fondue of Gruyère and Emmental, meant to be served at the table in two pots (about 16 to 20 people) or four pots (about 32 to 40). Keep it over a gentle flame and stir. This is not a buffet for a hundred.

  1. Toss the grated cheeses with the cornstarch until the shreds are coated. Rub the inside of each pot with a cut garlic clove and discard the clove, or leave it in and retrieve it later if you prefer a slightly stronger scent.
  2. In a heavy pot on the stove — one pot at a time, or two if you can watch both — warm the wine over medium-low heat until it is hot but not boiling. Add the cheese by handfuls, stirring in a figure eight as it melts, and waiting until each addition is mostly smooth before you add more.
  3. When the cheese is melted and the pot is smooth, stir in the lemon juice, the nutmeg, the kirsch if you are using it, and a little black pepper. The fondue should coat a spoon and run in a ribbon, not sit in a lump and not look thin like a soup.
  4. Transfer to table pots over a gentle flame. Stir now and then, and do not let it boil. If it thickens, loosen it with a spoonful of warm wine. If it breaks, try a little more lemon or a small cornstarch slurry off the highest heat; if it will not come back, start a fresh pot. Keep extra grated cheese cold until you need it. This fondue is for serving now, not for a long unattended hold.

Label the pot if it contains wine or kirsch. Offer a dipper that is not bread if someone cannot eat gluten — apple is the usual, or a blanched vegetable you have already tested in the cheese. Provide plenty of forks or skewers. Leftover fondue can be cooled and later used as a cooked cheese, but it is not a second service of the same pot after an unsupervised hour.

Dippers, labels, and a table that can take drips

Bread is the main dipper: cubes with a crust, in a basket or on a board, refreshed before the board looks sad. Apple or pear, cut not too thin, is the usual fruit. Cornichons, a few blanched vegetables, or small boiled potatoes can sit beside if you have already tried them in this cheese. A raw landscape of untested dippers will disappoint, and some vegetables will water the pot.

Set the table so the pot is stable and central, with forks, plates or napkins, the bread, and an exit that does not require reaching across a flame. A runner can dress the eating tables; keep any cloth away from the burner. Put towels where drips will go. A sheet pan under a crowded board is a useful, unromantic safeguard in a kitchen staging area, even if it never appears at the table.

Water on the table matters, and a little wine is traditional if you are already serving it; a dispenser of water nearby will be used more than a signature cocktail at a station where people are managing a skewer. Stemless glasses are kinder next to a flame than a tall stem. Coffee can wait until the pot is finished, or live at a different table.

When the pot runs low, refill it from a fresh batch you have just made, or take it off and end the station. Do not stretch a tired, broken cheese with a splash of wine and a prayer. Turn the flame off when you are done, and let the pot cool out of the reach of children. Fondue that sat, unsupervised, is not a leftover you send home in a pretty jar without a second thought.

A short fondue checklist

A week out, count the list against the pots you actually have, and decide whether fondue is a dinner, a cocktail-hour table, or a bad fit. Test every pot with a half-batch. Grate a small trial and eat it, so you know the cheese and the wine. Assign a person to each table, or to every two pots, who is not also in the photographs at that hour.

The day before, buy the cheese, wine, bread, and dippers in the quantities you need. Grate the cheese, toss it with cornstarch, and refrigerate it. Cube what bread can be done early, and bag it so it does not dry into crumbs. Write a short label for the wine and the kirsch.

The day of, cut the fruit, set the tables, and melt the cheese shortly before you light the burners. Taste. Then run the window you published, and turn the flames off when it ends.

The measure of wedding fondue is a smooth pot, enough bread, and a table small enough to watch. Two pots, or four, and a person who will stir will do that work. Make the Gruyère and Emmental, keep the flame gentle, and let the station stay as small as the pots really are. That is an honest plate, and it does not need to feed a hundred.
