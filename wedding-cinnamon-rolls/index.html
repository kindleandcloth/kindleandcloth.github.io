Wedding cinnamon rolls are a slightly enriched yeast dough, filled, cut, and baked in 9x13 pans, then iced close to service. Two pans make about twenty-four rolls; four pans, about forty-eight. An overnight cold rise is optional, and a brief hold is honest. Icing them at breakfast and leaving them under a lid is not.

The short version. Mix a soft yeast dough, roll it with butter, brown sugar, and cinnamon, and bake the rolls in 9x13 pans until they are cooked through and browned on top. Ice them close to the moment people will eat. This is not a biscuit bar, and it is not a waffle bar or a pancake bar, which already have their own posts. A sheet for the roll-out, and a towel over the rising dough, will do most of the kitchen work. The icing waits.

Top picks

  • Best make-ahead: shape the rolls in the pans, cover, and refrigerate overnight, then bake in the morning after a short warm-up.

  • Best finish: a simple icing spread close to service, not at dawn under a cover that sweats.

Yeast dough in pans, iced close to service

The useful purchases for this are ordinary, and they should match the work. A pyrex 9x13, which is the pan this recipe is actually built around, a half sheet pan, a fine mesh cocoa sifter, a macaroni and cheese pan deep, a lasagna pan deep ceramic, which is the actual tool this part of the day uses, not a generic stand-in, a icing spatula, a cinnamon roll pan, and a bench scraper stainless, for dividing dough without tearing up the counter.

Cinnamon rolls are a brunch wedding’s idea of generosity, and they are a timing problem. Yeast dough needs a rise. Icing needs a roll that has cooled just enough not to melt into glaze soup, and not so long that the top has gone firm and dry. If you treat them like muffins you can bake at eight and ice at nine and park until noon, you will serve a sticky pan with a sad top. If you treat them like a yeast bread, with a plan for the rise and a late icing, they will taste like a bakery tray that happened to come from your oven.

Two 9x13 pans, twelve rolls each, will serve about twenty-four. Four pans will serve about forty-eight. That is one roll per person, which is enough if there is also fruit, coffee, and something savory. If rolls are the main sweet and people will take seconds, make the larger batch, or make a third pan and keep it back. These are rich. They disappear anyway.

This is not the morning casserole in the breakfast guide, and it is not a civil brunch reception written as a whole meal plan. Those posts already exist. This one is the tray of rolls: dough, filling, pans, icing, and a hold that does not pretend a frosted pan can sit for three hours as if it were a pound cake.

You can mix the dough the day before and refrigerate it after the first rise, or — the calmer wedding method — shape the rolls in the pans, cover them well, and let them rise slowly in the refrigerator overnight. In the morning they come out, lose the chill for thirty to forty-five minutes, and go into the oven. You are not kneading at six a.m. while someone is looking for the coffee urn.

If a family member “always fries a batch of doughnuts instead,” this is not that post, and you do not have to deep-fry for a crowd to have a sweet breakfast. Rolls in a pan, baked, iced late, will do the job, and the cook can still be in the room when guests arrive.

Mixing, rolling, and an overnight option

Warm the milk so it is just warm to the touch, not hot, and bloom the yeast with a little sugar if you are using active dry; instant yeast can go straight in with the flour. Add melted butter that has cooled, eggs, salt, and enough flour to make a soft, slightly tacky dough. It should be richer than a lean bread and not as heavy as a cookie dough. Knead until it is smooth and elastic, by hand or in a mixer, and let it rise in a lightly oiled bowl, covered with a towel, until it is puffy and doubled. A cold kitchen will take longer. Do not turn the oven into a proof box you forget about.

Roll the dough on a lightly floured counter or a sheet into a rectangle, brush it with softened butter, and cover it with brown sugar and cinnamon, with a pinch of salt in the filling so it is not only sweet. Roll it up from the long side, reasonably tight, and cut it with a sharp knife or a piece of thread so you do not squash the spirals. Twelve pieces go into each buttered 9x13, in four rows of three, with a little space to swell.

For a same-day bake, let the pans rise until the rolls look puffy and are touching, then bake. For an overnight rise, cover the pans tightly and refrigerate them as soon as they are cut and nestled. In the morning, give them time on the counter to take the chill off, until they look risen, and then bake. If they still look tight, wait longer. A cold, under-proofed pan will bake up dense, and icing will not fix it.

Bake until the tops are well browned and the centers are cooked through, not merely pale and set. A pale roll will taste doughy and will not forgive a hold. If the tops brown early, tent loosely with foil and let the centers finish. Let the pans sit for ten or fifteen minutes so the first rush of steam drops. That is when the kitchen smells finished, and it is still too soon to ice if you have an hour before service. Park the pans, uncovered or barely covered with a towel, in a warm, not hot, place. A lid will sweat and make the tops wet.

The icing is powdered sugar, a little milk or cream, vanilla, and a pinch of salt, whisked until it is thick enough to sit on a roll and thin enough to find the spirals. Cream cheese icing is a fine variant if you like it, and it should be said on a label if you use it, because of the dairy. Either way, spread it close to service. An iced pan that waits will soak, then crust, then look tired. A board is optional; the 9x13 is already a serving dish if you set it on a towel and put a spatula in it.

Cinnamon rolls in 9x13 pans

The recipe below makes two 9x13 pans, about twenty-four rolls, or four pans if you double it. You can shape the pans the night before and refrigerate them, or mix and bake on the same day. Ice close to service.

Wedding Cinnamon Rolls in 9x13 Pans

A slightly enriched yeast dough rolled with butter, brown sugar, and cinnamon, baked in 9x13 pans, and iced close to service. Two pans make about 24 rolls; four pans, about 48. An overnight cold rise in the pans is optional and calmer for a wedding morning.

2 or 4 pans (about 24 or 48 rolls) · Prep 40 minutes plus rising · Bake about 25 minutes

  1. Stir the yeast and a pinch of the sugar into the warm milk and let it stand until it is foamy, a few minutes, if you are using active dry yeast; instant yeast can go in with the flour. In a large bowl, combine the milk, the rest of the dough sugar, the cooled melted butter, the eggs, the salt, and most of the flour. Mix, then knead, adding flour as needed, until you have a soft, smooth, slightly tacky dough. Let it rise, covered, until doubled, about an hour in a warm room.
  2. Punch the dough down and divide it in half for two pans, or in quarters for four. Roll each piece into a rectangle. Spread with the softened butter, then the brown sugar and cinnamon mixed with a pinch of salt. Roll up from the long side and cut into twelve pieces. Nestle them in a buttered 9x13. For a same-day bake, let the pans rise until puffy. For overnight, cover tightly and refrigerate. In the morning, let the pans warm and finish rising on the counter before they go in the oven.
  3. Bake at 350°F until the tops are well browned and the centers are cooked through, about 22 to 28 minutes. Tent with foil if the tops brown early. Cool for at least 10 minutes. Whisk the powdered sugar with the milk, vanilla, and a pinch of salt until smooth, and spread the icing close to service. Serve from the pans, with a spatula. Do not ice at dawn and hold the pans under a lid, and do not serve rolls that are still raw in the middle because the tops looked done.

Icing, a brief hold, and the table

Set the pans on towels, with a spatula in each, and put plates or napkins where people can reach them without stretching over the coffee. One person can cut the first few if the line is tight; after that, people can take their own. A messy swirl is fine. A pan that has been sitting iced in a warm room will look glossy and then dull, and the edges will dry. If the hour is long, ice one pan, hold the second, and ice it when the first is low. That is a better “cinnamon roll bar” than a row of pans that were all finished at nine.

Label the rolls if the icing has cream cheese, and if anyone in the list cannot eat gluten or dairy, this is not their breakfast. A second sweet — fruit, or a labeled plate of something they can eat — should actually be on the table. Coffee belongs nearby, but not so close that a spill takes a pan with it. The coffee-bar work is a different post; here the job is to keep the rolls in range of the cups without making the table a hazard.

Leftover rolls, covered once they are fully cool, will be fine later in the day and softer the next morning. They can be warmed, briefly, and iced again with a leftover bit of glaze if you saved some. That is a kindness for the house, not a reason to bake a fifth pan “for later” when the list is only twenty-four. Count the pans, count the people, and ice late. That is a wedding tray of cinnamon rolls.

A short cinnamon-roll checklist

Mix a soft enriched dough, fill it, and cut it into 9x13 pans, twelve to a pan. Use the overnight refrigerator rise if it will make the morning calmer. Bake until the tops are browned and the centers are done. Ice close to service, and ice a second pan later if the hour is long.

Do not treat a yeast roll like a muffin you can frost and forget, and do not knead at dawn if the pans can rest overnight instead. A wedding cinnamon roll is a bakery tray from a home oven, with a rise you planned and an icing you held back until people were actually ready to eat. If you do that, the pans will still be warm, the icing will still look like icing, and the cook will not have spent the morning standing over a tray that was finished too early and could only get worse as the hour of the brunch went on, which is a poor way to start the whole wedding morning you had planned.
