Top picks

  • Best make-ahead: bake the day before, cool, cover, and cut the day of.
  • Best pans: metal 9x13 pans, lined with precut parchment, with lids for the trip or the hold.
  • Best cut: a long knife or a bench scraper, wiped between passes, after a full cool on a rack.

A cool tray of squares, not a cookie table

The useful purchases are pan-and-cut tools. A pair of metal 9x13 pans, lined with parchment, will give you slabs you can lift and cut. A cooling rack is how they finish setting instead of steaming in a hot pan on a closed stove. An offset spatula spreads the batter into the corners. A bench scraper or a long knife, wiped between cuts, makes even squares. Lids help you travel or hold them without a sticky wrap on the top. A grid cutter is optional. Half-sheet pans are for a thinner brownie if that is how you already bake.

Brownies are a wedding workhorse for a reason you can defend in a house kitchen. They can be baked the day before, they cut into a count you can actually plan, they travel without a freezer, and they sit on a dessert table without asking for a server or a torch. They fail when you cut them warm and watch the edges drag, when you overbake them into a dry cocoa cake, or when you leave a dark, uncovered pan in a hot car and call it a hold. The batter is simple. The cool and the cut are the job.

They are not a cookie table, which is a station of cookies people take by the handful, even if a brownie sometimes appears on that table as one more option. They are not lemon bars, and they are not a peach cobbler you serve warm with a spoon. They are a dense, cool square. If the dessert table is already bright and citrus, this is the dark one. If you already have a soaked cake or a pudding, brownies are a good second dessert: a different texture, and a different plate.

Two 9x13 pans, cut into twelve generous squares each, will serve about twenty-four. Four pans will serve about forty-eight. If you want a smaller square on a mixed dessert table, you can cut each pan into eighteen and stretch the same bake a little. This post scales the recipe to the generous count, because people will take a full square when it is the only chocolate thing in the room. Keep a reserve in a cool place, and do not put every pan out at once on a warm afternoon.

A boxed mix is allowed if that is honestly how you bake brownies for a crowd, and you already like the result. This recipe is a scratch cocoa batter so you can shop from a list and scale it without depending on a particular box. Do not invent a hybrid of half mix and half scratch in a wedding batch you have never made that way.

A cocoa batter, a just-set bake, and a real cool

The method is a melted-butter cocoa batter, not a creamed cake. Melt the butter, whisk in the sugar, then the eggs and vanilla, then the cocoa, flour, and salt, and stop when you no longer see dry flour. It will look thick and glossy. Spread it in the pans, into the corners, and get it in the oven. You do not need a mixer, and you do not want to beat a lot of air into it if you want a fudgy square rather than a cocoa cake.

Line the pans with parchment and a little overhang if you want to lift the slab out for a cleaner cut. A light buttering under the paper helps it stay put while you spread. Metal pans brown the edges a little more reliably than glass; if you use glass, watch the center and give it a few more minutes rather than copying a timer from a metal pan. One oven may not hold four pans fairly. Bake in waves, and let the first wave begin its cool while the second bakes.

Bake at 350°F until the center is just set and a toothpick comes out with a few moist crumbs, not wet batter and not bone dry. The top may look slightly shiny and may crack a little. That is fine. If you wait until the center is fully cake-like, you will have a dry edge by the time the pan is cool. Pull them a little earlier than your nerves want, and let the residual heat finish the work on the rack.

Cool the pans on a rack until they are completely cool, not “cool enough to touch the corner.” A warm brownie will smear, stick to the knife, and glue itself back together in a stack. Two or three hours on the rack, or a cool overnight in the kitchen once they are no longer hot, is the honest timeline. Cover them once they are cool, so they do not dry out and so they do not pick up the smell of last night’s onions. Cut them the day you serve, or the night before, once they are truly cool, and keep the squares in a single layer or with parchment between layers.

A long knife, wiped between passes, is enough. A bench scraper helps you keep the lines straight. Trim a very thin edge if you want a tidy tray, and eat the trimmings in the kitchen. You can dust a little powdered sugar or cocoa on top, close to service, or leave them plain. A thick frosting is optional and will make a warm-day outdoor hold messier. If you frost them, do it when they are cool, and keep that pan in the shade or in the house.

Wedding brownies for a crowd

The recipe below is for two 9x13 pans, about twenty-four generous squares, or four pans if you double it. Bake it ahead, cool it completely, and cut it cold to the touch. Keep the reserve cool, and put out a modest tray.

Wedding Brownies

A melted-butter cocoa brownie, baked in 9x13 pans until just set, cooled completely, and cut into squares. Two pans serve about 24 generous squares; four pans, about 48. Bake them the day before if you can.

About 24 or 48 squares · Prep 20 minutes · Bake 25 to 32 minutes, plus a full cool

ItemTwo 9x13 pans (about 24)Four 9x13 pans (about 48)
Unsalted butter2 cups (4 sticks)4 cups (8 sticks)
Granulated sugar4 cups8 cups
Large eggs816
Vanilla extract2 teaspoons4 teaspoons
Unsweetened cocoa powder1 1/2 cups3 cups
All-purpose flour2 cups4 cups
Kosher salt1 teaspoon2 teaspoons
Baking powder1 teaspoon2 teaspoons

Directions

  1. Heat the oven to 350°F. Line two 9x13 pans with parchment, leaving an overhang. (For the larger yield, use four pans, or bake in two waves if your oven will not hold four fairly.)
  2. Melt the butter, then whisk in the sugar until it looks like wet sand. Whisk in the eggs and vanilla, then the cocoa, flour, salt, and baking powder, and stop when you no longer see dry flour. The batter will be thick.
  3. Divide the batter between the pans and spread it into the corners. Bake until the center is just set and a toothpick comes out with a few moist crumbs, about 25 to 32 minutes. Do not wait for a dry crumb.
  4. Cool the pans on a rack until completely cool, at least 2 hours. Cover once cool.
  5. Lift the slabs out, or cut them in the pan, into 12 generous squares per pan (or 18 smaller). Wipe the knife between cuts. Keep the reserve in a cool place, in a single layer or with parchment between layers.

Double every amount for four pans. A glass pan may need a few extra minutes; watch the center. Do not cut them warm. If you add chocolate chips or walnuts, fold in about 2 cups for two pans, 4 cups for four, and say so on the label.

A cool hold, a clean cut, and a label

Keep the cut squares in a cool place, or in the house, and put out a modest tray. A brownie can sit at a pleasant room temperature for a service window; it should not sit in a sunny backyard for the length of a reception, especially if you have frosted it or if the day is hot enough to gloss the top. Refill from the reserve. The same habit you already use for a leftover plan starts here: a lid, a person, and a tray that does not live in the sun.

Label them in ordinary words: brownies, with wheat, butter, and eggs. If you added nuts, or if they were made near nuts, say so. People who need to know will not always ask. A small card is enough. Tongs or a small spatula will keep the tray tidy; a pile of loose squares will not, once the first corner crumbles.

If you must travel with them, take the slab cool and whole and cut at the house, or cut at home and pack the squares in a single layer, with parchment between layers, in a lidded pan. A deep, warm stack is how they fuse. If the wedding is at a house without a cool corner, arrive early enough to park the reserve inside, not in a trunk.

One mixer is not required, but one large bowl and a second pair of hands will make the four-pan batch kinder. Clear a rack and a counter before you turn the oven on. The cool is the bottleneck as often as the oven is. If you are also baking lemon bars or a cake that day, give the brownies their own wave and their own shelf. They will wait. They will not wait well if you cut them to “get ahead” while they are still soft in the middle.

A wedding brownie does not need a garnish to justify itself. A few strawberries on the tray, or a little bowl of berries, is enough if you want color. A scoop of ice cream is a different dessert, and it needs a freezer, the way a cobbler sometimes does. For most backyard tables, a cool, even square and a napkin are the whole service.

A short brownie checklist

Stir the batter, spread it in 9x13 pans, and bake until the center is just set. Cool completely. Cut twelve generous squares to a pan, or smaller if the table is mixed. Two pans serve about twenty-four; four, about forty-eight. Keep the reserve cool, and label the tray, including nuts if you used them.

Brownies are a quiet kind of generous: a familiar square, a dark crumb that holds, and a count you can finish the day before. If you treat the cool and the cut as part of the recipe, they will look like you meant them. If you treat them as a pan you slice warm on the way out the door, they will look like a hurry. That work is ordinary, and it is worth doing early, while the racks are empty and the kitchen is still a kitchen, instead of discovering a soft slab when the coffee is already poured and the only thing left to do is hope a knife will tidy what time on the counter was meant to do.
