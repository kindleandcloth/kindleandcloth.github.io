A Brunch Wedding Reception You Can Host at Home

The short version: A brunch wedding reception works when you treat it as a real meal at an earlier hour, not as a pastry table with champagne. Serve at least one savory protein, something hot, fruit or a salad, and coffee that doesn't run out. Give the morning a clear end, so guests are not still grazing at 3 p.m. without a plan. If you also want a next-day "recovery" brunch, that is a second, smaller event — not a substitute for feeding people after the ceremony.

Best for most lists

A late-morning ceremony and a lunch-like brunch: a baked egg dish or a staffed omelet is optional, a real protein, potatoes or bread, fruit, and coffee. This is the version that replaces a dinner, so it has to be substantial. Our budget food ideas list brunch as a format; this guide is the menu and the clock.

Best if you want it easy

A restaurant or grocery drop-off of breakfast trays, plus your own chafers, a coffee plan, and a helper. You can bake one thing at home — a bread, a fruit crisp — without becoming the line cook.

Best as a second gathering

A next-morning backyard brunch for out-of-town guests, after a Saturday dinner wedding. Keep it short, keep it small, and don't invent a second reception you have to dress for. One good pot of coffee and a simple menu will do more than a repeat of the wedding food.

A brunch wedding can be a kindness. Guests travel better in the morning. The yard is cooler. You can be done while it is still afternoon, which is easier on older relatives, on people who have children, and on the two of you. The food, if you plan it as a meal, can cost less than a Saturday dinner with a long bar.

It can also feel thin. A tower of pastries, a bowl of strawberries, and a bottle of bubbly is a nice photo and a poor lunch. People will leave hungry, or they will raid a kitchen you thought you were done with. If brunch is the reception, it has to stand in for dinner: savory food, enough of it, a hot option, and a clock that tells everyone when the meal is happening.

Start with the clock, not the pastry

The most common working shape is a late-morning ceremony — 10:30 or 11 — and food ready when guests reach the yard or the house, with a clear last call for the hot dishes a couple of hours later. An early afternoon ceremony with a "brunch" that starts at 2 p.m. is really a lunch, and you should name it that way if the light and the appetite have already moved on. A sunrise ceremony is beautiful and hard on guests who are not in the wedding party; if you do it, feed people promptly, and have coffee ready before anyone is asked to stand on a lawn.

Tell guests the meal time on the invitation or the website, and tell them it is brunch. People eat breakfast at home before a 6 p.m. wedding. They may not eat before an 11 a.m. one, or they may eat a little and still need a real plate. Either way, they need to know. If the party will end by mid-afternoon, say that too, so no one plans a dinner you are not serving, and so out-of-town guests can plan a quiet evening or a reservation of their own.

A short program is a friend of this format: a ceremony, a toast, food, a cake or a sweet, some music, and a goodbye. You can still have dancing if you want it; you will have less darkness and less of a late-night arc, which is fine. Our day-of timeline is written with a more typical afternoon in mind, but the habit is the same: write the hour-by-hour, and include when the coffee goes out and when the hot pans come off the fuel.

A menu that isn't only sweet

Build the plate around something savory and something hot. A baked egg strata, a frittata, or a tray of scrambled eggs held correctly can be the hot center for a mid-size list; for a larger one, a drop-off kitchen or a staffed cook is kinder than a home skillet. Add a protein that isn't eggs: oven bacon or sausage (with a vegetarian sausage or a bean cake that is actually seasoned), smoked salmon if you can keep it cold, a roasted chicken you can serve at room temperature, or a simple ham. Fruit, a green salad, and potatoes or a good bread keep it a meal.

Pastries are a supporting act. A few kinds, in enough volume, are better than a bakery display that looks busy and leaves you with seven half-full boxes. If a friend wants to bake, give them one assignment, a platter they can leave, and a drop-off time. Yogurt, granola, and a smoothie pitcher belong at a next-day brunch more than at a wedding reception, unless the list is very small and very casual.

Dietary needs show up clearly at brunch: gluten in the pastry, dairy in the strata, pork in the breakfast meat, eggs as the only hot dish. Offer a labeled gluten-free bread or a savory option that isn't a muffin, and a vegetarian protein that isn't only fruit. A small card is enough. Don't make a guest interview the buffet.

Coffee, tea, and the drinks that belong in the morning

Coffee is not a side note. Run out of it, and the whole reception feels unfinished. Make more than you think, keep it hot, and offer decaf and hot water for tea without making them a scavenger hunt. A rented coffee urn, a restaurant box, or a careful sequence of brewed pots can all work; a single consumer machine on a patio will not keep up with sixty people. Put cups, milk, a non-dairy option, sugar, and spoons at the same station, with a trash or a tray for used stirrers, and refill before the carafe is empty.

A bubbly punch or a light cocktail can be part of a brunch, and it should not be the only beverage. Water and a juice or an iced tea in glass dispensers will be what many guests actually drink, especially if they are driving or they have children with them. Our bar and drinks plan still applies: ice, shade, and a pour that isn't the two of you. If you serve alcohol, give it a clear last pour so a mid-afternoon ending stays an ending.

The coffee station and the food line should not be the same table. People linger over milk and will block a pan of eggs. Give coffee its own small table, away from the sun, and keep a backup brew in the kitchen.

Heat, holding, and a table that can work in daylight

Brunch food has a lot of eggs, dairy, and fruit, which means temperature and time matter in the usual way. Hold hot dishes in chafing dishes with lids and enough fuel, or in a slow cooker on warm for something like oatmeal, potatoes, or a pulled filling. Cold dishes go over ice or come out in swapped platters from a refrigerator. Don't park a smoked-salmon plate in the sun because the light was pretty.

A daytime table can be simpler than a candlelit dinner, and it still wants a little intention: a linen or a cheesecloth runner, real plates or sturdy compostable ones, and enough forks. For a rental linen, browse RENT MY WEDDING. Shade helps the food and the guests. If the ceremony is outside and brunch is on a porch or under a tent, walk the path in the actual morning light so you are not surprised by glare, a hot slab of patio, or a coffee table that sits in a wind corridor.

Someone has to run the food. Name them. Their job is refills, a fresh pot of coffee, and the moment when the egg pan should come off the heat. The two of you will be in photographs and in conversations. You will not also be scrambling eggs.

How much to make, and what it tends to cost

If brunch is the meal, portion it like a lunch: a real serving of the savory center, sides that get eaten, and pastry as an extra, not as the plate. People often eat a little less than they would at a 7 p.m. dinner, and they will still eat more than "a croissant and a mimosa" if they have been standing at a ceremony. When in doubt, add savory, not more cake. Leftover muffins are easier than a table that ran out of the only protein.

Cost, again, moves with labor. A grocery or restaurant breakfast drop-off plus coffee and a helper is often the calm, less expensive path. A staffed brunch cook costs more and buys you eggs that stay decent, and a line that isn't a family project. Baking everything yourself is a morning job you will feel in your wedding clothes. Get a local quote that includes trays, delivery, coffee if they provide it, tax, and time, then add plates, fuel, and the drink table. Treat any published range as a sense-check, not a bid.

The next-day brunch, if you want one

A "recovery" or farewell brunch is a different event. It is usually a smaller list — the people who stayed over, the wedding party, the relatives who traveled — and it should be shorter and simpler than the wedding breakfast you didn't serve. Coffee, a baked dish or a drop-off tray, fruit, and something sweet from the night before is enough. You can host it at the same house, at a bakery, or at a restaurant, which is often the kinder choice if you were up late.

Don't require formal clothes, and don't build a second toast program unless you really want one. Tell people the end time. If you are tired, a reservation under someone else's name is a legitimate plan. The point is a relaxed meal and a goodbye, not a sequel.

The kitchen, the morning, and a few things not to serve

A brunch reception shares the house with getting ready. Hair, clothes, family, and a photographer will want the same rooms and the same quiet that a breakfast cook wants. If you can, finish the food work the day before, or give the kitchen to a helper and take getting-ready to a bedroom with a closed door and a different bathroom. Don't try to bake a coffee cake, steam a dress, and greet your parents at the same counter. Stage platters on a back table or a porch that is not in the photographs, and keep the wedding-party snacks somewhere that is not the buffet.

Children will eat this meal happily if there is fruit, bread, and a mild protein, and they will wander. Put a few high-chairs or a corner with a simple plate if the list needs it, and don't rely on a chocolate fountain as the children's plan. Older guests often like this format more than a late dinner; give them seats in the shade, a bathroom that is easy to reach, and coffee that doesn't require a line across the lawn. Our restroom guide still applies in the morning, sometimes more so, if everyone arrives at once.

Skip made-to-order omelets unless you have a cook and a second line; they stall a reception the same way a single taco window does. Skip ice cream as a main dessert unless you have a freezer plan. Skip a menu that is only smoked salmon and pastry if half the list doesn't eat fish and the other half is hungry. A simple fruit crisp, a cake, or a tray of brownies from a bakery you trust will end the meal; the dessert-table guide has more on shade and volume if you want a sweet table rather than a single platter.

Have a weather plan. Morning rain is still rain, and a sunny 11 a.m. can be hotter than a June evening under a tree. Know where brunch moves if the yard isn't possible, and don't set the coffee on a table that will flood or bake. Our weather plan is written for a longer day, but the decision is the same: pick the backup before Saturday.

An overnight breakfast strata for a crowd

A strata is the brunch dish that belongs in a wedding kitchen: bread, eggs, cheese, and a filling, assembled the day before and baked in the morning. One 9-by-13-inch pan usually serves about ten people as part of a brunch plate (with fruit, a second protein, and coffee). For 50, you will bake five pans; for 100, ten. That is a lot of oven space, so stagger the pans or borrow a second oven, and hold the finished trays in a low oven or in chafing dishes.

Recipe

Overnight Savory Breakfast Strata

A custard-soaked bread casserole you can fill with sausage, or with mushrooms and onions for a vegetarian pan. Soak it overnight. Bake it until the center is set. Label the pans.

  • Yield: 50 (five pans)
  • Prep: 45 minutes, the day before
  • Bake: about 1 hour 10 minutes

Steps

  1. The day before, cook the sausage until it is browned, or sauté the mushrooms and onions until their liquid cooks off. Cool the filling. Butter the pans. Scatter half the bread in each pan, then half the filling and half the cheese; repeat.
  2. Whisk the eggs, milk, mustard, salt, and pepper until the mixture is even. Pour it over the bread, pressing the top down so the cubes soak. Cover and refrigerate overnight, at least 8 hours and up to 24.
  3. In the morning, take the pans out while the oven heats to 350°F. Bake, uncovered, until the center is set and a knife comes out clean, and the interior is around 160°F, usually 55 to 75 minutes depending on the pan and how cold it started. If the top browns too fast, tent it loosely with foil.
  4. Rest 10 minutes, then cut into squares. Hold warm, covered, and don't leave an egg casserole out on a sunny table through a long toast. A vegetarian pan should be baked in its own dish and labeled — not just “the corner without sausage.”

Make ahead

The whole point is the overnight soak, so do not assemble it at 8 a.m. on the wedding morning. You can cook the filling two days ahead. Baked leftover squares reheat, covered, in a low oven, but a strata is at its best the morning it is baked. If oven space is tight, bake two pans, hold them, and rotate the next pans — and give that job to a helper, not to the person in the wedding clothes.

Whether the brunch is the wedding or the next morning, the standard is the same: people should be able to get a plate without a project, drink a cup of coffee that is still hot, and leave at a civil hour. Do that, and an earlier wedding day stops feeling like a compromise and starts feeling like the right shape for a house and a yard.
