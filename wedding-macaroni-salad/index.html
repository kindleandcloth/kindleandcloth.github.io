Wedding Macaroni Salad: A Make-Ahead Cold Side That Does Not Sit in the Sun

Wedding macaroni salad is a make-ahead cold side: elbow pasta, a dressing loosened with vinegar or pickle brine and mustard, celery and onion, and a bowl that stays cold. It is not a mayonnaise salad left in the sun.

The short version. Cook about two pounds of dry elbow pasta for twenty-four as a side, or four pounds for forty-eight. Dress it while it is barely warm, chill it thoroughly, and keep the bowl cold on the table — in a cooler nearby, or in a smaller serving bowl you refill. This is not a hot pasta bar, and it is not a potluck pasta salad you hope will be safe on a picnic table. A knife for the celery and onion, and a plan for ice, will do more than a pretty bowl with no shade.

Top picks

  • Best make-ahead: the night before, after the pasta has been dressed and has had time to cool completely.
  • Best dressing: mayonnaise loosened with vinegar or pickle brine and a little mustard, so it stays creamy after a chill, not tight and pasty.
  • Best service: a cold bowl, refilled from the refrigerator, never a full tray sitting in the sun.

A cold side you make ahead, not a mayo bowl in the sun

The useful purchases for this are ordinary, and they should match the work. A beverage cooler, to keep a cold dish actually cold on a warm afternoon, a pyrex 9x13, a chef knife, a pie carrier insulated, a salad bowl large stainless, so a cold side can be tossed and served without a mixing-bowl shuffle, a trifle bowl glass large, a macaroni and cheese pan deep, and a cambro food storage container, which is the actual tool this part of the day uses, not a generic stand-in.

Macaroni salad is a wedding workhorse because it can be finished the day before, it feeds a lot of people for the cost of pasta, and it sits beside a barbecue or a buffet without asking for a chafer. It fails when the dressing goes tight in the refrigerator, when the pasta was overcooked, or when a mayonnaise salad sits out on a warm afternoon until it is no longer a good idea. The recipe is simple. The hold is the job.

Two pounds of dry elbows, once cooked and dressed, will serve about twenty-four as a side. Four pounds will serve about forty-eight. That is a generous side, not a main. If this is one of several cold salads, you can make less. If it is the only starch beside grilled meat, this scale is about right, because people will take a full scoop.

A budget meal already mentions pasta salad as a potluck risk: too many bowls, no labels, and a table that is warmer than it looks. This post is the version you make yourself, in a quantity you can chill, with a dressing you can name, and a plan to keep it cold. It is not a request that twelve aunts each bring a half-bowl.

Mayonnaise and cooked pasta belong in the refrigerator, or in a bowl nested over ice, or in a small serving dish you refill. The two-hour window that food-safety teaching allows in the danger zone gets shorter in a hot backyard. Do not test it. Keep a reserve in the fridge, and do not put the entire four pounds out at once because the board looked empty.

Label it in ordinary words: macaroni salad, with mayonnaise, celery, and onion. If you add eggs or pickle, say so. People who need to know will not always ask, and a pale yellow salad can hide a lot of ingredients. If someone cannot eat eggs or dairy, this may not be their side, and another potato or green salad should actually be there, not promised.

Pasta, a loose dressing, and crunch that holds

Cook the elbows in well-salted water until they are just tender, not soft. Drain them well. A little leftover moisture will thin the dressing; a lot of leftover water will make soup. If you cooked the pasta in the only big pot, and the rest of the menu still needs that pot, spread the elbows in the pans right away so the stove is free, and dress them as soon as they are no longer steaming hard. I like to dress the pasta while it is still barely warm, so the dressing soaks in, and then chill the whole bowl. If you dress piping-hot pasta with a full load of mayonnaise, the dressing can break and look oily. Let the steam drop for a minute, then add the dressing and toss.

The dressing should be looser than you think you want at the start. Mayonnaise tightens in the cold, and pasta drinks it overnight. A few spoons of cider vinegar or pickle brine, and a spoon of mustard, keep it from turning into a stiff paste by the next afternoon. A little sugar is traditional in some family versions, including the sweeter “Amish wedding” bowls people search for. You can add a small amount if that is the salad you grew up with. The recipe below stays on the tangy side, so the bowl still tastes like a side and not like a dessert. Taste it cold, not only warm, before you commit to more sugar or more salt.

Celery and onion are the crunch that makes the salad feel finished. Cut them small, so a scoop is not a mouthful of raw onion. A little scallion or parsley on top at service is optional and looks like you meant it. Hard-boiled eggs are a common add; they also shorten how long some people are comfortable leaving the bowl out, and they should be on the label if you use them. I leave them optional. Pickles or pickle relish belong in the same sentence as the brine: good, if you like them, and declared.

Use a wide bowl or a couple of 9x13 pans for the chill, so the salad cools faster than it would in one deep stockpot. Stir once while it cools. Cover it well, so it does not pick up onion from the rest of the refrigerator, and so a crust does not dry on top. Towels under the serving bowl will catch what a cold salad always does when people scoop in a hurry.

Do not rinse the pasta under cold water and then expect the dressing to cling the same way, unless you are in a real hurry to cool it. A rinse can leave the surface slick. If you must rinse to stop the cooking, drain very well, and be prepared to add a little more dressing after the chill.

Wedding macaroni salad for a crowd

The recipe below is for two pounds of dry pasta, about twenty-four as a side, or four pounds if you double it. Make it ahead, taste it cold, and loosen the dressing if it has tightened overnight. Keep it cold from the refrigerator to the table.

Wedding Macaroni Salad

Elbow pasta tossed with a mayonnaise dressing loosened with vinegar or pickle brine and mustard, plus celery and onion. Make it ahead, taste it cold, and keep the bowl chilled. Two pounds of dry pasta serve about 24 as a side; four pounds, about 48. Do not leave a mayonnaise salad in the sun.

About 24 or 48 as a side · Prep 25 minutes plus chilling · Cook about 12 minutes

  1. Cook the elbows in a large pot of well-salted water until just tender. Drain well, and let the steam drop for a minute so the pasta is no longer piping hot. In a large bowl, whisk the mayonnaise, vinegar or brine, mustard, salt, pepper, and sugar if you are using it. Toss the pasta with the dressing, then fold in the celery, onion, and any pickles or parsley. The dressing should look a little loose.
  2. Spread the salad in a wide bowl or in 9x13 pans so it cools faster. Cover and refrigerate until thoroughly cold, at least two hours and preferably overnight. Taste it cold, and loosen with a spoon of mayonnaise and a splash of brine if it has tightened. Keep it refrigerated until service.
  3. Put out a modest bowl, nested over ice if the day is warm, and refill from the refrigerator. Do not set the entire batch on a sunny table. Label the salad, including mayonnaise and any eggs or pickles you added. Leftovers keep, covered and cold, and should be kept cold, not left out and put back.

Keeping it cold, and labeling it

Service is a smaller bowl and a plan, not a heroic platter. If the reception is outside, put the salad in the shade, on a towel, with a spoon, and bring a fresh cold bowl when the first one is low or no longer truly cold to the touch. A deeper buffet can hold a nest of ice under a metal or glass bowl. A plastic tub in the sun will not do the same job. If you only have the kitchen refrigerator, use it, and let someone be in charge of the refill.

A lid or a sheet of wrap between scoops, if the hour is long and bugs are out, is practical and not unfriendly. Set plates and napkins so people are not reaching across the meat to get to the salad. If you have a second cold side, give each bowl a little room, so the spoons do not migrate and the labels stay readable.

What you made ahead should taste like itself on the second day: creamy, a little tangy, with a crunch still in it. If it tastes tired or warm, it is not a leftover you put back out. Macaroni salad is a generous, inexpensive wedding side when you treat the refrigerator as part of the recipe. It is a problem when you treat the backyard like a walk-in cooler it is not. If a well-meaning helper wants to “put it all out so we don’t have to come back,” thank them and hand them the smaller bowl. The refill walk is part of hosting a cold salad, the same way stirring a chafer is part of hosting a hot one.

I would rather make this the day before, taste it in the morning, and spend the event watching the ice and the spoon, than cook pasta in the last hour and serve a lukewarm bowl because the refrigerator was already full. Make space in the fridge when you plan the menu, not after the pasta is dressed. A crowded, warm refrigerator will not chill four pounds of salad in time, and it will not do the rest of the food any favors either.

A short macaroni-salad checklist

Cook the pasta just tender, dress it a little loose, and chill it thoroughly, preferably overnight. Taste it cold and adjust. Put out only what you can keep cold, and refill from the refrigerator. Label the mayonnaise, and any egg or pickle you added.

Do not leave a full bowl of mayonnaise salad in the sun because the table looked better that way. A wedding macaroni salad is a make-ahead dish with a cold hold, and the hold is what makes it kind to serve, not only easy to make. If you do that one job well — a loose dressing, a real chill, a small cold bowl, a label — the salad will taste like a side someone meant, and it will still be a side you can be glad you served at the end of the afternoon. Guests do not need a surprising pasta salad. They need a cold one that tastes like celery, a little tang, and pasta that was not left to sour in the sun, and that is an ordinary, generous thing to put on a wedding table. Make it the day before, keep it cold, and let the hot food have the chafers. The refrigerator is this salad’s only important piece of equipment, and it should be treated that way from the start, not as an afterthought once the bowl is already sitting out in the open on the table in a warm backyard, where it cannot stay as truly cold as it really needs to stay for the meal.
