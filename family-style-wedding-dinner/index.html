A Family-Style Wedding Dinner You Can Actually Pass

A family-style wedding dinner works when the platters actually fit the tables, the food can be passed without a traffic jam, and the mains can wait a few minutes without falling apart. It is a shared meal, not a buffet in disguise.

The short version. Family-style means the food is served on platters at the tables, and guests pass them. It is not a buffet with extra garnishes, and it is not a plated dinner you are trying to make look casual. You need tables with enough width for platters and glasses at the same time, two mains or one generous main plus a real vegetarian platter, sides that can travel, and a couple of people to bring the food out in a wave. The dishes have to hold. A braise, a tray of vegetables, and bread will behave better than a steak that needs to be sliced at the last second for eighty people.

Top picks

  • Best for most small and mid-size lists: two mains (one clearly vegetarian), two sides, bread, and platters brought to the tables in one organized wave.
  • Best for a tight backyard: fewer platters, slightly longer tables, and a menu that can sit, rather than a wide buffet you don't have the lawn for.
  • Best if you want less passing: a short buffet, or plated dinners, instead of forcing family-style onto tables that can't hold the dishes.

What family-style actually means

You do not need a specialty aisle of props. You need a short, honest list. A linen table runner, which is the actual tool this part of the day uses, not a generic stand-in, a sandwich platter tray, a gravy boat stainless, a salad bowl large stainless, a salad bowl large stainless, so a cold side can be tossed and served without a mixing-bowl shuffle, a salad servers wood, a meat fork carving, and a cast iron roasting pan, if the main is actually going in the oven at that size.

Family-style is a service style, not a menu. Food comes to the table on platters and in bowls, and people pass them, the way they would at a large dinner at home. Guests should be able to take what they want without standing in a line and without waiting for a server to set a composed plate in front of them. That is the whole distinction.

It is a good fit for a backyard or a small rented room because it feels like a meal together, and because it can be more flexible than a formal plated service you may not have the staff for. It is a poor fit when the tables are too narrow, the guest list is huge, or the kitchen can't get every platter out in a reasonably close window. A buffet is kinder to a very large list. A small dinner at home can go either way; at twelve or twenty, passing is natural. At ninety, you need a plan for how many platters leave the kitchen at once.

What family-style is not: a buffet line you have simply moved closer to the seats, or a plated dinner with a “shareable” salad dropped in the middle as an afterthought. If the mains never leave a chafer on a side table, you are serving a buffet. Call it that and set it up well. The name is not the point. Whether people can eat together, while the food is still hot, is the point.

Tables, platters, and what actually fits

Before you fall in love with the idea, measure the tables. A standard six-foot banquet table already has to hold place settings, glasses, water, maybe a bottle of wine, and some kind of center. Platters need a landing place, and then they need a place to go once they have been passed. If your runner and bud vases leave no rectangle for a chicken platter and a bowl of vegetables, the flowers have to shrink, or the service style has to change.

A linen runner down the center is usually enough cloth. A cheesecloth runner on top of it can soften a rented table without a full drape that fights the platters. Keep centerpieces low and movable. Bud vases and a few hurricanes will do more than a wide arrangement no one can pass a dish around. Table numbers belong at the end or in a small stand, not in the only gap that would have held the main.

Platters themselves: you need more than you think, because two tables cannot share one chicken pan without someone walking a mid-dinner errand. A working rule is one main platter for every six to eight seats, plus bowls for sides, plus bread. A wide serving board can carry bread or a vegetarian roast that you would rather slice once and pass. Rent or borrow the real pieces; a jumble of unmatched home platters can work if they are large enough, and it will fail if they are dinner plates in disguise.

Long rectangular tables pass more easily than tight rounds, because there is a path down the middle and people are not handing a heavy dish across a wide circle. If you only have rounds, use more, smaller platters, and don't put a tall centerpiece in the way.

A menu that can sit on a platter

Choose food that still looks and tastes like itself after five or ten minutes on the table. A braise, a baked pasta, a tray of roasted vegetables, a bowl of dressed greens added at the last minute, and bread are the spine of this format. The chicken cacciatore below is in that family: bone-in pieces in a pepper-and-tomato sauce, cooked until tender, held hot, and brought out on platters with some of the vegetables and juices. It is not a last-minute sear, and it is not the simple roasted-thigh dinner in the budget food guide, which is a different, still-useful roast.

Two mains is the comfortable setup for most lists: one with meat, one that is truly vegetarian, not the same pot with the meat pushed to the side. If the list is small and everyone will eat the same main, one generous platter plus sides can be enough. Sides should travel: roasted potatoes, a grain that isn't glue, a green vegetable, a salad that is dressed just before it goes out. Put dressing on the side if the salad has to wait.

What to be careful with: anything that has to be sliced for a crowd at the exact moment of service (a large rare roast is possible, and it is a staffing decision), fried food, and delicate fish that dries on a platter. If you want steak, be honest about who is slicing it and how it will stay warm. A chafing dish in the kitchen or on a sideboard can hold backup portions so the last table is not eating a cooled pan.

Bread and water are part of the menu, not extras. A basket on each table and a filled water pitcher or carafe will save you a dozen mid-dinner interruptions. Wine on the table, if you are doing that, needs a little room; don't also pile the platters onto a surface that is already full of bottles.

Chicken cacciatore for the platters

Cacciatore — hunter-style chicken — is a braise of pieces with peppers, onion, and tomato, sometimes with a little wine, cooked until the meat is tender and the vegetables have gone into the sauce. It is a classic for a reason: it can be made ahead, reheated, and brought to the table in a way that still looks like dinner. Bone-in, skin-on thighs and drumsticks hold better than breasts, which dry if they sit. This is dinner for passing, not a weeknight pan for four with a different name.

The method is straightforward. Season and brown the chicken in batches so you have color and fond. Take it out, cook the peppers and onions in the same pot, add garlic and a spoon of tomato paste, then deglaze with white wine, add crushed tomatoes, and return the chicken to cook gently until it is done. A low oven is easier than a crowded stovetop when you are cooking for a crowd. You can do this a day ahead, chill it, and reheat it until the sauce is bubbling and the chicken is hot at the bone.

Public food-safety guidance: cook chicken to 165°F in the thickest part, off the bone. Cool leftovers in shallow containers. Reheat until steaming hot all the way through, then hold at or above 140°F until the platters go out. If a platter will sit on the table for a long, leisurely dinner, it is still better to send food out hot and reasonably promptly than to let a deep pan linger in the kitchen at a vague temperature.

  1. Season the chicken all over with salt and pepper. In wide, heavy pans (you will need more than one for these quantities), heat a thin film of olive oil and brown the pieces, skin-side down first, in batches, until the skin is colored. Transfer to trays. Pour off all but a few tablespoons of fat from each pan.
  2. Add the onions and peppers with a pinch of salt. Cook until they soften and take on a little color, scraping up the browned bits. Add the garlic for a minute, then the tomato paste, and cook the paste until it darkens.
  3. Pour in the wine and let it reduce by about half. Add the crushed tomatoes, bay leaves, and herbs. Nestle the chicken into the sauce, skin side up, along with any juices from the trays. The sauce should come partway up the pieces; add a splash of water if a pan looks dry.
  4. Bring to a simmer, then cook gently on the stove or in a 350°F oven, uncovered or partly covered, until the chicken is tender and registers 165°F in the thickest part, about 35 to 50 minutes depending on size. If the sauce is thinner than you want for a platter, lift the chicken out and reduce the sauce briefly, then return the pieces.
  5. Taste and salt again. Cool in shallow containers and refrigerate overnight if you are making it ahead. Reheat until the sauce is bubbling and the chicken is hot at the bone. Hold at or above 140°F, then transfer to platters with plenty of peppers, onions, and sauce. Finish with parsley as the platters leave the kitchen.

This is the meat main. Make a separate vegetarian platter — a baked pasta, a substantial braise of peppers and beans, or a filled vegetable that can sit — in its own pans, and label both. Don't try to treat this as vegetarian by offering only the sauce and vegetables from the chicken pot. For gluten-free guests, the braise itself is usually fine; watch any side you serve with it.

Service, helpers, and a short checklist

Name a lead in the kitchen and two or three people who can carry. The goal is a wave: salads first if they are separate, then mains and sides to every table in a close window, so one table is not twenty minutes behind another. A short briefing — which platter is which, which table has the vegetarian main, where leftover platters come back to — is enough. This is closer to hosting a large dinner than it is to running a restaurant, and it still needs a cue.

If you are also toasting, decide whether platters go down before or after the first toast so people are not holding a chicken dish in midair. If children are at the tables, think about the heavy, hot dish and whether a server should set it down rather than pass it over laps.

A week out, count platters, seats, and dietary needs, and cook a small test of the main if you have never made it at this scale. The day before, cook the braise, set the tables in their real places, and see whether a platter actually fits. The day of, reheat in time, dress the salad late, and send the food in one wave. Keep a backup pan hot for late tables or second helpings.

Family-style is a generous way to eat if the table can hold it and the food can wait a little. If either of those is not true, a well-run buffet or a small plated dinner is not a lesser version. It is the same hospitality, in a shape that fits the room. Baked ziti or a vegetarian cottage pie in 9x13 pans can still be a complete dinner, passed or served from a sideboard, if the table itself cannot hold platters.
