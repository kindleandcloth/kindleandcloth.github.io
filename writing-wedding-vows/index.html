Writing Wedding Vows You Can Actually Say: Short, on Paper, with a Backup

Writing wedding vows for a backyard ceremony is a short, specific job: a few honest sentences you can actually say, on paper, with a copy in the officiant’s hand if you go blank. It is not a speech, and it is not a poem you found the night before.

The short version. Write vows you can say in a minute or two, in your own words, and print them. Decide whether they will be public, private, or both — private vows, said earlier, are a complete choice, not a lesser one. Give the officiant a copy, or keep one in a notebook you can still read if your hands shake. This is not the officiant’s script, and it is not a ceremony layout. It is the part only the two of you can write, and it should be finished early enough that you can practice it out loud once.

Top picks

  • Best length: a minute or two, specific, and finished before the week of the wedding.
  • Best backup: a printed copy, and one in the officiant’s folder, in case you go blank.
  • Best alternative: private vows before guests arrive, if you do not want to perform the sentences in public.
  • Useful kit: kraft envelopes, micron pens, moleskine notebook, vow books, and calligraphy pens for invitations — the tools this post is actually about, not a generic stand-in.

Short vows, written down

The right objects are the ones you will still use after the weekend is over, or the ones that quietly do a job on the day. A kraft envelopes, for invitations or notes that should feel like paper, not like a card-aisle theme, a micron pens, a moleskine notebook, a vow books, a calligraphy pens for invitations, which is the actual tool this part of the day uses, not a generic stand-in, a vow books, a vow books, and a picture frame, if you are giving a photograph, or asking people to sign one.

A backyard ceremony is already close. People can see your face, and they can hear you if the sound is even slightly right. That closeness is a gift, and it is a reason not to write a four-minute essay. Short vows hold up outdoors. Long vows drift, especially if a plane goes over, or a child speaks, or you have to start a sentence again.

Write in ordinary language. “I love how you…” and “I promise to…” will do more than a string of metaphors you would not say at the kitchen table. Specific is better than original. A true detail from your life together — the way someone makes coffee, or shows up, or makes a house feel like a house — will sound like you. A borrowed poem will sound like a borrowed poem, even if it is beautiful.

Finish them before the week of the wedding. The night before is how you get a draft you have never said out loud, in a handwriting you cannot read. Write, wait a day, cut anything that is there to impress a guest, and then stop. You are not trying to win a comparison with anyone else’s vows. You are trying to say a few true sentences and still be able to look at the person in front of you.

Agree, together, on a rough length, so one of you does not write a page and the other a paragraph. You do not have to match sentence for sentence. You do have to agree that this is a short piece of the ceremony, not the whole program. If you want a reading, that is a different slot, and it should belong to someone else, on purpose.

A pen and a page you can hold in one hand are enough. A phone is a poor vow book. It goes to sleep, it catches the sun, and it looks like you are checking a message. Print the words, or write them clearly in a small notebook, and number the pages if there are two. Keep the rings in a small box until they are needed, so you are not holding metal and a fluttering page in the same hand while you try to start a sentence.

Private vows, public vows, or both

You can say vows in front of the guests, or only to each other, or both. All three are real weddings. A backyard does not require a performance. If the thought of speaking sentences you care about to a row of relatives makes you feel sick, you are allowed to say them earlier, in the room where you got dressed, or in the yard before anyone arrives, and then use a short, traditional exchange in the ceremony, or a single sentence, or a simple “I do.”

Private vows are not a workaround for people who failed to write something. They are often better sentences, because they do not have to work as a public speech. You can be more specific, and you can cry without feeling like you are on a stage. If you go this way, still write them down, and still decide who is in the room — only the two of you, or an officiant, or a photographer at a distance. A getting-ready morning can hold a short private moment if you put it on the clock, not if you hope there will be a leftover window.

Public vows do not have to be original essays. A short traditional form, said clearly, is enough, and it is kind to guests who came to see you married, not to hear a monologue. If you write your own and also want the traditional words, say the personal ones first, or the traditional ones first, and tell the officiant which is which, so they do not leave a hole in the script.

If only one of you wants to write personal vows, do not force a match. One person can say written vows; the other can say a traditional line, or a shorter promise. Announce that only if it would otherwise look like someone forgot their paper. Usually it does not need an announcement. It needs two people who have already agreed.

Do not surprise each other with vows you have promised will be short and then written as speeches. If you are not sharing the text in advance, at least share the length and the tone. A joke that only one of you will enjoy, in the middle of a serious promise, is a hard thing to stand inside. Save the joke for a toast, if it is a good joke. A vow can have warmth in it without becoming a bit. If you are unsure whether a line is a promise or a performance, it is usually a performance, and it can wait until people have a glass in their hand and permission to laugh.

How to write them so you can actually say them

Say the draft out loud, standing up, once, to the wall or to a trusted person who is not your partner if you want to keep the words new. You will find the sentence that is too long, the word you trip on, and the line that sounds like someone else. Cut those. Read them a second time more slowly than you think you should. A backyard will already speed you up.

Write the way you talk, then tighten it. If a sentence has two ideas, make it two sentences. If a promise is something you cannot reasonably keep — “I will always,” “I will never” — consider a promise you can stand behind on an ordinary Tuesday. “I will keep trying,” “I will tell you when I am stuck,” “I will come home” are less grand and more durable. You know your own life; write to that life.

Leave a margin, and a large enough hand, so you can find your place if you look up. Mark a breath. If you tend to cry, put the hardest sentence in the middle, not the first line, so you can start. There is no prize for getting through a vow without a pause. There is a cost to a first line you cannot get out at all.

If you are marrying in a language that is not the language of the whole guest list, you can say a short sentence in each, or you can say the vows in the language that is yours and let the officiant explain, briefly, that this is a promise in that language. Do not try to give a simultaneous translation yourself while you are also trying to speak to one person.

Keep a copy in a stout envelope or a folder with the license, so the officiant can hand it back to you if the wind takes a page, or if your mind goes empty. The friend who is officiating already has enough in their hands; put your vows in the same folder, marked, and tell them they may prompt you. That is not a failure of romance. It is how a close ceremony stays kind.

If you go blank, and what the officiant has

People go blank. Even in a backyard, even with twenty guests, even with words they wrote themselves. If it happens, look at the page. If the page is gone or unreadable, look at the officiant. A prompt of your first line, quietly, is enough to start the rest. This is why they have a copy. This is why you practiced once out loud. You do not need to apologize to the guests. You can smile, find the line, and continue.

If you cannot continue, the officiant can offer a short traditional vow and a chance to say “I do,” and you can try your own words again, or not. Decide that backup before the day, in one sentence, so no one is inventing a rescue in front of your parents. A early plan can include this as easily as it includes a date; it does not need a meeting. It needs an agreement.

Wind, sun, and tears all smear ink. Print in a high-contrast hand, or type, and carry a second copy. A pale script on textured paper is a stationery idea, not a vow plan. If you want the pretty card for a photograph, use it, and keep the readable copy behind it, or in the officiant’s folder.

An unplugged ceremony helps here, because you will not be speaking to a row of phones. Tell people the ceremony is for looking up, and then look at the person you are marrying, not at the back row. The vows are for that person. The guests are there to witness, not to be performed at. That distinction is easier to keep in a yard than in a ballroom, if you let the yard stay a yard.

Afterward, put the pages somewhere dry. They are easy to lose in a cleanup, and you may want them. You may also never read them again, and that is fine. They did their job in the air, once. A note in the same stack as other papers from the day is enough; they do not have to become a framed object unless you want that. If you do, a simple frame is plenty. The sentences do not get better behind glass. They just stay in the house.

A short vows checklist

Agree on length and on whether the vows are public, private, or both. Write them in your own sentences, early enough to read out loud once. Cut anything you would not say in the kitchen. Print a readable copy, and give one to the officiant.

Decide, in advance, what happens if you go blank. Do not start writing the night before, and do not surprise each other with a speech. Short vows, on paper, said to the person in front of you, are the whole work. That is enough for a backyard ceremony, and it is enough for a promise you can stand inside later, on an ordinary day, when no one is watching. The work is not to impress the row of people who love you. The work is to say something true, out loud, and then go on with the day you already planned, including dinner, and the ordinary life that starts when the chairs are folded.

If you get the words only mostly right, that is still a vow. A missed line, a pause, a laugh, a page that had to be turned twice — those are the version that actually happened, and they are the ones you will remember, not the clean draft on the kitchen table. Write carefully, then let the spoken version be human. If a guest later tells you the vows were their favorite part, that is a kindness, and it is not a reason to have written for them. You were talking to one person. The rest of the yard was lucky to hear it, and then it was time to walk back down the lawn and start the part of the day that includes other people’s voices again, and a plate, and a chair.
