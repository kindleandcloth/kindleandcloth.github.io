A hot pot of beans, not a can at the end of the table

The useful purchases are pots and a way to keep them hot. An enameled Dutch oven will go from oven to a low burner; an 8-quart slow cooker will hold a wedding amount once the beans are already thick. A chafing dish can do the same at a buffet. A ladle and a wooden spoon are the serving and stirring tools. A can opener that actually works, deep foil pans with lids if you are traveling, and an instant-read thermometer for the hold complete a serious kit. That is what this post is about, not a decorative bean pot you have never used at this scale.

Baked beans are the other workhorse on a backyard plate of barbecue or fried chicken, and they are often the side someone opens a few cans of, warms, and hopes. A wedding pot should taste like a dish: onion, molasses or brown sugar, mustard, a little sharpness, and beans that have spent enough time in the oven to go thick and glossy, not like a thin, sweet soup. Canned beans are the practical choice at this scale. You can cook dried beans if that is how you already work, and if you have the time and the pot, but this recipe will not pretend that a dried-bean soak is the only honest path when you also have a ceremony to run.

The barbecue menu already says two meats are enough, sides should be few and good, and a meatless pot of beans can be a real plate for someone who does not eat the meat. That last point is a hospitality rule, not a garnish. If you put bacon or salt pork in the main pot, make a second, labeled meatless pot, and make enough of it that it is a meal, not a spoonful at the edge of a tray. The vegetarian dinner guide is the longer version of feeding people who do not eat meat. Here it is enough to say: label the pot, and do not make the only beans in the room a bacon pot.

About four quarts of finished beans will serve about twenty-four as a side. About eight quarts will serve about forty-eight. That is a generous ladle, not a tablespoon on the rim of a plate. If beans are one of several sides, you can make a little less. If they are the main vegetarian plate, lean toward the fuller amount and say so on the card. A 4-quart pot is also a weight and a leftover plan; the leftover-food guide is how you pack what remains, hot or cooled in shallow containers, not left in a deep pot on the counter.

Onion, molasses, and an uncovered bake

Start with onion, cooked in a little oil or in the fat from bacon if you are making a meat pot, until it is soft and sweet, not raw and sharp. Garlic is optional. The sauce is molasses or brown sugar, or a mix, mustard, cider vinegar, and a tomato base — ketchup, tomato sauce, or a combination — plus salt and pepper, and a little hot sauce if you like a quiet heat. Taste the sauce before it meets the beans. It should be darker and sharper than you think you want, because the beans will soften it and the oven will concentrate it, and a shy sauce becomes a bland pot.

Drain the canned beans, but do not rinse them within an inch of their lives unless you dislike any trace of the can liquor; a little starch helps the sauce. Stir the beans and the sauce together in a pot large enough to go in the oven, or divide them between two pots if that is what you have. If you are using bacon or salt pork, you can nest pieces in the pot, or cook them first and stir in the meat, and you should still keep a meatless pot separate, labeled, with its own spoon. Do not fish bacon out later and call the pot vegetarian.

Bake uncovered at 325°F until the sauce is thick and the top looks slightly darkened in spots, usually an hour and a half to two and a half hours, depending on the pot and how wet you started. Stir once or twice, and add a splash of water if the edges dry out before the center is done. The test is a spoon: the sauce should coat, not run like juice. A slow cooker can finish and hold them, but a long cook on high with the lid on will give you a looser pot. If you start in a slow cooker, leave the lid offset near the end, or finish in the oven, so the beans can actually thicken.

You can bake a day ahead, cool the beans, refrigerate them, and reheat them gently with a splash of water, then hold them hot. Reheating should take the pot back to a serving temperature you are willing to call hot, and a hold should stay at or above 140°F. An instant-read thermometer is how you know, not a hand over the lid. A chafing dish or a slow cooker on hot will do the work during the reception if you stir occasionally and do not let the insert run dry.

Wedding baked beans for a crowd

The recipe below is for about four quarts, about twenty-four as a side, or about eight quarts if you double it. Bake until thick, and hold the pot hot. Make a labeled meatless batch if the main pot has meat in it.

Wedding Baked Beans

Canned navy or pinto beans baked with onion, molasses, mustard, vinegar, and a tomato base until the pot is thick and glossy. About 4 quarts serve about 24 as a side; about 8 quarts, about 48. Optional bacon or salt pork in one pot; a labeled meatless pot if anyone does not eat it. Hold at or above 140°F.

About 24 or 48 as a side · Prep 25 minutes · Bake 1 1/2 to 2 1/2 hours

Directions

  1. Heat the oven to 325°F. In a large Dutch oven or heavy pot, cook the onion in the oil (or in fat from the bacon, if you are making a meat pot) until soft and sweet, not browned hard, 8 to 12 minutes.
  2. Stir in the molasses, brown sugar, ketchup or tomato sauce, mustard, vinegar, salt, and pepper. Taste the sauce: it should be a little sharper and darker than you want the finished pot to be.
  3. Stir in the drained beans. If you are using bacon or salt pork in this pot, nest it in or stir the cooked meat in. For a meatless pot, use a second vessel, no meat, and a clean spoon, and label it.
  4. Bake, uncovered, until the sauce is thick and glossy and coats a spoon, 1 1/2 to 2 1/2 hours, stirring once or twice. Add a splash of water if the edges dry before the center is done.
  5. Hold at or above 140°F in the oven on low, in a slow cooker on hot, or in a chafing dish, stirring now and then. If you baked a day ahead, cool, refrigerate, then reheat gently with a splash of water until the pot is hot again, and hold as above. Leftovers should be cooled in shallow containers, not left in a deep pot on the counter.

Double every amount for about 8 quarts. You can split a large yield across two pots so it thickens fairly. A meatless pot should be cooked and served with its own spoon and a plain label. Reheat leftovers to 165°F if you are serving them hot again.

A hot hold, a label, and a spoon that stays in the pot

Put a ladle in the beans and keep it there. A pot at a buffet without a spoon is a pot people will hunt through a table to serve, and a meatless pot needs its own ladle so it stays meatless. Label both in ordinary words: baked beans with molasses and mustard; baked beans with bacon. If you added a hot sauce, you can say “a little heat.” People who need to know will not always ask.

Do not serve beans from a pot that has cooled into the middle range and sat there. Do not thin a pot with a lot of water at the last minute and call it done — give it a few more uncovered minutes, or start the sauce a little looser next time. Do not hide a burnt edge by stirring the dark bits through if they actually taste burnt; leave them, and serve from the center. And do not park the only vegetarian main next to the raw-meat cutting board or under a dripping meat tray.

If you are traveling with beans, use deep lidded pans and an insulated carrier, and get them back to a hot hold as soon as you arrive. A long, warm car ride is not a hold. The thermometer goes with you. If the pot has slipped below a temperature you trust, reheat it properly before it goes on the table.

A short baked-beans checklist

Cook the onion until soft, make a sauce that is a little sharp, stir in the drained beans, and bake uncovered until the pot is thick. About four quarts serve twenty-four; about eight, forty-eight. Hold at or above 140°F. Label a meatless pot if you make one, and give it its own spoon.

Baked beans are a humble wedding dish, and they are one of the ones people go back for. If you treat the bake and the hold as part of the recipe, the pot will taste like a plan, and it will still be hot at the last plate. If you treat them as a can you warm at the last minute, they will taste like a can you warmed at the last minute. That work is ordinary, and it is worth starting while the oven is free, instead of discovering a thin, lukewarm pot when the meat is already sliced and the only thing left to do is hope a lid will do the work an uncovered bake and a real hold were meant to do.
