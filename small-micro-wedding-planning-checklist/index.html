The Small & Micro Wedding Planning Checklist

A small wedding isn't just a big wedding with fewer chairs. With a short guest list, every choice gets more personal and more visible: the food, the setting, even the conversations you have with people who aren't invited. It's also more flexible. You can get married on a Tuesday, in a garden, at a favorite restaurant, or on a trail at sunrise.

There's no official cutoff for a "small" or "micro" wedding. People use the terms loosely, and what matters is that your guest list fits the experience you want. This checklist walks through planning in stages so nothing important slips through the cracks.

First: decide what kind of small wedding you're having

Before you book anything, sit down together and answer a few questions honestly. They'll shape every decision after this.

  • Who absolutely has to be there? Write those names first. Everyone else is a decision, not a default.
  • What do you want the day to feel like? A long dinner party? A hike and a picnic? A ceremony followed by drinks at your favorite spot?
  • Where will you put the money you save? Many couples with small guest lists spend more per person on food, photography, or a honeymoon.
  • Will you have a larger party later? Some couples marry with a handful of people and celebrate with everyone months afterward. Decide now, because it affects how you announce things.

Six months or more before

  • Set your budget and top three priorities
  • Draft the guest list and agree on the hard limit
  • Choose a date range and a few possible locations
  • Research marriage license requirements where you'll marry
  • Book your venue or confirm the private property you'll use
  • Book your photographer (shorter coverage packages are common for small weddings)
  • Decide on an officiant: a professional, a religious leader, or a friend who gets ordained

About the license: marriage license rules vary by state and county. Some places have a waiting period between getting the license and the ceremony, and licenses usually expire after a set number of days. Look up the exact rules on your county clerk's website and put the dates in your calendar.

About venues: small weddings open up places that can't host a crowd. Think restaurant private rooms, garden centers, wineries, cabins, historic homes, a friend's backyard, or a park pavilion. Parks often require a permit, and many have rules about decorations, amplified sound, and alcohol, so ask before you fall in love with a spot.

Four to six months before

  • Book catering, a restaurant, or plan a family-style meal with help
  • Send save the dates if anyone needs to travel
  • Set up a simple wedding website with the details
  • Start looking for your attire
  • Book any other vendors: florist, baker, musician, hair and makeup
  • Reserve a block of rooms or share lodging suggestions if guests are coming from out of town

With fewer guests, you can often skip full-service vendors. A great restaurant can handle food, drinks, and dessert in one booking. A local flower farm or grocery florist may do a simple bouquet and a few arrangements. A friend with a good playlist and a portable speaker can cover music for a dinner party feel.

Two to three months before

  • Send invitations (digital or paper) with a clear RSVP date
  • Plan the ceremony: readings, vows, music, and order of events
  • Buy or order rings
  • Schedule attire fittings
  • Plan the menu and any tastings
  • Make a rain or heat plan if anything is outdoors

For a small group, digital invitations make a lot of sense. They're quick to send and track, and you can message everyone if plans change. Rizz Invites offers digital wedding invitations with online RSVP, which keeps replies, meal choices, and reminders in one place. Print a few paper copies for grandparents or anyone who'd like a keepsake.

One month before

  • Apply for your marriage license within the valid window
  • Follow up with anyone who hasn't replied
  • Confirm details and arrival times with every vendor
  • Write a simple timeline for the day and share it
  • Make a shot list of must-have photos for your photographer
  • Plan seating, even informally
  • Buy small extras: a guest book, card box, and thank-you cards

Even at a dinner for twenty, a guest book is worth having. A linen or kraft guest book or a framed print that guests sign becomes something you'll actually look at later. An instant camera with a few packs of film on the table is another easy way to gather candid photos and notes in one book.

The week of the wedding

  • Confirm final headcount with your caterer or restaurant
  • Pack an emergency kit: safety pins, stain remover, pain reliever, bandages, phone charger, tissues
  • Prepare vendor payments and tips in labeled envelopes
  • Hand off decor and the timeline to whoever is helping set up
  • Check the weather and finalize the backup plan
  • Give your rings and license to someone reliable

The day of

  • Eat breakfast and drink water
  • Let one person be the point of contact for vendors
  • Make sure your officiant and witnesses sign the license correctly
  • Take a few minutes alone together before the celebration starts
  • Assign someone to collect gifts, cards, and the guest book

After the wedding

  • Return the signed license if your officiant isn't handling it
  • Order certified copies for name changes
  • Send thank-you notes
  • Share photos or an announcement with people who weren't there

The hard part: telling people they're not invited

This is the part of small weddings nobody likes. A few approaches make it easier:

  • Be consistent. A clear rule ("immediate family and a few close friends") is easier to explain than case-by-case decisions.
  • Tell people early and directly. A short call or message before invitations go out avoids hurt feelings later.
  • Share the day another way. A livestream for distant relatives, an announcement card with a photo, or a casual party later can include more people without changing your ceremony.
  • Hold the line kindly. If one person gets an exception, others will ask. "We're keeping it really small, but we'd love to celebrate with you after" is enough.

Where small weddings can splurge

When you're feeding a smaller group, the per-person budget stretches further. Popular places to put it:

  • The meal. A long, relaxed dinner with great food is often the part guests remember.
  • Photography. Good photos last longer than any centerpiece.
  • The setting. A place that means something to you, or one that's simply beautiful, does most of the decorating.
  • Personal touches. Handwritten notes at each seat, a meaningful reading, or favors that reflect your story.

Small weddings work best when you plan them on purpose, not as a scaled-down version of something else. Decide what matters, protect it in the budget, and let the rest stay simple.
