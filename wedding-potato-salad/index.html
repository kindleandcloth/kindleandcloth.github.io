Wedding potato salad is a make-ahead cold side: boiled potatoes, a mayonnaise dressing with mustard, celery and onion, and a bowl that stays cold. About six pounds of potatoes serve twenty-four as a side; about twelve pounds, forty-eight. It is not a pasta salad, and it is not a baked-potato bar.

The short version. Boil potatoes until they are just tender, dress them while they are still slightly warm with mayonnaise, mustard, and a little vinegar or pickle brine, and fold in celery and onion. Chill the bowl thoroughly, taste it cold, and keep it cold at service. This is not macaroni salad, and it is not a baked potato bar. It is a potato salad you can finish the day before, with a refrigerator as part of the recipe.

Top picks

  • Best make-ahead: the night before, after the potatoes have been dressed and have had time to cool completely.
  • Best dressing: mayonnaise loosened with mustard and a little vinegar or pickle brine, so it stays creamy after a chill, not tight and pasty.
  • Best service: a cold bowl, refilled from the refrigerator, never a full tray sitting in the sun.
  • Useful kit: a 12-quart stockpot, a peeler, a colander, a lidded mixing bowl, and salad tongs — the tools this post is actually about, not a generic stand-in.

A cold potato side, not another pasta salad

The useful purchases are ordinary, and they should match the work. A 12-quart stockpot will boil a wedding amount of potatoes, a peeler and a colander get them ready, a masher is optional if you like a slightly broken texture, a lidded bowl and an insulated carrier keep the salad cold, tongs or a wide spoon will serve it, and an egg slicer is worth having only if you are actually adding eggs and want them even.

Potato salad is a wedding workhorse for the same reason macaroni salad is, and it is a different dish. It can be finished the day before, it feeds a lot of people for the cost of potatoes, and it sits beside barbecue or fried chicken without asking for a chafer. It fails when the potatoes are waterlogged or undercooked, when the dressing goes tight in the refrigerator, or when a mayonnaise salad sits out on a warm afternoon until it is no longer a good idea. The recipe is simple. The hold is the job.

It is not macaroni salad. If you already have a pasta salad on the table, you may not also need this, unless the meal is a spread of cold sides and you want both. It is also not a baked potato bar, which is a hot station with toppings. This is a dressed, chilled salad, in a bowl you refill. About six pounds of potatoes, once cooked and dressed, will serve about twenty-four as a side. About twelve pounds will serve about forty-eight. That is a generous side, not a main. If this is one of several cold salads, you can make less. If it is the only starch beside grilled meat, this scale is about right, because people will take a full scoop.

Mayonnaise and cooked potatoes belong in the refrigerator, or in a bowl nested over ice, or in a small serving dish you refill. The two-hour window that food-safety teaching allows in the danger zone gets shorter in a hot backyard. Do not test it. Keep a reserve in the fridge, and do not put the entire twelve pounds out at once because the board looked empty. Label it in ordinary words: potato salad, with mayonnaise, celery, and onion. If you add eggs or pickle, say so. People who need to know will not always ask.

Potatoes, a loose dressing, and crunch that holds

Use a waxy or all-purpose potato that will hold a cube — Yukon Golds, or red potatoes, or a similar kind you already like in a salad. Starchy bakers will fall apart into something closer to a mash, which can be good if that is the salad you grew up with, and muddy if it is not. I like a potato that still looks like a potato in the spoon. Peel them if you want a softer salad; leave the skins on if they are thin and clean, and say so on the label if the skins are staying. Cut them into even, bite-size pieces so they cook at the same rate.

Start the potatoes in cold, well-salted water, in a pot large enough that they can move, and boil until they are just tender when you test one with a knife. Drain them well in a colander. A little leftover moisture will thin the dressing; a lot of leftover water will make soup. I like to dress the potatoes while they are still slightly warm, so the dressing soaks in, and then chill the whole bowl. If you wait until they are cold and closed, the dressing sits on the surface and tastes like a coat. If you dress them when they are steaming hard, the mayonnaise can look oily. Let the steam drop for a minute, then add the dressing and fold, not mash, unless a slightly broken texture is what you want. A masher is a tool, not a requirement.

The dressing should be looser than you think you want at the start. Mayonnaise tightens in the cold, and potatoes drink it overnight. Mustard — yellow or Dijon, or a mix — and a few spoons of cider vinegar or pickle brine keep it from turning into a stiff paste by the next afternoon. A little sugar is traditional in some family versions. You can add a small amount if that is the salad you grew up with. The recipe below stays on the tangy side, so the bowl still tastes like a side and not like a sweet picnic surprise. Taste it cold, not only warm, before you commit to more sugar or more salt.

Celery and onion are the crunch that makes the salad feel finished. Cut them small, so a scoop is not a mouthful of raw onion. Hard-boiled eggs are a common add; they also shorten how long some people are comfortable leaving the bowl out, and they should be on the label if you use them. I leave them optional. Pickles or pickle relish belong in the same sentence as the brine: good, if you like them, and declared. A little scallion or parsley on top at service is optional and looks like you meant it.

Use a wide, lidded bowl, or two, for the chill, so the salad cools faster than it would in one deep stockpot. Stir once while it cools. Cover it well, so it does not pick up onion from the rest of the refrigerator, and so a crust does not dry on top. Towels under the serving bowl will catch what a cold salad always does when people scoop in a hurry.

Wedding potato salad for a crowd

The recipe below is for about six pounds of potatoes, about twenty-four as a side, or about twelve pounds if you double it. Make it ahead, taste it cold, and loosen the dressing if it has tightened overnight. Keep it cold from the refrigerator to the table.

Wedding Potato Salad

Boiled potatoes folded with a mayonnaise dressing loosened with mustard and vinegar or pickle brine, plus celery and onion. Dress the potatoes slightly warm, chill thoroughly, and keep the bowl cold. About 6 pounds of potatoes serve about 24 as a side; about 12 pounds, about 48. Do not leave a mayonnaise salad in the sun.

  1. Put the potatoes in a large pot, cover with cold salted water, and bring to a boil. Cook until just tender when pierced, 15 to 20 minutes depending on the size of the pieces. Drain well, and let the steam drop for a minute so the potatoes are no longer piping hot. In a large bowl, whisk the mayonnaise, mustard, vinegar or brine, salt, pepper, and sugar if you are using it. Fold the potatoes into the dressing, then fold in the celery, onion, and any eggs, pickles, or parsley. The dressing should look a little loose.
  2. Spread the salad in a wide, lidded bowl so it cools faster. Cover and refrigerate until thoroughly cold, at least two hours and preferably overnight. Taste it cold, and loosen with a spoon of mayonnaise and a splash of brine if it has tightened. Keep it refrigerated until service.
  3. Put out a modest bowl, nested over ice if the day is warm, and refill from the refrigerator. Do not set the entire batch on a sunny table. Label the salad, including mayonnaise and any eggs or pickles you added. Leftovers keep, covered and cold, and should be kept cold, not left out and put back.

Keeping it cold, and labeling it

Service is a smaller bowl and a plan, not a heroic platter. If the reception is outside, put the salad in the shade, on a towel, with tongs or a spoon, and bring a fresh cold bowl when the first one is low or no longer truly cold to the touch. A deeper buffet can hold a nest of ice under a metal or glass bowl. An insulated carrier will move the reserve from a neighbor’s refrigerator if yours is already full. A plastic tub in the sun will not do the same job. If you only have the kitchen refrigerator, use it, and let someone be in charge of the refill.

A lid or a sheet of wrap between scoops, if the hour is long and bugs are out, is practical and not unfriendly. Set plates and napkins so people are not reaching across the meat to get to the salad. If you have a second cold side — including a macaroni salad — give each bowl a little room, so the spoons do not migrate and the labels stay readable. People should be able to tell, at a glance, which bowl has egg in it.

What you made ahead should taste like itself on the second day: creamy, a little tangy, with a crunch still in it, and potatoes that still have a shape. If it tastes tired or warm, it is not a leftover you put back out. Potato salad is a generous, inexpensive wedding side when you treat the refrigerator as part of the recipe. It is a problem when you treat the backyard like a walk-in cooler it is not. If a well-meaning helper wants to “put it all out so we don’t have to come back,” thank them and hand them the smaller bowl. The refill walk is part of hosting a cold salad, the same way stirring a chafer is part of hosting a hot one.

I would rather make this the day before, taste it in the morning, and spend the event watching the ice and the spoon, than boil potatoes in the last hour and serve a lukewarm bowl because the refrigerator was already full. Make space in the fridge when you plan the menu, not after the potatoes are dressed. A crowded, warm refrigerator will not chill twelve pounds of salad in time, and it will not do the rest of the food any favors either.

A short potato-salad checklist

Cook the potatoes just tender, dress them a little loose while they are still slightly warm, and chill the bowl thoroughly, preferably overnight. Taste it cold and adjust. Put out only what you can keep cold, and refill from the refrigerator. Label the mayonnaise, and any egg or pickle you added.

Do not leave a full bowl of mayonnaise salad in the sun because the table looked better that way. A wedding potato salad is a make-ahead dish with a cold hold, and the hold is what makes it kind to serve, not only easy to make. If you do that one job well — a loose dressing, a real chill, a small cold bowl, a label — the salad will taste like a side someone meant, and it will still be a side you can be glad you served at the end of the afternoon. Guests do not need a surprising potato salad. They need a cold one that tastes like potato, a little mustard, and celery that was not left to sour in the sun, and that is an ordinary, generous thing to put on a wedding table, next to the meat, where a cold side actually belongs.
