Glazed Ham for a Wedding Crowd: How Much to Buy, How to Warm It, and a Brown Sugar Glaze

A glazed ham is the friendliest main for a winter or holiday wedding buffet: it comes fully cooked, it only has to be warmed, and it's delicious hot or just warm. Plan about half a pound of bone-in spiral ham per guest when it's the main, about a third of a pound when it shares the table, and an oven plan before you buy a single ham.

The short version. Buy fully cooked, bone-in spiral-sliced hams, about 1/2 pound per guest as the main. Warm them covered, low and slow, cut side down with a splash of water, until a thermometer reads about 130°F; then brush on a brown sugar–Dijon glaze and finish uncovered until it bubbles and the ham reaches 140°F. Hold it at 140°F or above, carve along the bone, and get leftovers into the refrigerator within two hours. The real constraint is oven space, so count your ovens first.

Why ham works for a wedding at home

Picture a buffet on a cold December evening: a big, glossy ham on a board at the head of the line, the glaze caramelized at the edges, a basket of biscuits beside it, and the smell of brown sugar and mustard drifting out every time the kitchen door opens. It looks generous. It feels like a holiday. And unlike a roast or a turkey, nobody had to cook it from raw that morning.

That's the real case for ham. A fully cooked spiral ham only needs to be warmed and glazed, so the risk is low and the timing is forgiving. It's good hot, good warm, and fine at room temperature for the length of a buffet. It suits almost every crowd, and it pairs with sides you can make ahead.

Here's the honest turn. Ham for fifty is not one ham. It's three or four, and every one of them wants an oven for a couple of hours. The plan that decides whether this goes smoothly isn't the glaze. It's how many hams fit in how many ovens, and who's watching the thermometer.

How much ham to buy

A bone-in ham loses weight to the bone, so you buy more than you serve. Most portion guides land in the same place: about 1/2 pound of bone-in spiral ham per guest when ham is the main, about 1/3 pound when it shares the buffet with another protein like chicken or a vegetarian main, and about 1/3 pound of boneless ham per guest as the main. Add a little if you want leftovers for ham biscuits the next morning, which you probably do.

Most grocery spiral hams are half hams in the 7 to 10 pound range, which is exactly the size you want. Bigger hams take longer and dry out at the edges before the middle is warm. Several medium hams cook more evenly and give you more glazed outside, which is everyone's favorite part.

Order ahead in December. Stores sell a lot of ham that month, and you want five matching hams, not four of one brand and a stray. Check the label for "fully cooked." A ham labeled "cook before eating" is a different job with a different temperature, and it isn't what this recipe is for.

The oven plan, before anything else

Count your ovens and measure them. A standard home oven usually fits two half hams in one large roasting pan, or two pans side by side if your oven is wide. That's about 25 guests per oven. For 50, you need two ovens' worth of space at the same time, or a second round of warming, which means the first hams sit and wait.

Three honest ways to get there:

Borrow a kitchen. A neighbor's or a relative's oven, two doors down, warming two hams on the same schedule. Wrap them well and carry them over in an insulated cooler lined with towels; they'll stay hot for an hour.

Add a roaster oven. An electric roaster oven plugs into a counter outlet and holds a ham like a small oven. It's also the best way to keep a ham hot on the buffet. Check your circuits first; a roaster and a coffee urn on the same outlet is how a kitchen goes dark. Our backyard wedding power plan walks through that.

Stagger it. Warm the first hams, wrap them tightly in foil and towels, and hold them in an insulated cooler while the second round warms. It works, but someone has to own the timeline.

Glazed spiral ham for a crowd

This is the warm-low, glaze-at-the-end method: the ham heats gently under foil so it doesn't dry out, then the glaze goes on at the end at higher heat, so it caramelizes rather than burns. The recipe is written for two hams, about 24 guests as the main; the second column doubles it for four hams and about 48.

Brown Sugar-Dijon Glazed Spiral Ham for a Crowd

Fully cooked bone-in spiral hams, warmed under foil, brushed with a brown sugar, honey, and Dijon glaze, and finished in a hot oven until glossy. About 24 servings from two hams; about 48 from four.

About 24 or 48 servings · Prep 15 minutes · Warm about 1 1/2 to 2 hours · Glaze 10 to 15 minutes

Directions

  1. Heat the oven to 325°F. Unwrap the hams and discard any glaze packets. Set each ham cut side down in a roasting pan, and pour about 1/2 cup water or cider into the pan for each ham.
  2. Cover each pan tightly with heavy-duty foil, crimping the edges so steam stays in.
  3. Warm for about 10 to 12 minutes per pound, usually 1 1/4 to 1 3/4 hours for a 7 to 8 pound ham, until a thermometer in the thickest part, not touching the bone, reads about 130°F.
  4. Meanwhile, make the glaze: in a small saucepan, combine the brown sugar, Dijon, honey, orange juice, butter, cloves, and pepper. Simmer over medium-low, stirring, for 3 to 5 minutes, until smooth and slightly thickened.
  5. Remove the foil and turn the oven to 400°F. Brush the glaze generously over each ham, letting it run between the slices.
  6. Return the hams to the oven, uncovered, for 10 to 15 minutes, brushing once more halfway, until the glaze is bubbling and lightly caramelized and the ham reads 140°F. Watch closely; sugar goes from glossy to burnt quickly.
  7. Rest 10 minutes. To serve, cut along the bone to release the spiral slices, and spoon some pan juices over them.

Hold warmed ham at 140°F or above, in a low oven, a roaster oven, or a chafing dish with a little pan juice so it doesn't dry out. Refrigerate leftovers within two hours, and within one hour if the buffet is outdoors above 90°F. If you're warming in batches, a ham wrapped in foil and towels in a clean insulated cooler stays hot for about an hour; check it with a thermometer before serving. The glaze can be made a day ahead and rewarmed.

A wide 2-quart saucepan handles a double batch of glaze without boiling over, and a silicone basting brush gets the glaze into the spiral cuts and washes clean, which a bristle brush never quite does after sticky brown sugar.

A make-ahead timeline

Two weeks out: order the hams and confirm the pickup date. Count ovens, borrow one, or buy a roaster. Line up the person in charge of the ham.

Two or three days before: pick up the hams and keep them in the refrigerator. Buy a roll of 18-inch heavy-duty foil; regular foil is too narrow to cover a roasting pan in one sheet and tears when you crimp it.

The day before: make the glaze and refrigerate it. Lay out the pans, foil, thermometer, knife, and serving boards.

The day of: work backward from dinner. If dinner is at 6, hams go in around 3:30, glaze goes on around 5:15, and slicing starts around 5:45. Put the wireless thermometer probe in one ham per oven and let the alarm tell you when it's time to glaze.

Holding it hot on a buffet

Don't put a whole ham on the buffet and expect guests to carve it. Slice it in the kitchen, put the slices in a warm vessel with a spoonful of pan juice, and refill from the kitchen in smaller amounts. A roaster oven set to a low hold works well on the table itself; chafing dishes work too. Everything you put out should be at 140°F or above, and whatever comes back gets refrigerated rather than returned to the line.

Ham goes first in the line, after the plates and before the sides, so it doesn't sit while people debate the potato salad. Our wedding buffet setup post covers the order, line width, and labels.

Carving and serving

A spiral ham is already sliced, so carving it is mostly releasing it. Stand the ham on its flat cut side, hold it steady with a carving fork, and run a long carving knife along the bone, around and down, so the slices fall away. Then cut the big slices in half. Smaller pieces move faster through a line and nobody takes the whole end.

Ham loves biscuits, mustard, and something sharp. A basket of warm biscuits and a little crock of grainy mustard makes a small sandwich possible, and our wedding biscuit bar scales that up. For sides, think make-ahead: a baked macaroni and cheese, roasted vegetables, and a green salad. The winter wedding food ideas guide has more that holds well.

Leftovers, the good part

Leftover ham is one of the real pleasures of serving it. Refrigerate it within two hours of coming off the heat, in shallow containers, and save the bone for soup. Ham biscuits at the farewell brunch, ham and cheese sliders for the late-night snack, and a pot of split pea soup the week after all come from the same bone. Our leftover wedding food guide covers containers, labeling, and who takes what home.

What to skip

Skip one giant ham instead of several medium ones. Skip uncovered warming, which dries out the slices. Skip glazing at the start, which burns. Skip guessing when it's warm; use a thermometer. Skip a whole ham on the buffet for guests to wrestle with. And skip leaving the buffet out for the whole party; two hours is the limit for anything perishable sitting at room temperature.

A short ham checklist

Buy fully cooked bone-in spiral hams, about 1/2 pound per guest. Count your ovens before you buy. Warm them covered at 325°F with a splash of water to about 130°F, glaze, and finish hot to 140°F. Slice in the kitchen, hold at 140°F or above, and refill in small amounts. Refrigerate leftovers within two hours. Then make biscuits in the morning.
