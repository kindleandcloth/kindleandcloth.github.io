How to Self-Cater (or Semi-Cater) a Backyard Wedding

The short version: Self-catering a backyard wedding is a labor plan before it is a menu. It works for a list you can actually cook for, with food that can be made ahead, rented or borrowed gear, and helpers who are not also in the wedding party photographs. Semi-catering — a restaurant or grocery drop-off you finish and serve at the house — is the middle path most families should look at first. The savings are real when the work is honest. They disappear when the two of you become the kitchen staff.

Best for most lists

Semi-cater: order trays from a restaurant, a specialty kitchen, or a grocery catering desk you already trust, then hold, plate, and serve at the house. You still need heat, a helper, and a table plan. Our budget food ideas introduce drop-off; this guide is the labor, the gear, and a realistic guest cap.

Best if you really want to cook

A make-ahead menu for a small list — a stew, a roast, a baked pasta, a serious salad — finished the day before, held correctly, and served by people who are not getting married that afternoon. Choose food that waits. Don't choose a tasting menu.

Best to skip

Cooking a multi-course dinner for 80 the morning of the wedding, with the two of you as head chefs. It can be done by professionals, or by a family that has done this scale before and is not also standing in a receiving line. For everyone else, it is how a wedding day turns into a shift.

"We're just going to cook" is a sentence that sounds like thrift and feels like a second job. Sometimes it is the right job: a small dinner, a kitchen you know, a menu you have already made for a crowd, and a sister who likes running a service window. Sometimes it is a way to spend more than a drop-off would have cost, once you count the late-night grocery runs, the missing chafers, and the hour you were supposed to be getting dressed.

This guide is for the backyard version of self-catering and semi-catering — not for a venue that requires their own kitchen, and not for a potluck, which has different hospitality rules and will get its own post. The aim is a meal that tastes like you, arrives on time, and leaves the two of you free to be at your wedding.

Cap the list, or cap the ambition

The right objects are the ones you will still use after the weekend is over, or the ones that quietly do a job on the day. A chafing dishes, to hold food at a safe serving temperature once it leaves the kitchen, a slow cooker, a beverage dispensers, a pie carrier insulated, a oven thermometer, so you are not guessing at 165°F or at a hold above 140°F, a instant read thermometer, a hotel pan pans, and a cambro food storage container, which is the actual tool this part of the day uses, not a generic stand-in.

There is a number of guests beyond which a home kitchen, however willing, is the wrong tool. That number depends on the menu, the helpers, the refrigeration, and whether you have a second oven at a neighbor's house. For many households, a sit-down meal you cook yourselves is calmest under about thirty, maybe forty if the food is a single make-ahead dish and the service is buffet. Past that, semi-catering or a staffed cook is usually the kinder plan, even if you still bake the dessert or make the salad.

If the list is already fixed and large, don't try to make the kitchen grow to match it. Change the service style: fewer dishes, drop-off mains, a taco bar or a barbecue someone else holds, a brunch that can be tray-baked. Pride in a homemade meal is a real feeling, and it is not more important than a dinner that arrives while guests are still happy to eat it.

Write the headcount down, including vendors who will eat, and don't cook for a hopeful RSVP. Cook or order for the number you have, plus a little. Running out is worse than leftover pasta.

Semi-catering is still work, and it is usually the right work

Drop-off food is not a day off. It is a shorter day. You (or, better, a helper) still have to confirm pans and counts, make space in the refrigerator, receive the delivery, move food into chafing dishes or a warm hold, set the buffet, keep things hot and cold, and clean up. You do not have to butcher a hog or make a reduction at 10 a.m. That swap is the whole point.

Choose a kitchen you already like, and order a menu they make all the time, not a custom tasting. Ask how the food will arrive — hot, cold, sliced, in pans that fit a standard chafer — and how long it can be held. Ask about leftovers and about labels for allergens. Get the quote in writing, including delivery, tax, and whether serving spoons and racks are part of the order or your problem. Then write your own list: fuel, plates, a table, a helper, ice, trash.

A slow cooker on warm, or two, can hold a stew, a pulled meat, or a side that came in a deep foil pan. Don't overfill them, and don't treat a row of appliances as a casual plug-in on a kitchen island already running a coffee urn. Our power plan is relevant even when the "caterer" is a grocery bag and two cords. Put hot holds on a sturdy table, and keep cords out of the walking line.

If you are cooking, choose a menu that can wait

The foods that belong in a self-catered wedding are the foods that are done early: a braise, a baked pasta, a roasted tray of vegetables, a poached salmon you can serve cold, a chili, a pulled pork you finish the day before, a composed salad that is dressed in batches. The foods that don't belong are the ones that need a last-minute skillet, a perfect medium-rare, or a fryer. A wedding day will not give you a calm hour at the stove.

One main, two sides, a bread, and a dessert is a dinner. Three mains and six sides is a restaurant, and you are not opening a restaurant. Repeat a dish rather than adding a new one "for variety." People would rather have enough of something good than a little of six things that all needed their own pan. For inspiration that is already worked out as a station or a style, the taco bar, barbecue menu, and brunch reception guides are all compatible with a self- or semi-catered house — pick one lane and stay in it.

Taste the menu at this scale, at least once, before the wedding week. A recipe that works for eight may break at thirty: too little oven, too much salt, a sauce that splits when held. Write a prep schedule backward from service, and put shopping on a day that is not Friday. Freeze or refrigerate what can be made midweek. The morning of should be reheating, assembling, and holding, not chopping onions in a veil.

Gear, space, and a kitchen that can still close the door

List the gear as carefully as you list the food: hotel pans, chafers and fuel, a thermometer, sheet pans, cooling racks, a cutting board you can sanitize, extra spoons, foil, labels, a cooler that is actually cold, a trash plan, and a place to put dirty dishes that is not the dining table. Rent what you will not use again; borrow from people who have hosted; buy only the pieces you will keep. For linens or a styled piece you don't want to own, browse RENT MY WEDDING. A party-rental house will rent chafers, and that is often cheaper than owning a set you will store for years, unless you already want the set.

Count plates, forks, glasses, and napkins from the same headcount, plus extras. Dishwashing is a hidden second event. If you don't have a dishwasher and a helper who will run it, rent or use compostable ware you are willing to stand behind, and have a bin system that doesn't overflow at 8 p.m. Our notes on service ware in the food-ideas post still apply; this is the same problem with more pans in the sink.

Protect one room, if you can, from becoming prep overflow: a bedroom for getting ready, a living room for guests, a bathroom that is not also the caterer's hand wash. A backyard wedding already asks a house to be a venue. It should not also have to be a restaurant with no back of house. A folding table on a porch, a labeled cooler, and a "kitchen closed" moment after service will help the house feel like a house again.

Helpers, money, and a timeline you can actually hand off

Name the people. A self-catered wedding without a captain is a wedding where the couple ends up plating salad in their reception clothes. The captain can be a friend, a parent, a day-of coordinator, or a hired kitchen helper. They need a written timeline, a copy of the menu, the vendor phone numbers, and permission to send the two of you away from the stove. Pay them, or thank them in a way that is not "you can also stay late to wash pans."

Count the real money. Groceries, delivery, ice, fuel, rentals, compostable ware, the helper, and the last-minute store run are all part of the number. Compare that total to a caterer's quote for the same headcount, not to the cost of a Tuesday night pasta. Sometimes you will still save a lot. Sometimes you will save a little and spend a weekend. Both can be acceptable, if you know which one you are choosing. Get a local catering quote anyway, so the comparison is real. Treat published per-person ranges as a sense-check, not as your bid.

A simple timeline: midweek shopping and any freezer work. The day before, cook what holds, set tables, stage gear, label pans, and clear the refrigerator. The morning of, receive deliveries, start holds, finish only the things that must be last-minute (a salad dressing, a bread in the oven, a garnish). Twenty minutes before service, uncover pans, put out utensils and plates, and walk the line. After the rush, get leftovers into a cold hold or into the bin if they have been out too long, and close the kitchen so the party can be a party.

Food safety is not optional because the cook is a cousin. Hot food stays hot, cold food stays cold, and perishable dishes don't sit on a sunny picnic table through the toasts. Use a thermometer if you are reheating a big pan. Don't leave a cream sauce or a chicken salad out as decoration. If you wouldn't serve it at a family picnic after three hours in the sun, don't serve it at a wedding either.

What to tell guests, and the house around the meal

Tell people what dinner is. "We're cooking a buffet at the house" or "dinner is drop-off from our favorite restaurant, served in the yard" is enough, and it sets expectations for time, dress, and dietary questions. Collect allergies the way any host would, and put labels on the table even if you think everyone already knows. A cousin will bring a plus-one you haven't cooked for. A small card is cheaper than a guess.

Think about the house as a small restaurant's least glamorous problems: parking, trash, a place for coats, a bathroom plan, and a neighbor who may not want a delivery van in the shared driveway at 3 p.m. Tell neighbors when food will arrive. Put a clearly marked bin for recycling and one for waste near the buffet, and empty them before they overflow. Our restroom and rentals guides cover the pieces that aren't food and still decide whether the evening feels calm.

Drinks should not be improvised at the same moment as the lasagna. Set a separate drink table, with water, ice, and whatever else you are actually serving, so the kitchen isn't also the bar. Glass dispensers and a simple pour are enough for a house meal; the bar plan will keep you from running out of ice. Dessert can be one good thing from a bakery, or a cake you bake on Thursday, not a third project for Saturday morning. The dessert-table guide has the shade and volume notes if you want a small sweet spread.

A baked ziti you can assemble ahead

If you are actually cooking the main, choose something that can be assembled the day before, baked, and held. Baked ziti is the Italian-American version of that idea: pasta, a tomato sauce, ricotta, and mozzarella, in pans you can lift. One 9-by-13-inch pan usually serves eight to ten. For 50, plan five or six pans; for 100, ten to twelve, and a lot of oven time — or bake in batches and hold in chafing dishes.

Make-Ahead Baked Ziti for a Backyard Wedding

A sturdy baked pasta with a ricotta layer and enough sauce that it doesn't dry out in a chafer. Assemble it the day before. Don't invent a last-minute skillet pasta on Saturday morning.

Steps

  1. Warm the sauce with the garlic and a pinch of oregano if it needs it, and salt it properly. It should taste a little stronger than you want in the finished dish, because the pasta will soak it up. Let it cool enough to handle if you are assembling ahead.
  2. Boil the pasta in well-salted water until it is a little shy of al dente — it will finish in the oven. Drain; don't rinse. Toss it with most of the sauce, reserving a few cups for the tops of the pans and for loosening leftovers.
  3. Beat the ricotta with the eggs, half the Parmesan, salt, and pepper. Butter the pans. Layer pasta, spoonfuls of ricotta, and mozzarella, repeating once, and finish with sauce, mozzarella, and the rest of the Parmesan.
  4. If you are baking now, heat the oven to 375°F and bake, covered with foil, 25 minutes, then uncovered until the cheese is browned in spots and the center is hot, 15 to 25 minutes more. Rest 10 minutes before cutting. If you are holding for a buffet, move portions to covered chafers and stir in a spoon of reserved sauce if the edges look tight.

Make ahead

Assemble the pans up to a day ahead, cover, and refrigerate. Take them out while the oven heats, then add 10 to 15 minutes to the covered bake. You can also bake them the day before, cool, refrigerate, and reheat, covered, until the center is hot. Label any meat sauce clearly, and make at least one pan with a sauce that is honestly vegetarian — not a meat sauce with the sausage picked out. A baked pasta is only a bargain if a helper, not the couple, is the one opening the oven.

Self-catering can be one of the more personal things you do for a wedding at home. It can also be the thing you regret if you treat labor as free and time as endless. Pick a menu that waits, a list that fits the kitchen, and a person who will run the pass — with a runner on the service table and a board for the things that can sit at room temperature. Then go and be in the yard, where you actually belong.
