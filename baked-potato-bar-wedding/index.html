A Baked Potato Bar That Eats Like a Wedding Dinner

A baked potato bar is a practical wedding dinner when you want a hot meal people can build themselves. Bake the potatoes in the oven, keep them hot, and put a slow-cooker bean chili on the table as the vegetarian topping that makes a potato into a plate.

The short version. A potato bar works in a backyard if the potatoes are fully baked, held hot, and served with toppings that include a real, hearty option — a bean chili you can make in a slow cooker the day before, plus a few cold toppings that do not all need a volunteer with a spoon. Plan one large potato per guest, plus extras, and bake them until a knife slides through, not until they merely feel hot. Reheat the chili to 165°F if it was made ahead, then hold it at or above 140°F. Put the line in a logical order: plates, potatoes, chili, then the rest, with a separate vegetarian space that is actually vegetarian. Skip a dozen toppings no one asked for, and skip holding cooked potatoes in the temperature danger zone while you take photographs.

Top picks

  • Best for most backyard lists: russet potatoes baked in the oven, a slow-cooker bean chili, and a short set of cold toppings, with the line clearly laid out.
  • Best if you also want a meat topping: a second, labeled pot — pulled chicken or chili with meat — kept completely separate from the bean pot.
  • Best if oven space is tight that afternoon: parbake earlier, finish in a hot oven before service, and do not try to bake a hundred potatoes in one thin tray at the last minute.

Why a potato bar works, and when it doesn't

You do not need a specialty aisle of props. You need a short, honest list. A slow cooker, for a hold, not for starting a raw batch at the last hour, a instant thermometer, a potato bar toppings caddy, a hotel pan, a tongs stainless 12 inch, which do more work on a buffet than a decorative serving set, a salad servers wood, a chafing dish 8 quart stainless, and a potato bar toppings caddy, which is the actual tool this part of the day uses, not a generic stand-in.

A potato bar is a hot, inexpensive meal that guests understand without a sign. It is naturally friendly to a mixed table: a vegetarian chili topping can be the main, butter and cheese can be enough for a child, and a second meat pot can sit beside the beans without touching them. It also travels from kitchen to yard better than a plated dinner, as long as you have a way to keep potatoes and chili hot.

It does not work if you treat the potato as the only thing on the plate and then put out shredded cheese and hope. A plain baked potato is a side. The chili below is what makes the bar a dinner. It also does not work if the potatoes are underbaked, if the line has no beginning, or if the only vegetarian option is a pot that had meat in it earlier. If your list is very small, a simpler plated dinner or a family-style main may be less work than a station. If your list is large, the bar is often kinder than trying to plate in a home kitchen.

Think about the yard. You need a table long enough for a line, shade, a trash for foils and cups, and a path that does not cut through the ceremony chairs. Put plates at the start, napkins at both ends, and a place for people to add toppings without reaching over the chili. Chafing dishes or slow cookers can hold the hot toppings; the potatoes can stay in a low oven or in an insulated cooler lined with clean towels for a short hold, then come out in waves so they do not sit cooling on a tray.

Tell people what it is. “Dinner is a baked potato bar, with a bean chili and a few toppings” is enough, on the website or in a note, so a guest who expected a plated roast is not surprised, and a guest who does not eat potatoes has time to eat beforehand or to ask you. Hospitality includes the warning.

The potatoes, from the oven

Use large russets, one per person plus extras for appetite and for a potato that splits. Scrub them, dry them, prick them, rub them with a little oil and salt, and bake them directly on a rack or on a heavy sheet pan at about 400°F until a knife slides in easily and the inside feels fluffy, often 50 to 75 minutes depending on size. Do not wrap them tightly in foil if you want a dry skin; foil is for holding after they are done, and even then only for a short time.

You can parbake earlier in the day — most of the way, until they are almost tender — cool them if they will wait more than a short stretch, and finish them in a hot oven before dinner so they are actually hot inside. What you should not do is bake them at noon, leave them on the counter until six, and call them dinner. Public food-safety guidance is to keep hot food at or above 140°F, and a potato is dense enough to cool slowly through the temperature range you do not want to linger in.

An instant-read thermometer is more useful for the chili than for the potatoes, but it will also tell you whether a held potato is still in a safe hot range if you are unsure. Wear gloves or use tongs to move them; a tray of hundred-degree potatoes will burn the volunteer who is trying to be helpful. Split each potato, or let guests split their own, but have a knife and a towel at the start of the line so the line does not stop while someone wrestles a whole one onto a plate.

Sweet potatoes can be a second, labeled tray if you know they will be eaten. Count them as part of the same one-per-person math, not as an extra automatic potato for every guest.

Slow-cooker bean chili, the topping that makes it a meal

This chili is built to sit on a potato: thick enough that it does not run off the plate, seasoned enough that a vegetarian guest is not making a dinner out of cheese. It uses canned beans, onions, peppers, garlic, tomatoes, and a straightforward chili-spice mixture, cooked until the flavors come together and the liquid has reduced. You can start it in a pot and move it to a slow cooker to hold, or cook it entirely on low in one or more cookers. For fifty, plan on two eight-quart cookers, or a stockpot plus a cooker; do not fill any cooker to the lid.

Make it the day before if you can. Chili is better after a rest, and the wedding day is a poor time to discover you under-seasoned a pot for a hundred. Cool it in shallow containers, refrigerate, and reheat until it is steaming and registers 165°F, then hold it at or above 140°F. Stir now and then, and keep a little reserved broth or water to loosen it if it thickens too much on the hold.

If you also want a meat chili, make it in a separate pot, with its own spoons and its own label. Do not try to serve both from a divided chafer that will be crossed by the same ladle. A pulled chicken in a second cooker is another option, cooked and held with the same temperature rules, but the bean chili should still be there and should still be a full recipe, not an afterthought of canned beans warmed in tomato sauce.

Slow-Cooker Bean Chili for a Wedding Potato Bar

A thick vegetarian bean chili meant to go over baked potatoes. The smaller batch makes about six to seven quarts, enough as a hearty topping for fifty potatoes; double it for a hundred, in more pots than one.

  1. In a wide pot, warm a film of oil and cook the onions and peppers with a pinch of salt until they soften and take on a little color. Add the garlic, chili powder, cumin, and paprika, and stir for a minute until the spices smell toasty. Stir in the tomato paste and cook it until it darkens slightly.
  2. Add the crushed tomatoes, beans, and broth. Bring to a simmer. Transfer to slow cookers, dividing the batch so no cooker is filled to the lid, or keep it on the stove at a gentle simmer.
  3. Cook on low until the chili is thick, glossy, and tasting like one thing rather than tomatoes and beans, usually 4 to 6 hours in a slow cooker, or about 1 hour 15 minutes on the stove, stirring now and then. Add a splash of broth if it gets thicker than a spoonable topping. Season with salt, pepper, and chipotle or cayenne if you want heat.
  4. If you are making it ahead, cool in shallow containers and refrigerate. Reheat until steaming and the center of the pot registers 165°F. Hold at or above 140°F in a slow cooker or a chafer, with a ladle in each pot, and loosen with reserved broth if it tightens on the hold.

This chili is the vegetarian main topping, not a side of beans. Keep it in its own pot, with its own ladle, even if a meat chili sits beside it. Leftovers should be cooled in shallow containers and refrigerated. Taste for salt after reheating, because cold chili will have tasted duller in the morning than it will on a potato at night.

Toppings, the line, and holding the heat

After the chili, keep the toppings short: grated cheese, a dairy-free option if you need one, sliced scallions, pickled jalapeños, a bowl of salsa, sour cream or a similar cool spoonful over ice, maybe steamed broccoli or sautéed vegetables if you have a way to keep them hot. That is enough. A board is not required; bowls with spoons are. Label anything that is not obvious, and do not put nuts over a chili that was otherwise a safe option for someone who cannot eat them.

Set the line so it has a beginning and an exit. A helper at the potatoes for the first twenty minutes will keep the first wave moving. After that, the station can often run with someone checking heat, ice, and trash. If you also have a drink station, keep it far enough away that the two lines do not lock together.

When dinner is clearly over, stop holding food that is no longer hot, pack leftovers that have been handled safely, and do not send home a pot that sat out for hours because it “still looked fine.” The potato bar is a generous dinner when the potatoes are actually baked and the chili is actually a meal. It is a disappointing one when either piece is an afterthought. Aim at the first version, and the station will feel like a dinner, not a topping bar looking for a main course.

A short potato-bar checklist

A week out, count potatoes, pots, and dietary needs, and decide whether there will be a second meat topping. The day before, make the chili, and confirm oven space and holding pans. The day of, bake or finish the potatoes, reheat the chili to 165°F, set the line, and assign one person to the first wave.

Have extras, have lids, and have a plan for the end of service. If you are also offering a salad or a simple green, put it off the main line so people can take it without stopping the potatoes. A potato bar does not need a speech. It needs a hot potato, a real chili, a short line, and a table that was set before anyone was hungry. Do those four things, and you can skip the rest of the Pinterest toppings without anyone missing them.

Write the hold temperatures on a card for the helper: chili reheated to 165°F, held at 140°F or above; potatoes kept hot and served in waves.

If the weather turns, move the station under a tent or indoors rather than leaving chafers in the rain. The meal can travel a little. The heat cannot be an afterthought once the lids are on. The bean chili on this bar is a topping, not the white chicken chili you would serve as a bowl dinner. A mac and cheese bar or a vegetarian cottage pie is a different station if you want a second hot pan, not more toppings.
