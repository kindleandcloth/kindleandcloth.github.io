A Stock Tank Drink Cooler for a Backyard Wedding (and a Water Station That Is Not the Bar)

A backyard wedding runs out of cold drinks faster than it runs out of almost anything else, and the bar should not be the only place to get water. A stock tank packed with ice, plus a second, clearly labeled water station, is the self-serve setup that keeps lines short and guests comfortable.

The short version. Use a 40-gallon stock tank as the self-serve drink trough: bottles and cans in ice, a scoop, and a place for empties. Set a second station — a beverage dispenser or a 5-gallon cooler — labeled "water," away from the cocktails, so nobody has to wait for a bartender to get a drink of water. Plan ice in waves, keep the tank in the shade, and assign someone to refill it. A pretty tub with no ice plan is just a heavy decoration.

Top picks

  • The trough: a 40-gallon oval stock tank, which holds drinks for a crowd and looks at home in a yard.
  • A more finished bar shape: an Igloo Party Bar cooler, if you want a lid, a drain, and a slightly more "bar" look.
  • The water station that is not the bar: a 2-gallon glass dispenser or a 5-gallon insulated water cooler, clearly labeled, in the shade.

Why the bar should not be the only place to get a drink

At a house wedding, the drink line is one of the first things that goes sideways. Everyone arrives thirsty, the bartender is still opening wine, and the people who just want water are standing in the same queue as the people who want a spritz. On a warm afternoon, that line is not a charming problem. It is guests standing in the sun.

A self-serve trough of drinks, and a second station that is only water, is the fix people who have hosted a backyard wedding recommend over and over. It is also cheaper than adding a second bartender to do a job a tank can do. Our budget wedding bar guide covers what to buy, how much ice is actually enough, and how to keep the station itself from becoming a puddle. This post is the tank, the water, and the refill plan.

You can make this look considered instead of like a farm-supply afterthought. A clean tank, a wooden crate beside it for a bottle opener, a small sign, and a few herb sprigs in the water dispenser are enough. The tank does not need a floral installation. It needs ice and a drain plan.

The tank, the ice, and how to set it up

A 40-gallon oval stock tank is the size that shows up in the best backyard photos and, more usefully, holds enough ice and bottles for a crowd of 60 to 100 without constant panic. Rinse it well; it was designed for water, sometimes for animals, and you want it clean before it holds drinks. Set it on level ground, in the shade, close to where people already walk, and not on a slope that will send meltwater toward the dance floor.

If you would rather have a lid and a built-in drain, an Igloo Party Bar or a rolling cooler with a drain is easier to empty and to move. The stock tank is the look and the capacity. The rolling cooler is the practical backup, and it is a good way to stage the second wave of ice in the shade of the house until you need it.

Fill the tank with a layer of ice first, then drinks, then more ice. Cans and bottles packed standing up waste less space than a jumble, and they are easier to grab. Leave a gap at one end for an ice scoop, not a drinking cup. Put a bottle opener on a crate or a hook right there, with a jar or a small bucket for caps. A towel on the edge of the tank, replaced once or twice, keeps the wet-hand problem from spreading.

Ice is the part people underestimate. Plan on about one pound of ice per guest for drinks, plus more if the day is hot and the tank is even partly in sun, and buy it in bags you can add in waves rather than one giant pile that melts into a warm bath by dinner. Keep backup bags in a freezer, a second cooler, or a neighbor's garage. Someone — not you, in your dress — should check the tank at the end of cocktail hour and again during dinner, scoop out some melt if needed, and add ice and drinks.

A water station that is not the bar

This is the piece that does as much work as the tank and gets skipped more often. Set a glass beverage dispenser, or better for a crowd, a 5-gallon insulated water cooler, a short walk from the cocktails, in the shade, with cups and a sign that says water. If you want a second flavor, lemonade or a non-alcoholic pitcher can sit beside it, labeled with simple drink labels so nobody has to ask. One of them should always be plain water.

Put cups upside down, or in a covered sleeve, so they stay clean. Have a place for empties. If children or a lot of non-drinkers are coming, this station should be easier to find than the bar, not tucked behind it.

In hot weather, the water station is a safety issue, not a courtesy. People who have been sitting in the sun, or dancing, will drink what is closest. Make the closest thing water, and make it obviously water.

Shade, meltwater, and the end of the night

Keep the tank out of direct afternoon sun if you can. A porch, a tree, the north side of the house, or a market umbrella over the station will do more for the ice than any extra bag you buy. If the only open spot is sunny, start with more ice than you think and accept that you will refill sooner.

Meltwater has to go somewhere. A tank without a drain will need to be bailed or carefully tipped onto a garden bed, not onto a walkway that will become a slip hazard. A cooler with a drain can run, through a hose if you have one, toward grass or a gravel area. Do not drain it onto an electrical cable or toward the dance floor. Our power plan is the reminder that water and cables should not share a puddle.

At the end of the night, fish out the last bottles, drain the tank, and give it a rinse before the leftover ice turns into a muddy pool. Leftover unopened drinks go back in a cooler or a fridge and become the next day's lunch. Leftover ice can go on a compost pile or a garden; it should not sit in the tank until Monday.

If you are using a real bar in addition to the tank, tell the bartender that water and a few non-alcoholic options live at the self-serve station, so they are not pouring water all night. The tank is there to make their job easier, not to compete with it.

What to skip

Skip a pretty copper tub that holds twelve bottles and looks finished in a photo. Skip putting only beer and soda in the tank and calling water "whatever the bartender has." Skip leaving the ice in the sun and hoping. Skip a tank on a slope, a tank with no opener, and a tank with no plan for meltwater. And skip making the couple responsible for the refill. Write the job down, give it to someone, and let the drinks stay cold without you.

A short stock-tank checklist

Get a 40-gallon tank or a large party cooler, rinse it, and set it in the shade on level ground. Pack ice, then drinks, then ice, with a scoop, an opener, and a place for caps. Build a second, labeled water station away from the bar. Buy more ice than you think, in bags you can add in waves, and assign a refill person. Drain the tank before you go to bed. That is the difference between a drink station and a warm puddle of cans.
