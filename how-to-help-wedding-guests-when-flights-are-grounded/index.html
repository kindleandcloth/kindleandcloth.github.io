How to Help Wedding Guests When Flights Are Grounded

When flights are canceled, the couple is often the last to hear, and the first expected to have a plan. You do not have to rebook anyone's ticket. You do have to tell people, clearly, what the day looks like now, and how they can still be part of it.

The short version. Send one message as soon as you know the ceremony is still on, moving, or waiting on a few delayed arrivals. Give guests a single number to text, a simple yes-or-no decision (I can still make it / I cannot), and a livestream link if you have one. Keep the local guests, the officiant, and the vendors on a calmer timeline rather than canceling the whole day because a few planes are on the ground. Then let people use their own refund and rebooking rights, which we walk through in what to do if a wedding or honeymoon flight is grounded.

Top picks

  • For a ceremony people can still watch from a delayed gate: a plug-in wireless lavalier on the officiant or the person saying vows, feeding a phone or laptop.
  • For the table that holds the plan: an acrylic sign holder with a printed update, the livestream QR code, and the number to text.
  • For guests who make it, but later than they meant to: a small USB-C power bank and a hanging toiletry bag in a welcome kit, plus a notebook for the person who is tracking arrivals.

Start from the facts, then tell everyone the same thing

On Friday, November 13, 2026, the FAA issued a nationwide ground stop after a telecommunications outage at air traffic control centers, then lifted it shortly after 8 p.m. Eastern. Reuters, citing FlightRadar24, reported more than 4,700 flights canceled and 10,000 delayed on Friday, with hundreds more already canceled for Saturday, and analysts said schedules could take days to recover. The details, and what a couple should do about their own tickets, are in our companion guide. This post is for the host side of the same weekend.

Do not make guests hunt for information in a group chat of 40 messages. Write one update, send it by text and email, and put it on the wedding website if you have one. A wedding weekend itinerary is the right place to change a rehearsal time or a shuttle, as long as the first line is the thing people need: whether they should still get on a plane, still get in a car, or watch from where they are.

A message you can send today

You can copy this and change the brackets:

Hi [name] — this is [couple / coordinator]. The nationwide flight ground stop on Friday has been lifted, but many flights are still canceled or delayed, and it may take time for airlines to catch up. Our ceremony is still planned for [time] at [place]. If you can get here safely, we would love you there, even if you arrive in whatever you flew in. If you cannot, please do not go to heroic lengths or spend money you will not get back. Text [number] with “I can make it,” “I’m delayed, new eta ___,” or “I can’t make it,” and we will [send a livestream link / save you a seat at a later gathering / both]. You are not in trouble either way. We are glad you were going to be with us.

Give one person the phone that receives those replies, and a notebook or a simple list, not a shared spreadsheet that three people are editing. The job is a headcount and a few arrival times, not a project plan. If you already printed a welcome sign, slide a half-sheet into an acrylic holder at the entrance with the same phone number, the new timing, and the livestream QR code, so the people who did arrive are not answering questions all afternoon.

A livestream is a backup, not a consolation prize

If even a handful of people you love cannot get a flight, a live video is the kindest spare plan you can set up in an afternoon. It does not need a production company. It needs a phone or laptop with a charged battery and a hotspot or reliable Wi-Fi, someone who is not in the wedding party to start it and leave it alone, and sound that actually picks up the vows. A small wireless lav on the officiant, or on whoever is speaking, will do more for distant guests than a prettier camera angle. Test it from the last row, the way we describe in our outdoor wedding microphone guide, and send the link in the same message as the update, not as a follow-up people will miss.

Tell guests how long the stream will run, and that they can watch in whatever they are wearing, including an airport chair. If the ceremony is unplugged for the people in the chairs, say so, and still allow the one official stream. Those two things can live together; our note on an unplugged backyard wedding is that phones down in the rows is about presence, not about locking the day inside the county.

Keep the local guests, and flex the day

Count who can still get there by car, who is already in town, and who is truly stuck. That number is your ceremony. If it is twelve people in a living room or a yard instead of eighty under a tent, it is still the wedding, and it may be a better one than a room of empty chairs you held for flights that will not land. A wedding at a parent's house or a simple backyard plan is allowed to absorb a day like this. You can host a larger later reception when travel is ordinary again.

Move the start time if a short delay would bring in the parents or the wedding party, and only if vendors can move with you. Ask the officiant, the photographer, and the person doing food, in that order, what they can actually do this evening or tomorrow. Do not promise guests a 6 p.m. ceremony before those people have said yes. If you already have a shuttle from a hotel, see the shuttle plan and tell the driver the new loop times, or cancel the empty runs.

Guests who land late can still come in travel clothes. Say that out loud. A basket of toiletries in a hanging bag, a loaner power bank, and a quiet chair is a better welcome than a forced apology at the bar. If you packed welcome bags, leave a few extras at the door for the people who arrive after dinner.

What you can and cannot fix for their tickets

You cannot refund a guest's airfare, and you should not try to negotiate fifty airline chats. Point them to the airline app and to the DOT refunds page. If their flight was canceled, or significantly delayed or changed, and they choose not to travel, they are generally entitled to a refund even on a nonrefundable ticket. They may also be offered a rebooking. That is their decision, based on whether they can still arrive in time to have a day, not a night in a hotel they will sleep through.

What you can do is take the social pressure off. Say, in the message, that you will not be hurt if they use the refund and stay home. Say that a heroic redeye, a huge new fare, or a six-hour drive after a missed night of sleep is not what you want from them. Then keep your word when a favorite cousin texts that they cannot come.

What to skip

Skip a group chat debate about whose airline is worse. Skip postponing the entire wedding in a late-night message and walking it back at breakfast. Skip asking guests to forward you every boarding pass so you can "see what you can do." Skip making the livestream the only plan if most of your people are local and already dressed. And skip filling empty seats with last-minute acquaintances just so the room looks full. The people who made it are the wedding.

A short checklist for hosts

Send one update with the plan, a reply number, and a livestream link if you have it. Give one person the replies and a written list. Ask the officiant, photographer, and food person whether a later start is possible before you advertise one. Set up a simple stream with a lav and a test. Welcome late arrivals without a dress code. Point stranded guests to their airline and to the DOT refund rules, and let them decide. Then go be at your own wedding with the people who are in the room.

Questions couples actually ask

Should we cancel if a lot of guests cannot fly in?

Not automatically. If you, the officiant, and enough of the people you cannot imagine doing this without can still be there, a smaller ceremony and a livestream is usually kinder than a full postponement. Cancel or move the day when the couple or the legal ceremony cannot happen, or when vendors cannot hold the event at any nearby time. Tell guests either way, in one message, as soon as the decision is real.

Do we need to refund guests for their flights or hotel rooms?

Usually no, not as a host obligation, and you should not promise it in a group text. Guests who had a flight canceled or significantly changed can typically get their airfare refunded by the airline if they choose not to travel, under DOT rules. Hotels depend on the booking. Be warm, be clear, and do not become everyone's travel agent.

How late can we move the ceremony on the same day?

As late as the officiant, the photographer, and the food can still do their jobs, and as late as your guests with children, elders, or a long drive home can reasonably stay. Ask the vendors first. A shift of an hour or two can catch a delayed arrival. A ceremony at 10 p.m. because one flight might land is how everyone else has a worse day.

What if we do not have a good internet connection in the yard?

Use a phone hotspot, or move the camera just inside a doorway where the vows can still be heard. A lav helps when the phone is not right next to the couple. If a live stream will be too fragile, record the ceremony (with the officiant's okay) and send the video that night. A few hours late is better than a dropped call in the middle of the vows.
