Wedding Charcuterie Cups: A Grazing Table You Can Hold in One Hand

Wedding charcuterie cups are a grazing table you can hold in one hand: cheese, cured meat, a skewer of marinated mozzarella and olives, fruit, and a cracker standing up at the back, built in a paper cup a few hours ahead and set out in cold waves through cocktail hour. One recipe below makes 24 cups; double it for 48.

The short version. Plan about one cup per guest for a cocktail hour before dinner, plus roughly ten percent. Prep everything the day before, assemble the cups no more than about three hours ahead, and keep them refrigerated. Put crackers in last and stand them at the back so they stay crisp. Carry trays out in waves rather than leaving every cup on the table, and pull anything that's been out about two hours (less on a hot day). Label meat-free and nut-containing cups clearly.

Top picks

  • Best cup: 12-ounce paper cups lined with precut parchment squares, corners up.
  • Best skewer: 4.7-inch bamboo knot picks, long enough to stand above the rim and grab.
  • Best display: acrylic risers for height, so a table of cups looks like a display, not a row of drinks.

Why cups beat a board at cocktail hour

Picture the first ten minutes after the ceremony: everyone's holding a drink, half of them are still hugging people, and the grazing table has a crowd three deep reaching across each other with toothpicks. Now picture a tray coming around with little paper cups, a fan of salami and a cracker poking out of each one. People take one and keep talking. That's why charcuterie cups have become a cocktail-hour favorite. They solve the one-free-hand problem.

Here's the honest part. Cups are more assembly than a board. Forty-eight cups means forty-eight little arrangements, and a cup of cheese and cured meat is still perishable food that needs to stay cold. They also cost a bit more in containers and labor than one big board. They're worth it for a stand-up cocktail hour, a backyard where the table space is tight, or a crowd where a shared board makes people nervous. For a seated, leisurely spread, a board may still be the better tool.

If you'd like the board version, our guide to a wedding grazing table covers it in full, with a whipped feta recipe. For passed hot bites alongside the cups, see cocktail-hour appetizers.

How many cups, and what goes in one

For a typical cocktail hour before a full dinner, plan about one cup per guest, plus roughly ten percent for the people who take a second and the helpers who never got a first. If the cups are the main food at a longer stand-up reception, plan closer to two per guest alongside other bites. If there's also a grazing table or several passed appetizers, one per guest is generous.

A good cup has four or five things in it, so it feels like a snack and not a sample: something salty (folded salami or prosciutto), a cheese or two, something fresh (grapes or apple), something briny (olives or a little pickle), and something crunchy (a cracker or a breadstick). A skewer of marinated mozzarella and olives adds height and makes the cup look finished. A couple of dried apricots or a few nuts round it out.

Make at least two versions so the meat-free guests aren't picking salami out. Keep each version on its own tray with its own label. If any cup has nuts, make it obvious, or leave nuts out entirely and put a small bowl of them elsewhere.

Building them without a 2 a.m. assembly line

Do all the prep the day before. Cube the cheese, marinate the mozzarella, wash and cut the grapes into small clusters, fold the salami, and drain the olives. Store each component in its own container in the refrigerator. That way, assembly is just placing things, not cutting, and it goes fast.

Line each paper cup with a parchment square so the corners stand up above the rim. It looks intentional, keeps grease off the cup, and gives the cracker something to lean against. A 4-inch square in a 12-ounce cup is about right.

Set up an assembly line on a long counter or a folding table: cups, then salami, then cheese, then skewer, then fruit, then crackers at the very end. Two or three people working one station each will build 48 cups in well under an hour. Cut cheese into small shapes with mini cutters if you want a little whimsy, or keep it to clean cubes with a small cheese knife set. Cubes are faster and look just as good.

Assemble no more than about three hours before serving, cover the trays loosely, and refrigerate. Add crackers and breadsticks last, standing upright at the back of the cup against the parchment, so they don't touch the wet cheese and go soft.

This recipe makes 24 classic cups with a marinated mozzarella skewer in each; the second column doubles it for 48. Make the meat-free version by swapping the salami for a second cheese and an extra skewer.

Wedding Charcuterie Cups with Marinated Mozzarella Skewers

Paper cups lined with parchment and filled with folded salami, cheddar, a skewer of lemon-herb marinated mozzarella and olives, grapes, dried apricots, and a cracker at the back. Prep the day before, assemble a few hours ahead, and keep cold. 24 cups; 48 doubled.

Directions

  1. The day before, whisk the olive oil, lemon zest, oregano, pepper flakes, salt, and pepper in a bowl. Add the drained mozzarella pearls, toss, cover, and refrigerate overnight.
  2. Also the day before: cube the cheddar, fold the salami, wash and dry the grapes and cut them into small clusters, and drain the olives. Refrigerate each in its own covered container.
  3. Up to about 3 hours before serving, thread each pick with a mozzarella pearl, an olive, a second pearl, and a second olive.
  4. Line each cup with a parchment square, corners up. Add 2 folded salami slices, 3 cheddar cubes, a grape cluster, and 2 apricots, then stand the skewer in the cup.
  5. Cover the trays loosely and refrigerate until serving. Right before the trays go out, tuck 2 crackers or a breadstick upright at the back of each cup, against the parchment, and add a rosemary sprig.
  6. Serve in waves from the refrigerator. Pull any cups that have been out about 2 hours, or about 1 hour in heat above 90°F.

For meat-free cups, replace the salami with a second cheese (such as a firm gouda) and add a second skewer. Label meat-free, nut, and gluten-free cups and keep them on separate trays. Drain the marinated mozzarella well before threading so it doesn't drip into the cup. Keep everything refrigerated until it goes out.

Shopping, timing, and who does what

A week out: buy the shelf-stable things: cups, parchment, picks, crackers, breadsticks, dried apricots, olives, and salami in sealed packages with dates that clear the wedding. Count the cups twice. Running out of containers at assembly is a silly way to lose an hour.

Two days out: buy the cheese, mozzarella pearls, grapes, and herbs. Make room in the refrigerator, or line up a borrowed one. Forty-eight cups on trays take more shelf space than the raw ingredients did, so measure your trays against the shelves now, not at noon on the day.

The day before: marinate the mozzarella, cube, fold, wash, and drain everything, and store each component in its own container. Write the assembly order on a sheet of paper and tape it to the wall above the station.

The day of: assemble about three hours ahead with two or three helpers, cover, and refrigerate. Name one person, not the couple, to run the trays out in waves during cocktail hour and pull anything that has been out too long. That's a perfect job for a friend who wants to help but doesn't want to cook.

If you're paying a caterer or a charcuterie business instead, ask exactly what goes in each cup, how far ahead they assemble, how they keep the cups cold on the way, and whether they'll stay to run the trays. A drop-off with no one to refill is a display that empties in fifteen minutes and then sits.

Display, serving, and keeping them cold

The display is where cups go from "snack" to "wedding." Set them on acrylic risers of a few heights so they read as a tiered display, or line them up on slate boards that double as trays when it's time to carry a round out. Wood risers suit a rustic backyard; clear acrylic disappears and lets the food show.

Serve in waves. Put out one tray when guests arrive, keep the rest in the refrigerator, and bring out the next tray as the first empties. That way the display always looks generous, and nothing sits out long. Perishable food shouldn't stay out more than about two hours, and about one hour if it's over 90°F. In a backyard in the sun, that clock runs faster than you'd expect, so put the display in the shade.

A small chalkboard label by each tray ("Classic: salami, cheddar, mozzarella," "Meat-free," "Contains nuts") saves a lot of questions. Put a small bowl for used picks and a stack of napkins at the end of the display. Somebody will want to put their skewer down, and the lawn is not where you want it.

If you want a drink to go with them, the wedding sangria guide is a natural pairing, and cups are a favorite at a backyard bridal shower too.

The kit, in one place: 12-ounce paper cups and precut parchment squares for the cups themselves; bamboo knot picks for the skewers; a small cheese knife set and mini cutters for prep; acrylic risers and slate boards for the display; and mini chalkboard labels so nobody has to guess what's inside.

What to skip

Skip assembling the night before; the crackers will be soft and the cheese will sweat. Skip soft, runny cheeses that collapse in a cup; firm cheeses hold up. Skip putting every cup out at once. Skip a sunny table. Skip unlabeled nuts. And skip making forty-eight different arrangements; a consistent cup looks better on a riser and goes much faster. If you have leftovers, our guide to leftover wedding food covers what's safe to keep: refrigerated, untouched cups yes; cups that sat out for hours, no.

A short charcuterie-cup checklist

Plan about one cup per guest plus ten percent. Prep every component the day before and marinate the mozzarella overnight. Line paper cups with parchment. Assemble up to about three hours ahead, crackers last and standing at the back. Make a labeled meat-free tray. Display on risers in the shade, serve in waves from the refrigerator, and pull anything that's been out about two hours. Then grab a cup yourself while there's still a tray in the fridge.
