A Food Truck Wedding That Doesn't Stall Dinner

The short version: A food truck wedding works when the truck can actually get in, stay legal, stay powered, and feed your list without a single-file line that stalls the evening. Get a quote that includes travel, hours, menu, and staffing; walk the access path with the operator; and plan a second service window or a second truck if the headcount is large. A truck is only a bargain if dinner still happens on time. Our food ideas post flagged that; this guide is the logistics and the cost structure.

Top picks

Three ways to use a truck without turning dinner into a queue

Best for most lists

One truck with a short menu, a clearly staffed window, and a plan for a second wave or a held side so early guests aren't still waiting when speeches start. Confirm access, power or a generator, and permits before you fall in love with the paint job.

Best for a larger list

Two trucks, or a truck plus a drop-off or a salad-and-bread table, so the line can split. Two menus that share a cuisine are often calmer than two unrelated concepts competing for the same hungry hour.

Best as a late bite, not the dinner

A truck that arrives after the meal for a short, clearly timed window — a slice, a taco, a grilled cheese — can be a very good late-night plan. That is a different quote and a different parking problem than feeding everyone at 6 p.m. See our late-night snacks guide for timing.

A food truck at a backyard wedding can feel like the right kind of casual: a cook who already knows their menu, a little theater, and food that doesn't ask you to invent a kitchen. It can also be the most expensive traffic jam you have ever hosted, if the truck can't clear the gate, the generator is louder than the vows, or eighty people form a single line at one window.

Hire a truck the way you would hire a caterer, not the way you would book a lunch. You are buying access, time, a menu that can feed your list, and a line plan. The vehicle is just the kitchen that happens to roll in.

What you are actually buying

If you are going to buy anything for this, buy the tools the work actually uses. A wedding food-truck quote is usually some mix of a per-person or a per-ticket price, a minimum spend, travel, hours on site, staff, and sometimes a generator or a permit fee. Some operators sell tickets or tokens; some run an open window for a set window of time; some will plate and send trays to a buffet table so the truck is the kitchen, not the line. All of those can work. What you need in writing is what is included, how many people they can realistically serve in an hour, what happens if you go over, and when they will arrive and leave.

Ask for a menu that is short. A truck with twelve items will serve more slowly than a truck with three excellent ones. A taco truck, a pizza oven truck, a grilled-cheese or smash-burger window, a falafel or a noodle shop — pick a cuisine your list will eat, and offer one clearly vegetarian option that is a real dish. If you also want a salad, bread, or a dessert, it may be kinder to add a small table or a drop-off than to make the truck do everything through one window.

Get the quote in writing, including tax, gratuity if they expect one, overtime, and whether leftover food can be left with you. Then add your own numbers: plates if they don't provide them, a drink table, shade for the line, and any permit or HOA cost. City and season change the bid; treat a per-person range you read in a roundup as a sense-check, not a bid. Get a local quote, and get a second one.

Access, the ground, and a truck that can actually park

Walk the path before you pay a deposit. Measure the gate, the driveway, the overhead branches, and the turn. Ask the operator for the truck's height, length, and a weight they are willing to put on your lawn or a paver driveway. Soft ground after rain, a steep grade, a low arbor, and a shared alley are the usual surprises. If the truck cannot reach the yard, you will be serving from the street, which may be illegal, unkind to neighbors, or both.

Stand where the window will be and imagine a line of thirty people. The line should not block the ceremony exit, the restrooms, the bar, or a fire exit from a tent. Put the nose of the truck where the operator wants it for service and for their own kitchen door, and make sure a guest in dress shoes isn't standing in gravel or mud. A firm, lit path matters after dusk; our lighting plan and restroom guide still apply.

Neighbors and towns have opinions about commercial vehicles on residential streets. Ask about permits, idling, hours, and whether a food truck is even allowed at a private event in your city. Some places treat this as catering and some treat it as a mobile restaurant. The operator may know the rule; you should still check, because the fine or the shutdown will be on your Saturday, not on their brochure. Tell neighbors when a truck will arrive and where it will sit.

Power, water, smoke, and noise

Many trucks bring a generator. That generator has a volume, an exhaust, and a fuel plan. Listen to it, or ask for a model and a honest word about noise, before you park it twenty feet from the ceremony. If the truck can tie into house power, that is a different problem: it is a load, and it is not a casual plug on a kitchen island. Our power plan is the right reading if anyone suggests running the truck off the house. Use outdoor-rated cords only for the things you actually control — lights, a drink station — and don't improvise a tie-in you don't understand.

Ask how the truck handles water, wastewater, and trash. A backyard wedding already produces a surprising amount of waste; a truck should not add a pile of oil or gray water to your side yard. Agree on a bin, a pickup, and who empties what. If the cuisine involves a grill or a fryer, think about smoke and grease relative to a tent wall, a dry hedge, and guests in pale clothes.

The truck needs a little back-of-house: a place for staff to stand, a cooler, a way to reach the door without walking through the receiving line. Treat the operators as vendors with a schedule, a meal if they are there for a long window, and a bathroom plan that isn't "the one house bathroom during cocktail hour."

Count how many people the operator says they can serve in thirty minutes, and believe the slower number. If that number is smaller than your list, you need a second truck, a second wave (family tables first, or a released line), or a buffet the truck plates in pans. Don't hope that people will "just trickle over." They will all be hungry at once, right after the ceremony photos.

Give the line shade, a little structure, and something to drink so people aren't standing empty-handed in the sun. A dispenser of water or iced tea at a nearby table helps; the bar should stay separate so the food line is not also the beer line. Put a short menu on a sign, with the vegetarian item marked, so decisions happen before the window, not at it. Our taco bar and pizza reception notes on labels and flow still apply if the truck is serving those cuisines.

Someone from your side should be the truck's person: a helper who can find the operator, confirm a headcount change, and not pull the couple out of photographs because the propane is low. Exchange numbers the day before. Confirm the arrival window again the morning of.

Plates, seating, and what guests need to know

A truck window is not a dining room. If dinner is coming from that window, people need a place to sit, a trash plan, and a drink that is not only at the bar. Set out real tables — folding tables are fine — and enough chairs for at least two-thirds of the list. A backyard full of guests eating from paper boats in a driveway will feel like a tailgate, and not always in a good way. Put trash and recycling where the line ends, not at the bumper, and give someone the job of emptying them.

Tell guests, clearly, that dinner is a food truck. Put the cuisine and the service window on the website: the name of the truck, what they serve, and when the window will be open. If you are using tokens, say so, and have extras in someone's pocket. If there is a vegetarian or vegan item, name it. If the truck cannot do a gluten-free or allergen-safe plate, do not imply that it can. A short printed menu at the front of the line, with those same notes, keeps decisions from happening at the window.

A small table next to the line can hold water, a citrus drink, extra napkins, utensils if the truck doesn't provide them, and that menu. That table is also where you can put a salad or bread if the truck is only doing the main. In warm weather, a cold drink at the line is not a flourish; it is how you keep a queue from getting cranky. Use glass dispensers in the shade, with a ladle or a spigot that actually works, and a backup pitcher in a cooler. The bar should stay separate, so the food line is not also the beer line. Our bar plan still applies.

Plates and napkins are easy to under-count. If the truck does not supply them, buy more than the headcount — pizza, tacos, and anything saucy will take more than one napkin. Compostable ware is a reasonable choice if you have a way to actually compost or if you just prefer the look; it is not automatically better if it all goes in the same bag. Decide that in advance, and label the bins.

Timing, weather, and a backup if the truck is late

Build time into the day for the truck to park, level, and get the kitchen hot before you want the first plate. A 5 p.m. dinner does not mean a 4:55 arrival. Put the arrival on the day-of timeline, and don't park the truck during the ceremony unless you have already walked the sound and the sight line.

Weather is a truck problem as well as a guest problem. Rain can make a lawn impassable. Heat is hard on a kitchen and on a line. Wind is hard on signs, napkins, and some hoods. Agree on a backup parking spot, and have a small backup food plan — even a grocery tray you can hold in a chafer — if the truck cannot get in. That is not pessimistic. That is how you avoid a yard full of hungry people and a vehicle stuck at the corner.

When the window closes, know whether the truck will stay for a late-night service or leave, and how you will end the line politely. Leftovers are not always yours; ask. Food that has been held correctly can be a kindness to send home. Food that sat in a warm box on the bumper is not.

A watermelon agua fresca for the line

People waiting at a truck window need something cold in their hands that is not only a beer. A watermelon-lime agua fresca — the classic agua de sandía — is the usual answer: ripe fruit, water, lime, and a little sugar, strained so it pours cleanly from a dispenser. Make it the morning of, keep it cold, and set it at a table beside the line, not on the truck's bumper.

Recipe

Watermelon-Lime Agua Fresca for a Food-Truck Line

A strained watermelon drink, sharpened with lime, served from a dispenser in the shade. It is a line drink, not a cocktail.

  • Yield: about 50 glasses (8 ounces)
  • Prep: 40 minutes, plus chilling
  • Cook: none

Four large melons, strained and loosened with a little water, should yield a little over 3 gallons — enough for about fifty 8-ounce servings. Taste as you go; sweetness varies with the fruit.

Steps

  1. Cut the watermelon away from the rind and discard any large seeds. Working in batches, blend the flesh until it is a loose puree. Add a little of the cold water if the blender needs help.
  2. Strain the puree through a fine-mesh strainer or a double layer of cheesecloth into a clean pot or pitcher, stirring so the juice runs through. You can leave a little pulp if you like a cloudier drink; for a dispenser, a cleaner strain clogs less.
  3. Stir in the lime juice, a pinch of salt, and the smaller amount of sugar. Taste. The drink should taste like watermelon, not like punch. Add water only if it is too thick to pour, and more lime or a little sugar only if it needs it. Chill it thoroughly.
  4. Pour into dispensers set in the shade, or nestled in a tub of ice. Put ice in the glasses, not usually in the dispenser, so the last guests don't get a watery drink. Label it, set out cups, and keep a backup pitcher cold. Someone should watch the spigot and wipe the table.

Make ahead

You can juice the limes and chill the melons the day before. The agua itself is best made the morning of; watermelon oxidizes and can taste dull if it sits overnight. If you need a drink that holds longer, make a hibiscus agua (jamaica) instead: steep dried hibiscus in hot water, sweeten lightly, chill, and finish with lime — it will keep overnight and doesn't depend on a ripe melon. Keep a plain water dispenser next to either one.

A food truck can be a wonderful backyard dinner when the vehicle, the permit, the power, and the line are all solved before Saturday. Do that work, and the painted truck can be what it should be: a kitchen that showed up and fed people well, on time. Keep a board or two for toppings at the end of the line, crates as a low back shelf, and an extra slow cooker only if you have a real power plan for a second hot pot.
