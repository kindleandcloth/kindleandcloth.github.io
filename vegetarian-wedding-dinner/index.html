A Vegetarian Wedding Dinner That Doesn't Feel Like a Side

A vegetarian wedding dinner should be a main, not a plate of sides. A mushroom-and-lentil cottage pie with a mashed-potato lid can be assembled ahead, baked in 9x13 pans, and held hot, so a backyard list does not have to rely on a single stuffed portobello at the end of a meat line.

The short version. If some or all of your guests do not eat meat, give them a real main: a pan with a filling, a topping, and a side, not a garnish next to someone else’s roast. This cottage pie — mushrooms, lentils, a simple mash — bakes in 9x13 dishes and holds at or above 140°F. Two pans feed about 20 to 24 with a salad and bread; four pans feed about 40 to 48. Keep it completely vegetarian (use soy sauce, not anchovy Worcestershire), label it, and if you also serve a meat dish, give that its own pans and its own utensils. This is not a potato bar, and it is not the squash soup in the winter food guide. It is dinner.

A main, not a side

Buy the few things you will actually use, not a themed set you will store once. A instant thermometer, so you are not guessing at 165°F or at a hold above 140°F, a pyrex 9x13, a lodge dutch oven, a lodge skillet, a macaroni and cheese pan deep, which is the actual tool this part of the day uses, not a generic stand-in, a lasagna pan deep ceramic, a oven thermometer, and a instant read thermometer, so you are not guessing at 165°F or at a hold above 140°F.

A lot of “vegetarian options” at weddings are an afterthought: a stack of roasted vegetables, a pasta with no protein, a stuffed mushroom that looks lonely on a large plate. Guests who do not eat meat notice, and so do guests who do, because a weak option tells the whole table that one list was designed and the other was accommodated. If your wedding is vegetarian, or mostly is, or simply includes people you care about who do not eat meat, plan a main with the same seriousness you would give a roast.

Cottage pie does that work without asking you to run a restaurant. The filling is savory and brown, the lid is familiar, and the pan can wait. It is not trendy. It is also not a baked-potato station; the mash is a crust, not a bar of toppings. People can be served a square, or they can serve themselves from a dish with a spatula, the way they would a lasagna.

Count from the list. As a single main, two 9x13 pans will feed about 20 to 24 people with salad and bread; four pans will feed about 40 to 48. If you also have a meat main, you can make less pie, but do not make so little that the vegetarian guests are visibly rationed. It is better to have leftover pie the next day than a short, awkward tray.

Say what is in it. Mushrooms, lentils, potatoes, butter, milk, wheat if you thicken with flour. If someone does not eat mushrooms, this is not their dish — have a second, honest option, even a simple baked pasta or a bean stew in its own pot, rather than a pie that pretends not to be what it is. If you need it to be vegan, you will have to change the mash (olive oil and a splash of unsweetened plant milk, tasted carefully) and skip the butter in the filling; do that on purpose, in writing, not as a quiet substitution you hope no one will notice.

The filling, the mash, and the timing

Brown the mushrooms in batches in a skillet or a wide pot so they actually take on color. Crowding them steams them, and steamed mushrooms make a gray filling. Onions, carrots, and celery go in after, then tomato paste, then the lentils and stock, and a little flour to help the sauce sit. Soy sauce, or a vegetarian Worcestershire, gives you the savory depth that meat stock would have given. Taste for salt at the end, not at the beginning, because the liquid will reduce.

Cook the lentils until they are tender but still hold their shape. Canned lentils, drained and rinsed, will work if you are short on time; add them later so they do not collapse. The filling should be thick enough that a spoon dragged through it leaves a path. If it is soupy, it will flood the mash in the oven. Cool it slightly before you top it, especially if you are assembling ahead.

The mash should be smooth enough to spread, not a heap of lumps that leaves gaps. Warm milk and butter help. Spread it to the edges of the pan so the filling does not bubble over in a way that dries the corners, and rough the surface with a fork so it browns. You can assemble the pans a day ahead, refrigerate them, and bake them the next day until the center is hot and the top is patched with gold. A thermometer in the middle of the filling should read at least 165°F if the pans were cold. Hold, covered, at or above 140°F.

Mushroom-and-Lentil Cottage Pie for a Wedding Crowd

A savory mushroom-and-lentil filling under a mashed-potato lid, baked in 9x13 pans. The smaller batch is two pans; the larger is four. Use soy sauce or a vegetarian Worcestershire. Hold at or above 140°F; reheat leftovers to 165°F.

  1. Cover the potatoes with salted water in a large pot, bring to a boil, and cook until a knife slides through, 15 to 20 minutes. Drain well, then mash with the mash butter and enough warm milk to make a spreadable mash. Season with salt and pepper. Cover to keep warm, or cool if you are assembling ahead.
  2. Meanwhile, heat a thin film of olive oil in a Dutch oven or a wide, heavy pot over medium-high heat. Brown the mushrooms in batches, without crowding, until they give off their liquid and take on color. Transfer each batch to a bowl.
  3. Lower the heat to medium. Add the filling butter and a little more oil if the pot is dry, then the onions, carrots, and celery. Cook, stirring, until the onions are soft and the carrots have begun to give, 8 to 10 minutes. Stir in the garlic, then the tomato paste, and cook a minute more. Sprinkle in the flour and stir until it disappears.
  4. Return the mushrooms to the pot. Add the lentils, stock, soy sauce or vegetarian Worcestershire, thyme, and bay leaves. Simmer, stirring now and then, until the filling is thick and savory and a spoon leaves a path, 10 to 15 minutes. Stir in the peas, if using, and cook 2 minutes more. Remove the bay leaves. Taste and add salt and pepper. It should taste a little stronger than you want the finished pie to taste.
  5. Heat the oven to 400°F. Divide the filling between two buttered 9x13-inch dishes (four for the larger batch). Spread the mash over the filling, all the way to the edges, and rough the top with a fork. Bake until the filling bubbles at the edges and the lid is patched with gold, 25 to 35 minutes, longer if the pans were refrigerated, until the center of the filling reaches at least 165°F. Rest 10 minutes before serving. Hold, covered, at or above 140°F. Reheat leftovers to 165°F.

Brown the mushrooms in batches; a crowded pan will steam them. For the larger recipe, use two pots, or make the filling in two rounds. Do not use a traditional Worcestershire sauce if you need the pie to be vegetarian, unless you have checked the label.

How to serve it, and what to put beside it

A green salad, a sharp dressing, and bread or a board of something to drag through the sauce are enough beside the pie. A second vegetable — buttered peas or a simple braised green — is welcome and not required. You do not need a gravy boat if the filling was moist enough, though a small jug of extra sauce, made from a ladle of filling thinned with stock, can help the first slices.

Serve from the pans, or cut squares onto plates if you have a person who can do that quickly. A chafing dish works if you can keep the lid on between servings so the mash does not dry. Use a thermometer, and do not wander away from a dairy mash on a warm table. Label the dish “mushroom and lentil cottage pie, vegetarian,” and if a meat main is also being served, put them on separate tables or at least with a clear gap and separate utensils.

This is a winter and early-spring dish, but it will work on a cool evening in a yard if the holding is honest. In high summer, think about a cooler room and a shorter hold, or choose a different main. Leftovers reheat well the next day, which is a kindness in a house that just hosted a wedding. A vegetarian dinner that people go back to for a second square has done its job: it was the meal, not the alternative.

A short vegetarian-dinner checklist

A day or two ahead, cook the lentils if you are starting from dry, and you can make the filling and the mash and assemble the pans. Keep them cold. The day of, bake to 165°F in the center, then hold at or above 140°F. Toss the salad close to service, set the bread out, and put a person with a spatula at the pie if the list is long.

Tell the caterer, or the aunt who is helping, that this is a main, not a side. Write the label. Taste a square before you serve it. After the meal, cool leftovers promptly and reheat them later to 165°F, or let them go. The point of a vegetarian wedding dinner is not to announce a philosophy. It is to feed the people at the table as if you meant to. A baked ziti with a labeled meatless pan, or a family-style table with a real vegetarian platter, can sit beside this pie if you need two mains. If you do that — a real pan, a clear label, a salad, a place to sit — the dish will not need a speech, and no one will have to pretend that a side is a meal. That is the standard, and it is not a difficult one once you have decided, early, to meet it in the same week you plan the rest of the wedding menu at home, without leaving it as a last-minute side.
