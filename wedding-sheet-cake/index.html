A homemade wedding sheet cake is the relaxed, generous version of the wedding cake: one flat, frosted rectangle that serves about 48 from a two-layer half sheet, cuts into a neat grid, and rides to the party flat in a box. Bake the layers ahead, freeze them, and frost the day before.

The short version. A two-layer half sheet (about 13 by 18 inches) serves roughly 48 squares of 2 by 2 inches. For 100 guests, bake two: one decorated for the cutting, one plainer cake in the kitchen. Bake each layer as its own batch in a 1-inch half-sheet pan, cool completely, wrap, and freeze. The day before, stack the layers on a cake board with buttercream, frost, decorate with a few flowers or a simple piped border, and keep it cool. Cut it in a grid with a long knife, wiping between cuts.

Why a sheet cake works at a backyard wedding

Imagine a long, low cake on a wooden board at the end of the dessert table, white buttercream swooped across the top, a cluster of garden roses at one corner, the couple's initials piped in the other. It looks like it belongs at a dinner party. That's the appeal. Sheet cakes have been a real wedding trend for a few years now, and it's easy to see why: they're generous, they cut cleanly, and nobody has to engineer a tower.

For a wedding at home, a sheet cake also solves the hard parts of a homemade cake. There are no dowels, no stacking tiers, no car ride with a three-story cake sliding on the back seat. It's two layers and a board. A confident home baker can make one. A nervous one can make one too, if they bake ahead and give themselves a quiet evening to frost it.

Here's the honest part: it's still a project. Two layers for 48 people means about twelve cups of flour, a lot of butter, and real refrigerator space. If baking for a crowd sounds like more than you want on your plate, a grocery bakery sheet cake dressed up with your own flowers is a perfectly good answer, and our budget wedding dessert table has other shortcuts.

How many servings you get

Sheet cakes are easy to count because they're a grid. For a cake about two inches tall, a 2-by-2-inch square is a generous party serving. A two-layer half sheet, roughly 13 by 18 inches, cuts into about 54 squares on paper; plan on about 48, because the edges and the first few cuts are never perfect. A quarter sheet, the size of a 9-by-13 pan, gives about 24.

If there are other desserts, such as a donut wall or a cookie table, plan a little less cake, since not everyone takes both. If the cake is the only dessert, plan on one square per guest and a few extra for the people who come back.

The kitchen cake is an old caterer's trick worth borrowing. Only one cake has to be beautiful. The second gets a plain coat of the same buttercream, waits in the kitchen, and is cut into squares on a tray while the first one is being photographed.

Baking two thin layers, not one thick one

A deep 13-by-18 pan of batter is hard to bake evenly in a home oven: the edges dry out before the center sets. Two thin layers, each baked in a one-inch-deep half-sheet pan, bake faster and more evenly, and stacking them with buttercream in the middle gives you a taller, more wedding-like cake.

Mix one layer's worth of batter at a time. A full two-layer batch is more than most stand mixers can handle, and mixing one at a time means the second batch is fresh when the first comes out of the oven. Grease the pan, line it with a precut parchment sheet, and grease the parchment too. Wrapping the pan in damp cake strips keeps the edges from baking faster than the middle, so the layer stays flat and doesn't need much trimming.

Bake until a toothpick in the center comes out clean and the top springs back. Let the layer cool in the pan for about fifteen minutes, then turn it out onto a cooling rack, a cake board, or a second sheet pan lined with parchment. Thin layers are fragile when warm; let them cool completely before you try to move them.

Wrapped well, the layers freeze beautifully. Two layers of plastic wrap and a layer of foil, flat on a sheet pan, for up to about two weeks. Frozen layers are also far easier to stack than fresh ones, because they don't tear.

White almond sheet cake for a crowd

White almond is the flavor most people think of as "wedding cake": a tender white crumb, a little vanilla, and a whisper of almond. This recipe makes one two-layer half-sheet cake, about 48 squares; the second column doubles it for two cakes, about 96. Mix and bake one layer at a time.

White Almond Wedding Sheet Cake with Vanilla-Almond Buttercream

A tender white cake flavored with vanilla and almond, baked as two thin half-sheet layers, stacked, and frosted with a fluffy vanilla-almond buttercream. About 48 servings per cake; about 96 from two.

Directions

  1. Heat the oven to 350°F. Grease a 13x18-inch half-sheet pan, line it with parchment, and grease the parchment. Wrap the outside with damp cake strips if you have them.
  2. For one layer, use half the cake ingredients: in a bowl, whisk 3 cups flour, 1 tablespoon baking powder, 1/2 teaspoon baking soda, and 3/4 teaspoon salt.
  3. In a stand mixer, beat 2 sticks butter with 2 cups sugar on medium-high for 3 to 4 minutes, until pale and fluffy. Add 6 egg whites in three additions, beating well after each, then 2 teaspoons vanilla and 1 teaspoon almond extract.
  4. On low speed, add the flour mixture in three parts, alternating with 1 1/2 cups buttermilk in two parts, starting and ending with flour. Mix just until no streaks remain.
  5. Spread the batter evenly in the pan, all the way into the corners. Bake 22 to 28 minutes, until a toothpick in the center comes out clean and the top springs back.
  6. Cool in the pan for 15 minutes, then turn out onto a parchment-lined rack or board and cool completely. Repeat steps 2 through 6 for the second layer.
  7. Wrap each cooled layer in two layers of plastic wrap and one of foil. Refrigerate up to 2 days or freeze up to about 2 weeks. Thaw in the wrapping.
  8. For the buttercream, beat the butter until creamy, about 3 minutes. On low, add the powdered sugar a few cups at a time, then the vanilla, almond, salt, and enough cream to make it fluffy and spreadable. Beat on medium-high 2 to 3 minutes. Double batches are easiest made in two rounds.
  9. To assemble, set one layer on a 19x14-inch cake board, spread about 2 cups buttercream over it, and set the second layer on top, bottom side up for a flat top. Spread a thin crumb coat over the top and sides, chill 30 minutes, then finish with the rest of the buttercream and decorate.
  10. Keep the finished cake cool, covered, and out of direct sun. Serve within about a day of frosting.

Room-temperature egg whites and buttermilk matter here; cold ones make the batter curdle. If your layers dome, trim the tops flat with a long serrated knife once they're cool. For a 9x13 version, bake each half-sheet layer's batter in one 9x13 pan for about 30 to 35 minutes; two 9x13 layers make a two-layer cake for about 24. Buttercream softens fast in warm weather; for a hot day, keep the cake inside until just before the cutting.

Stacking, frosting, and keeping it simple

Do this the day before, in a cool kitchen, with the layers still chilled. Set the first layer on a sturdy 19 by 14 inch cake board, which leaves about half an inch of board around the cake, enough to lift it without putting a thumb in the frosting. If the layers domed, level them with a long serrated knife first.

Spread the filling, set the second layer on top upside down so its flat bottom becomes the top, and press gently. Then do a thin crumb coat over everything, chill for half an hour, and finish with a thicker coat. A long offset spatula covers the width of a half sheet in a single pass, which is most of the secret to a smooth top.

Then stop. The prettiest sheet cakes do one thing well. Pick one finish:

Swoops. Use the back of a spoon to make loose waves across the top. Forgiving, modern, and it hides almost anything.

A corner of flowers. A cluster of roses, a little greenery, and nothing else. Use flowers that are safe near food and grown without sprays, wrap the stems in floral tape or put them in a straw, and add them last.

A piped border and initials. A simple shell border around the edge and your initials or the date in one corner. Practice the letters on parchment first.

A few drops of pure almond extract go a long way in the buttercream. Taste as you add; almond can go from lovely to medicinal quickly.

Getting it to the party in one piece

A sheet cake is the easiest wedding cake to move, as long as it stays flat and cool. Slide the boarded cake into a half-sheet cake box, close it, and carry it level, on a flat floor of the car, never on a seat. A non-slip shelf liner underneath stops it from sliding. Air conditioning on, and no errands on the way.

At the party, keep it inside or in the coolest, shadiest spot until it's time to cut. Buttercream softens in warm weather and melts in direct sun, and a cake board on a hot table is a slow-motion disaster. If you're also planning the rest of the sweets, our wedding cupcakes post covers the same keep-it-cool rules for a tower.

Cutting and serving

The couple cuts the first slice from a corner, then a helper takes over. Lightly score the whole top into a grid of 2-inch squares with the tip of a long knife, then cut. Dip the knife in hot water and wipe it between cuts for clean edges, and use a small spatula to lift each square onto a plate. Cut the kitchen cake at the same time on a tray, so the line keeps moving.

The toasts and the cake cutting fit naturally together: glasses up, then the cake. Our guide to how much champagne for a wedding toast covers the pour, so both happen in one short, sweet stretch.

What to skip

Skip a single deep pan of batter in a home oven. Skip frosting warm layers. Skip decorating every inch; one good finish beats five. Skip non-food-safe flowers touching the frosting. Skip leaving the cake in the sun while people take photos of it. And skip baking it the morning of the wedding; this is a make-ahead cake on purpose.

A short sheet cake checklist

Count about one square per guest. Bake one thin layer per batch in a half-sheet pan, cool completely, wrap, and freeze. Frost the day before on a cake board, with a crumb coat and a short chill. Pick one finish. Box it, drive it flat, and keep it cool. Score a grid and cut with a hot, clean knife. Then eat the first piece, together.
