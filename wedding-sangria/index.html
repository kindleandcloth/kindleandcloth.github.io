Wedding sangria is a signature drink you make the night before: red wine, a little brandy and orange liqueur, juice, and sliced fruit, chilled overnight and topped with sparkling water at the station. One batch of the winter red sangria below pours about 24 glasses; a 2-gallon dispenser holds roughly one and a half batches.

The short version. Mix the wine, brandy, orange liqueur, orange juice, a little sugar, and the fruit the day before, and refrigerate it overnight. Add the sparkling water at the station, not in the fridge, so it stays lively. Serve it over ice in the glass, not with ice melting in the dispenser. One batch is about 24 five-ounce glasses; plan sangria as one choice on the bar, not the only drink. Make a labeled alcohol-free version so nobody has to ask.

Why sangria earns its place on a backyard bar

Picture a glass dispenser on a table by the porch, deep red and full of orange wheels, apple slices, and cranberries, a stack of glasses beside it and nobody waiting on a bartender. That's the whole pitch. Sangria looks festive, it's made ahead, it pours itself, and it stretches a few bottles of everyday red into something that feels like a choice you made on purpose.

Here's the honest part. A dispenser is still alcohol on a self-serve table, and sangria tastes lighter than it is, especially with brandy in it. It also goes flat if you add the bubbles too early, and watery if the ice melts in the jar. None of that is hard to fix. It just needs a plan, which is what follows.

If you're still building the whole bar, our guide to a budget wedding bar and drinks covers beer, wine, quantities, and who pours, and includes a batched Paloma if you want a second signature. For guests who aren't drinking, non-alcoholic wedding drinks has a full station plan; we also give an alcohol-free version of this sangria below.

How much sangria to make

A sangria glass is usually about five ounces with fruit and ice. One batch of the recipe below makes roughly 24 of those. A 2-gallon dispenser holds about one and a half batches, so for a bigger list, keep a second batch in the refrigerator in a pitcher and refill the dispenser as it drops, rather than filling a second dispenser that sits out and warms up.

How many batches depends on what else is on the bar. A common planning rule is about one drink per guest per hour across everything you're serving. If sangria is one of three choices (say beer, wine, and sangria), it might carry roughly a third of that. If it's the only cocktail at a short reception, it'll carry more. For a 50-person cocktail hour with beer and wine also available, two batches is a reasonable starting point. Write your guess down, then check it against the drinkers you actually know.

These are starting points. A hot afternoon, a long reception, or a crowd that loves wine will drink more. Leftover sangria (without the bubbles) keeps for about a day in the refrigerator, but the fruit gets soft, so don't overshoot too much.

Choosing the wine and the fruit

Use a dry, fruity, inexpensive red that you'd happily drink on its own. Spanish reds like garnacha or tempranillo are traditional, but any soft, unoaked red works. This is not the place for an expensive bottle, and it's also not the place for a wine you wouldn't drink. Sugar and fruit soften a wine; they don't fix a bad one.

For winter, lean on oranges, apples, and cranberries, with a cinnamon stick in the batch. Pears and pomegranate seeds are lovely too. In summer, swap in peaches, berries, and lemon. Cut fruit into pieces small enough to pour through a dispenser spigot, or keep large slices for the glasses and strain the batch into the dispenser. A sharp paring knife and a cutting board with a juice groove make short work of a dozen oranges without a puddle on the counter.

Fresh orange juice is a noticeable upgrade over the carton. A citrus press handles a few cups in minutes. Squeeze it the day you mix the batch.

Winter Red Sangria for a Crowd

Dry red wine with brandy, orange liqueur, fresh orange juice, apples, oranges, and cranberries, chilled overnight and lengthened with sparkling water at service. About 24 glasses per batch; about 48 doubled.

Directions

  1. In a large pitcher or food-safe container, stir the sugar into the orange juice until it dissolves.
  2. Add the wine, brandy, and orange liqueur, and stir well with a long spoon.
  3. Add the orange slices, diced apples, cranberries, and cinnamon sticks. Cover and refrigerate at least 4 hours, or overnight. Taste and add a little more sugar if it needs it.
  4. If the batch will sit longer than about a day, lift out the orange slices and cinnamon sticks so the pith and spice don't turn it bitter.
  5. At the station, pour the chilled sangria into the dispenser and stir in the sparkling water. Serve over ice in each glass, with a spoonful of fruit.
  6. Refill from the refrigerated reserve as the dispenser drops, adding sparkling water to each refill in the same ratio.

For an alcohol-free batch, replace the wine, brandy, and liqueur with 6 cups unsweetened cranberry-pomegranate juice and 3 cups cooled brewed black tea, keep the orange juice, fruit, and cinnamon, and top with sparkling water. Label both dispensers clearly. Serve the alcoholic batch only to adults of legal drinking age, and keep it out of reach of kids.

A make-ahead timeline

A week out: buy the wine, brandy, and liqueur, and do the batch math one more time with your final headcount. Freeze big ice cubes now and bag them, so the trays are free for another round. Clear a refrigerator shelf, or borrow a second fridge or a big cooler, because a few batches take up more room than you'd think.

The day before: squeeze the orange juice, slice and dice the fruit, and mix each batch in its own lidded pitcher or food-safe container. Label each one with the time you mixed it, and label the alcohol-free batch so nobody tops it up with wine by mistake. Taste one batch for sweetness and adjust them all the same way.

The morning of: chill the sparkling water, set up the station table, and wash the dispenser and the spigot. Run a little water through the spigot to make sure it doesn't drip, which is much better discovered at ten in the morning than at the toast.

Right before guests arrive: pour the first batch into the dispenser, add the sparkling water, set out the ice, glasses, spoon, and sign, and hand one person the job of refills.

Setting up the station

Put the glass dispenser on a sturdy, level table in the shade, with the spigot hanging over the edge so a glass fits under it. Pour the chilled base in right before guests arrive, then the sparkling water. Keep the reserve in a lidded glass pitcher in the refrigerator, not on the table in the sun.

Ice goes in the glass, not the dispenser. Ice melting in a jar for two hours waters down the last third of the batch. Make big cubes ahead in large ice cube trays; they melt slower than small ones. Keep a cooler of ice and a scoop next to the station, and ask someone to check it every half hour.

Shatterproof stemless glasses are the right call on grass, patio stone, and anywhere kids are running. They also stack, and they wash. Put a long bar spoon on a saucer by the dispenser for fishing fruit into glasses, and a small bowl for spent fruit. A short sign helps: "Winter sangria: red wine, brandy, orange, apple, cranberry." The alcohol-free version gets its own dispenser and its own sign, a few feet away.

The kit, all in one place: a 2-gallon glass dispenser for the station and a lidded glass pitcher for the reserve; a citrus press, a paring knife, and a grooved cutting board for the prep; a long bar spoon for stirring and serving fruit; large ice cube trays for slow-melting cubes; and shatterproof stemless glasses for the lawn.

Drinking responsibly at a self-serve station

A self-serve dispenser is convenient, and it also means nobody is watching how much anyone pours. Brandy-spiked sangria goes down easily. A few simple habits help: keep the glasses on the small side, put water and food right next to the station, and close the sangria before the end of the night, the same way you'd close a bar. Make sure the alcohol-free version is just as easy to find.

Keep the station out of reach of kids, and away from the kids' table, which our post on kids at a backyard wedding suggests putting on the other side of the yard anyway. If you're hosting at home, check whatever your local rules say about serving alcohol at a private event, and consider whether a hired bartender makes sense for a larger crowd; the bar guide goes into that.

Plan rides. A line on the website about rideshares or a shuttle, and a host who's watching the door at the end, does more than any sign.

What to pair with it

Sangria loves salty, savory food: cheese, cured meat, olives, toasted nuts, and bread. A cocktail hour of charcuterie cups is an easy match, and so is a small grazing table. For a winter dinner, it sits nicely next to the hearty, holdable mains in our winter wedding food ideas. Later in the evening, when the sangria closes, a hot cocoa bar takes over beautifully.

What to skip

Skip expensive wine; the fruit and sugar hide it. Skip adding sparkling water to the whole batch the night before; it'll be flat by the toast. Skip ice in the dispenser. Skip leaving the reserve in the sun. Skip a glass dispenser on a wobbly folding table on grass. And skip making sangria the only drink at a long reception; give people beer, wine, water, and something alcohol-free too.

A short sangria checklist

Mix the base the day before and chill it overnight. Add sparkling water at the station. Serve over big ice cubes in the glass, not in the jar. Keep the reserve cold in a pitcher, and refill as you go. Set up a labeled alcohol-free dispenser nearby. Put water and food beside it, keep it away from kids, and close the station before the end of the night. Then pour yourself one and go find your people.
