The short version. Toss peaches with sugar, a little starch, lemon, and cinnamon, put them in 9x13 pans, cover with a simple biscuit topping, and bake until the fruit bubbles and the top is browned. Serve warm, with ice cream scooped from a freezer if you offer it. Fresh, frozen, or canned peaches in juice will all work. This is not a pie bar, and it is not a cold make-ahead tray. It is a cobbler, in pans you can actually get into a home oven.

Top picks

  • Best fruit: whatever good peaches you can get — fresh, frozen, or canned in juice and drained — without apologizing for the season.
  • Best bake: fruit bubbling at the edges and a topping that is actually browned, not pale.
  • Best service: warm, not piping and not cold, with ice cream from a freezer, not a softening tub in the yard.
  • Useful kit: a deep 9x13 baker, a ceramic cobbler dish, an ice cream scoop, a long serving spoon, and a mixing bowl set — the tools this post is actually about, not a generic stand-in.

A warm tray dessert, not a pie bar

The useful purchases are the pans and the tools for a warm dessert, not a pastry shop. A deep 9x13 baker or a ceramic cobbler dish holds the fruit without boiling over, a mixing bowl and a pastry blender make the biscuit topping, a rimmed sheet under the pans will catch drips in the oven, a cinnamon shaker is enough spice, and a long spoon and a solid scoop will serve it without fishing in a hot pan with a teaspoon.

A cobbler is fruit, baked down a little, under a topping that is closer to a biscuit than to a pie crust. That is the whole idea, and it is why the dessert belongs at a backyard wedding. You can mix the topping in a bowl, you can use fruit that is not perfect, and you can bake in the same 9x13 pans you already use for a tray dinner. You do not have to roll a dozen crusts, and you do not have to frost anything at the last hour.

It is not a pie bar. A pie bar is a station of pies, with servers and a table plan. This is one dessert, in pans, spooned into bowls. It is also not a dump cake, and it is not a bakery cobbler you reheat and hope. It is a pan you bake until the fruit moves when you jostle it and the top is the color of a proper biscuit.

Two 9x13 pans will serve about twenty-four, if the scoops are generous and this is the main dessert. Four pans will serve about forty-eight. If you also have a cookie table or a cake, you can make less, because people will take a smaller spoonful. If this is the only sweet at the end of a barbecue, this scale is about right, because a warm cobbler disappears faster than a plated slice.

The oven is the constraint. Two pans can usually share a home oven; four pans may mean a second bake, a neighbor’s oven, or a staggered hold. Plan that before you buy fruit, not when the first two pans come out and the guest of honor is already asking about dessert. A cobbler will wait a little, loosely covered, in a warm place. It will not wait through a second, forgotten bake while you are supposed to be in a photograph.

Peaches, a biscuit topping, and pans you already have

Fresh peaches, when they are actually ripe, are the luxury version, and they should be peeled or not according to how you like to eat them. I usually peel them for a wedding pan, because a tray of skins is a distraction some guests do not want. Frozen peaches, thawed and drained, are an honest choice, and often the better one if you are getting married in a month when a peach is a hope and not a fruit. Canned peaches in juice, drained well, will also make a cobbler you can be proud of. Do not use peach pie filling from a can and call it the same dessert. You want fruit you can still recognize, not a gel.

The fruit needs a little sugar, a little starch so the juices thicken, a squeeze of lemon, and cinnamon if you like it. Taste the fruit. Sweet peaches need less sugar; pale ones need a bit more, and they still need the lemon. A light hand with cinnamon is enough. This is a peach cobbler, not a spice cake. Butter dotted on the fruit before the topping goes on is a small kindness, and it helps the juices feel finished.

The topping in the recipe below is a straightforward biscuit dough: flour, a little sugar, baking powder, salt, cold butter, and milk or buttermilk, mixed only until it comes together. A pastry blender or your fingers will do. You can drop it in spoons over the fruit, or pat it into rough rounds. Gaps are fine. The fruit should bubble up through them. A topping that is rolled thin like a lid will go pale and tough in the hold; a thicker, rougher topping will stay more like a biscuit.

Use a deep pan. A shallow 9x13 will boil over, and the sheet you put underneath is not a decoration — it is how you keep the oven floor from becoming a smoked-peach event in the middle of getting dressed. Butter the pans or do not, as you prefer; the fruit will stain either way. If you have a deeper ceramic dish you like, use it for one of the pans and accept that it may bake a few minutes longer than the metal one. Start checking when the top is browned and the juices are moving, not at a clock time you invented.

Wedding peach cobbler for a crowd

The recipe below is for two 9x13 pans, about twenty-four servings, or four pans if you double it. Mix the topping close to baking, so the baking powder is still awake. Serve the cobbler warm. If you offer ice cream, keep it frozen until you scoop it.

Wedding Peach Cobbler in 9x13 Pans

Peaches baked under a simple biscuit topping in 9x13 pans, until the fruit bubbles and the top is browned. Fresh, frozen, or well-drained canned peaches in juice all work. Two pans serve about 24; four pans, about 48. Serve warm. If you offer ice cream, scoop it from a freezer.

About 24 or 48 · Prep 30 minutes · Bake 40 to 50 minutes

  1. Heat the oven to 375°F. Butter two 9x13 pans (or four, if you are doubling) and set them on a rimmed baking sheet. In a large bowl, toss the peaches with the sugar, cornstarch or flour, lemon juice, cinnamon, and salt. Taste and add a little more sugar if the fruit is very tart. Divide the fruit between the pans and dot with the diced butter.
  2. In a second bowl, whisk the flour, sugar, baking powder, and salt. Cut in the cold butter until the mixture looks like coarse crumbs with a few larger pieces. Stir in the milk or buttermilk just until a soft dough forms. Drop the dough in spoonfuls over the fruit, leaving some gaps. Sprinkle with a little sugar if you like.
  3. Bake until the topping is well browned and the fruit is bubbling at the edges, 40 to 50 minutes, a little longer if the pans are very full or ceramic. Let the cobbler stand at least 15 minutes so the juices thicken. Serve warm, with a wide spoon. If you offer ice cream, scoop it from a freezer, not from a tub that has been sitting out. Leftovers keep, covered and chilled, and can be warmed gently. Do not leave a dairy topping or a melting tub of ice cream in the sun.

A warm hold, ice cream from the freezer, and a spoon

A cobbler should be served warm, which is a narrower window than a cold tray dessert, and a kinder one than “straight from the oven.” Straight from the oven, the fruit is lava and the topping is still setting. Stone cold, it tastes like a leftover. Pull the pans a little before you need them, let them stand, and hold them in a warm oven, turned low, or on the back of a stove that is still slightly warm, loosely covered with a foil tent so the top does not steam itself soft. A chafer is usually too much heat. A sunny dessert table is too little care.

If you offer ice cream, the ice cream is a separate job. Keep the tub in a freezer, or in a cooler packed like a freezer, and scoop to order, or scoop a tray of balls in advance and keep those frozen until service. A softening tub next to a warm cobbler looks generous and becomes soup. A heavy scoop and a pitcher of hot water to dip it in will keep the line moving. If you do not want that job, serve the cobbler alone, or with a jug of cream kept cold, and do not apologize. A good cobbler does not require a second dessert on top of it.

Set the pans on towels, with a long spoon, and stack small bowls where people can reach them. The first scoops will be the neatest. After that, the pan will look like a pan, which is fine. What is not fine is a dry corner of topping and a burnt edge because the pan sat over a burner, or a cobbler that went cold because dessert was announced an hour late. Give dessert a window, the way you would give a toast a window. The dessert-table guide can help you place the pans; this post will only say they need a little room, a spoon rest, and a sign if the cobbler has an obvious allergen — wheat in the topping, dairy if you served cream or ice cream, and fruit, which is obvious and still worth a word if you added berries or almond.

I would rather bake this the afternoon of the wedding, or the morning, and hold it warm for a short hour, than bake it the day before and try to bring a biscuit topping back to life. You can bake it earlier and warm it, loosely covered, in a moderate oven until the fruit is hot and the top has recovered a little. It will be good. It will not be quite as good as a cobbler that was baked closer to the meal. If the oven is already promised to a tray dinner, make the cobbler first, hold it, and give the oven back. The fruit will wait more patiently than a pan of rolls.

A short peach-cobbler checklist

Choose fruit you can stand behind, including frozen or canned if that is the honest peach. Toss it with enough sugar and a little starch, cover it with a rough biscuit topping, and bake until the juices bubble and the top is browned. Give the pans a rimmed sheet underneath, and give dessert a real window. Serve warm, with a wide spoon, and keep any ice cream frozen until it is scooped.

Do not call this a pie bar, and do not leave a warm dessert to fend for itself on a sunny table. A wedding peach cobbler is a tray dessert with a short, warm hold, and the hold is what makes it kind to serve. If you do that one job well — bubbling fruit, a browned top, a pan you can actually get out of the oven, a scoop that is still a scoop — people will eat it standing up and ask, later, if there is more. There should be more, in the second pan, still warm, still obviously peaches, which is a very good thing to be able to say at the end of a backyard meal, when the plates are already in the bin and someone is still holding a bowl because they were not quite finished, and you can point them back to the table and mean it.
