A Budget Wedding Bar and Drinks Plan You Can Actually Run

The short version: A backyard bar works when the drinks are simple, the ice is serious, and someone other than the two of you is in charge of pouring. Beer, wine, a lot of water, and one batched cocktail will serve most lists better than a long open-bar menu. Count the glasses, the cooling, and the shade, not just the bottles.

Three drink plans that stay generous

Best default

Beer, wine, a non-alcoholic option that isn't an afterthought, and water everywhere. It's easier to shop, easier to serve, and easier to keep cold.

Best if you want a signature pour

One batched cocktail in a drink dispenser, pre-mixed (minus any sparkling top-up) so a helper can ladle, not shake, all night.

Best for the food pairing

Match the bar to the menu, not to a fantasy hotel setup. See our budget wedding food ideas and, for a dinner in the house, how to host a small wedding dinner at home.

The drinks table is where a backyard wedding can feel abundant — and where a budget can quietly leak, one specialty bottle and a bag of late ice at a time. Here's the thing: guests remember being able to get a cold drink without a line, and they remember water when it's hot. They do not remember that you didn't hire a mixologist.

This is the bar plan that sits next to the food, not a second event. Keep it in the shade, keep it stocked, and give it a closer so you're not opening beers at midnight in your dress.

How much to buy, without a formula you have to worship

You do not need a specialty aisle of props. You need a short, honest list. A beverage dispensers, which is the actual tool this part of the day uses, not a generic stand-in, a stemless wine glasses, a beverage cooler, a bar tool, a cocktail napkin dispenser, for water, tea, or a non-alcoholic drink that should not live in a jug with no ladle, a ice bucket tongs stainless, a bar tool, and a ice bucket tongs stainless, which do more work on a buffet than a decorative serving set.

Every crowd is different, and a Saturday in July is not a Sunday in October. Still, you need a starting point so you're not shopping twice. A commonly used planning range for a multi-hour reception is about one drink per guest in the first hour, then fewer after that — often landing somewhere around two to four drinks per adult over a typical evening, with plenty of people drinking less, and a few drinking more. Build from your actual list: who is there, how long the party runs, and whether dinner is seated and unhurried or a shorter toast-and-cake.

Buy a little extra of the inexpensive things (water, soda, beer you can return unopened if the store allows it) and be tighter on the expensive bottles. Unopened alcohol is sometimes returnable; mixed leftovers are not. Check the store's policy before you count on it.

Non-drinkers and drivers are not a side note. A good zero-proof option — a flavored sparkling water, a batch of something that looks like a cocktail, iced tea, or a nice soda — should be as obvious as the wine. Put it in a pretty dispenser, not a lone can at the back of a cooler.

A long open bar in a backyard is usually more work than it's worth: more ice, more glassware, more chance of a sticky table in the dark, and a higher bill. If you go that way, keep the spirit list short and skip anything that needs a twelve-step garnish.

For the batch, pick a drink that can sit for an hour or two without falling apart — a spritz you top with bubbles at the station, a whiskey ginger, a punch, a big batch of palomas. Write the recipe on a card, including the non-alcoholic version. Taste it a week ahead at the same dilution you'll actually serve; pitcher drinks get strong on paper and weak once the ice melts, or the reverse.

Ice, shade, and the station itself

Ice is a line item, not a last-minute bag from the gas station. You'll need it for drinks, for cooling bottles, and often for the food station too. A working starting point many hosts use is something like a pound of ice per guest, more in real heat, split between coolers (for the bottles) and a separate supply for glasses. Don't make people fish a warm beer out of a puddle of meltwater; use a drink tub with ice for service and keep backup bags in a hard cooler with a drain or a freezer in the house.

Put the bar in the shade, on firm ground, with a way to get behind it. A 6- or 8-foot table, a runner or a tablecloth, a tub, a towel, a trash can, a recycling bin, and a place for empties — crates make a low back shelf for backups — will do more than a "bar cart" styled for photographs. If you're renting a high cocktail table for looks, still give the working bar a real table. Our rentals checklist is where that extra 6-foot table often belongs.

Keep water off the bar if you can — a second station, or carafes on the tables, so the drink table isn't the only place to deal with thirst. In heat, this is not optional.

Cans and bottles stay colder than a lonely bottle of white on a table. Nest whites and beers in ice. Reds can sit, but not in the sun. If you're serving a batched cocktail, nest the dispenser in a tub of ice or keep a backup pitcher in a cooler. A board for citrus and garnishes keeps the wet work off the pour.

Glassware, cups, and the washing question

Real glasses look better and feel better. They also need a rental count, a return bin, and someone willing to rinse. If you'd rather buy a set you'll use again, a sturdy stemless wine-glass set is a calmer backyard shape than tall stems in the grass. If you rent, match the glass to the drink list: a wine glass and a water glass for everyone is a different order than a full cocktail set. Add breakage and a few extras.

Compostable or sturdy recyclable cups are a reasonable choice at a more casual backyard, especially if you're not renting glassware. Avoid the thinnest plastic, which tips and blows. If you go this way, put a clearly marked bin next to the bar, or you'll spend Sunday picking cups out of a hedge.

Don't mix a "no glass on the lawn" rule with a tray of your good wine stems at dusk. Pick a policy and set the table to match it.

Count glassware from the drink list, not from a generic per-person formula. If you're only serving wine, water, and one batch cocktail, you may need a wine glass and a sturdier tumbler, not a full cocktail set. Add extras for breakage and for the helper who will otherwise start washing mid-toast.

Service: who pours, and how the line stays short

Self-serve can work for beer, wine, water, and a ladle-from-the-dispenser cocktail, as long as the station is obvious and restocked. It does not work if the ice is in a closed cooler under the table and only you know that.

A single hired bartender or a paid helper is often the best money you'll spend after the food, especially past about 40 guests. They keep the ice filled, the trash moving, and the two of you out of the weeds. Give them a simple menu, a start and end time, and a plan for what happens if someone has had enough.

Stagger service with the food. If dinner is announced and the bar is the only place to be, you'll get a line. Open the station before guests are thirsty, and keep table water filled so the first rush is softer. For a small seated dinner, you may not need a "bar" at all — bottles on a sideboard and wine on the table can be the whole plan, which is how a dinner at home often runs best.

Permits, neighbors, and ending the evening

Check whether your town restricts outdoor alcohol service, open containers, or a temporary event permit, especially if you're using a caterer with a license. A private home wedding is usually simpler than a public park, but it isn't a free-for-all. If you're hiring a bartender, ask about their licensing and insurance.

Neighbors hear clinking glass and a sudden cheer after a speech. Tell them the plan and the time you expect music and service to end. Stop pouring at a set hour, and shift to water and coffee if the night continues. That's kinder than an unofficial last call that never comes.

Have rides in mind. Put a number for a cab or a rideshare pin on a small card at the bar, and don't be shy about it. The goal is a generous table, not a complicated one.

A worked example, then go get a real count

Imagine a Saturday evening for about 50 adults, with a two-hour stretch of drinks before and during dinner, and a slightly shorter stretch after. One reasonable shop — not a rule, and not a quote — might be two cases of a lighter beer, a case of something darker or a cider, a case of white or sparkling, half a case of red, a batched cocktail that yields 40 to 50 small pours, a couple of cases of sparkling water, and more still water than you think, plus citrus and a bag of something salty at the station so people aren't drinking on empty stomachs. Then ice, in serious quantity, and a backup run already assigned to a friend if the afternoon is hot.

If half the list doesn't drink, or the party is a lunch, cut the alcohol first, not the water and the zero-proof option. If the party runs late and includes dancing, you may need more beer and more ice, not more kinds of amaro. Recalculate from the people who are actually coming.

Coffee and tea at the end of the night are a kindness, especially in cooler weather or after a long dinner. A borrowed urn, decent beans or tea, milk, and sugar on a small side table is enough. Don't make it a third bar that needs a staffer unless you already have one standing there.

Kids, if they're at the wedding, need their own obvious drinks — water, juice, something that isn't in the cocktail dispenser — and cups they can actually hold. Put those at the water station, not next to the wine.

A simple bar checklist

  • Choose the drink list and write a shopping quantity from your headcount and the length of the evening
  • Plan ice in two supplies: cooling and serving, with shade for the station
  • Rent or buy glassware to match the list, plus water service at the tables
  • Set a working table, tubs, towels, trash, and recycling, with a restock box in the house
  • Assign a bartender or a closer who is not either of you
  • Include a non-alcoholic option that looks as considered as the wine
  • Check permits, neighbor timing, and a ride plan
  • Return unopened bottles if you can, and deal with empties before Monday

One last practical note: label things. A small card for the batched cocktail (and the proof-free version), a sign for water, a marker on the trash versus recycling. Guests shouldn't have to ask whether the pretty pink pitcher is boozy. Labels also help your helper when you're in the middle of photos.

A batched Paloma, and a grapefruit drink without tequila

The bar guide already asks for one batched cocktail that can sit for an hour or two without falling apart. A Paloma is a good candidate: tequila, grapefruit, lime, a pinch of salt, and soda added in the glass so the bubbles stay alive. Batch the sour mix ahead, chill it, and top each drink at the station. Not everyone will want a cocktail — the amounts below are for 50 Paloma servings, not for 50 guests times two. If your list is mostly beer and wine, cut the batch in half.

Steps

  1. The morning of, or the night before, combine the tequila, grapefruit juice, lime juice, and a pinch of salt. Stir, taste, and add a little syrup only if the mixture is harsh. It should be bright, not candy-sweet. Chill it thoroughly, in pitchers or in a dispenser kept in the shade or on ice.
  2. Make the non-alcoholic batch the same way, without tequila, and label both containers clearly. Don't make people ask which is which.
  3. At the station, fill a glass with ice, pour about 5 ounces of the batch, and top with 2 or 3 ounces of cold soda. A salted rim is optional; a dish of salt and a lime wedge are enough for the people who want one. Don't add the soda to the whole dispenser, or the last guests will get a flat drink.
  4. Keep extra juice and soda cold. Taste the batch once after it has sat on ice — juice can taste duller as it chills, and you can adjust with a little lime. Ice, as this guide already says, is not the place to go short.

Make ahead

The tequila-and-juice mix can sit, covered and cold, overnight. Juice it the day before if you have the refrigerator space; otherwise, good not-from-concentrate grapefruit juice is an acceptable shortcut, with fresh lime doing the bright work. Cut the citrus in the morning, not at 5 p.m. A named person should pour, so the two of you are not stuck at the dispenser during the toasts.

A backyard bar is a hospitality station. Keep the menu short, the ice honest, and the water obvious. That's what feels generous — not a row of bottles nobody asked for.
