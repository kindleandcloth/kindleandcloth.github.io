A Wedding Sandwich Bar of Focaccia Slabs, Including a Roasted-Vegetable One

A wedding sandwich bar is focaccia or ciabatta slabs, filled and cut into squares: one chicken or Italian slab, and a labeled roasted-vegetable slab. It is a line people can walk through, not a deli counter you have to staff.

The short version. Buy or bake large slabs of focaccia or ciabatta, split them, fill them, and cut them into squares. Two slabs will feed about 24; four slabs, about 48. Keep a roasted-vegetable slab clearly labeled, with its own knife. This is not pulled pork — that recipe already lives in the backyard barbecue menu — and it is not a made-to-order sandwich station. A board and a sheet pan for roasting vegetables will do most of the work.

A bar you can walk through, not a deli counter

The failure mode of a sandwich bar is a kind person standing behind a table, building each sandwich while a line forms into the yard. The success is a set of slabs that were filled in a quiet kitchen, cut into squares, and set out with signs. People take a square, or two, and move. You can refill a board. You cannot clone a sandwich maker at 5 p.m.

Focaccia and ciabatta work because they are already slabs. A good bakery loaf, or a sheet you bake yourself if you already have a reliable recipe, can be split horizontally, filled, and pressed lightly so the filling stays in when you cut. Soft sandwich bread will collapse. A baguette is a different shape and a fussier cut. If you only have rolls, you can fill them ahead and arrange them, but the method below is for slabs, which are faster to portion and clearer on a board.

Two slabs, cut into modest squares, will feed about 24 as a main. Four slabs will feed about 48. If the sandwiches are part of a larger buffet, you can make less, but then the other food has to be real. If they are the main at a lunch or a casual dinner, do not underestimate how many squares disappear when people are standing and talking. A square should be a handful, not a quarter of a sheet.

This bar can live at a lunch reception, a casual dinner, or a hearty cocktail hour if dinner is later. It is a little heavy for a farewell brunch unless the morning is really a lunch. It is a natural partner to a simple salad, not to a second cooked-to-order station. A buffet setup still applies: plates first, food, an exit, and a place to stand.

Pulled pork is not the filling here, even though it is a popular sandwich. That recipe is already in the barbecue menu, and repeating it would be a second pulled-pork post. Chicken, Italian meats, or a generous vegetable slab will do this job without repeating that work. If your heart is set on pork, use the other guide, and skip this bar or run the vegetable slab only.

Two fillings, and a vegetable slab that is actually separate

Choose one meat or poultry slab and one vegetable slab, and stop. Three fillings means three boards, three knives, and a table that no longer looks simple. An Italian slab can be sliced meats and cheese, a spread, and something crisp or pickled — lettuce or a small amount of dressed greens, roasted peppers, or a relish that is not swimming. A chicken slab can be sliced roasted or poached chicken, a light mayonnaise or a pesto, and something with texture, not a dry pile of plain breast.

The vegetable slab should be a real sandwich, not an afterthought of raw cucumber. Roast a mix of vegetables on a sheet pan until they give up water and take on some color: peppers, zucchini, red onion, maybe eggplant or mushrooms. Cool them so they do not steam the bread. Layer them with cheese if you like, or without, and a spread that helps the filling cling. If the slab is meant to be vegetarian, keep meat off that board entirely, and label it where a guest can see it without asking. If it also needs to be without cheese, say that too, and do not put a cheese knife on that loaf.

Spread the cut sides of the bread so the filling has something to hold. A thin layer is enough; a thick smear will slide. If you want the slab to press, set a second pan or a cutting board on top for ten or twenty minutes, not an hour under a crate that flattens it into a pancake. Then cut with a long, sharp knife, wiping the blade when the filling starts to drag.

Hold assembled slabs cool if they contain mayonnaise, chicken, or cheese and you are more than a short time from service. A backyard in the sun is not a holding area for a dairy-and-meat loaf. Bring the cut squares out in waves if the hour is long, and keep the reserve in a cold place. This is closer to a labeled vegetarian main than to a tray you can leave out like a bowl of pretzels.

Bread can be bought. That is not a compromise; it is often the better wedding choice, because the filling and the cutting are already enough work. If you bake the focaccia, bake it ahead and be honest about your oven space. A runner and towels will catch crumbs either way.

Focaccia sandwich slabs

The recipe below is for two slabs or four, each about the size of a half-sheet or a large bakery focaccia. Two slabs are about 24 as a main; four, about 48. One filling is chicken or Italian; the other is roasted vegetable, labeled, with its own knife.

Focaccia Sandwich Slabs

Large focaccia or ciabatta slabs, split, filled, lightly pressed, and cut into squares. Make one chicken or Italian slab and one roasted-vegetable slab, and label the vegetable slab. Two slabs feed about 24 as a main; four slabs, about 48.

  1. Heat the oven to 425°F. Toss the vegetables with the oil, salt, and pepper, spread them on one or two sheet pans, and roast until they are tender and some edges are browned, 25 to 35 minutes. Cool completely so they will not steam the bread.
  2. Split each slab horizontally. Spread a thin layer of pesto, mayonnaise, or mustard on the cut sides. For the meat or poultry slab, layer the chicken or Italian meats, cheese if you are using it, and peppers or greens. For the vegetable slab, layer the roasted vegetables and cheese if that slab includes cheese. Keep meat off the vegetable board.
  3. Close the slabs and, if you like, set a second pan or a cutting board on top for 10 to 20 minutes so the filling settles. Do not flatten them hard. If the filling includes mayonnaise, chicken, or cheese, keep the assembled slabs cool until you are close to service.
  4. Cut into modest squares with a long knife, wiping the blade as needed. Arrange on a board, label the vegetable slab, and give it its own knife. Bring squares out in waves if the hour is long, and keep the reserve cold. Do not leave a dairy-and-meat slab in the sun.

Buy the bread if that is the calmer path; the filling and the cutting are already enough work. For a list of 24 as a main, make two slabs; for 48, make four. If one slab must be without cheese as well as without meat, assemble it that way and say so on the card. A simple salad beside the board is enough.

Cutting, labeling, and a table that can take crumbs

Cut close to service so the edges do not dry out, but not so late that you are slicing while people watch and wait. A quiet kitchen twenty minutes before the hour is the right time for the first board. Refill from the reserved, still-whole section of a slab if you can; a board of only end pieces looks like a leftover table.

Signs should name the filling in ordinary words: “roasted vegetable and provolone,” “chicken and pesto,” “Italian meats.” If there is a nut in the pesto, say so. If there is pork in the Italian slab, say so. If the vegetable slab is vegan, say so only when it is true. A pretty leaf on a skewer is not a label.

If you are feeding people who must avoid gluten, this bar is not their meal unless you have a truly separate, labeled gluten-free option made with its own bread, board, and knife. A square of focaccia with the filling scraped off is not a solution. It is kinder to have a different plate for that guest, planned in advance, than to improvise at the table.

Set the boards where crumbs can fall onto a cloth, not into a gift pile or a grass that you wanted clean. Keep a small bowl for used napkins, and a stack of plates if the squares are messy. People can eat a tidy square by hand; they cannot eat a dripping one while holding a glass and a program. If the hour is outdoors, keep the reserve inside.

This is a generous, informal main, closer to a walk-through lunch than to a plated family-style dinner. It can still be enough of a meal if the squares are substantial and the salad is real. What it cannot be is an afterthought of grocery wraps dumped from a plastic tray at the last minute, unless that is honestly all you can do, in which case skip the “bar” language and just feed people.

A short sandwich-bar checklist

A day or two ahead, order or buy the slabs, and shop for vegetables, fillings, and a spread. Decide which slab is vegetarian, and whether it is also without cheese. Roast the vegetables ahead and cool them.

The day of, split, fill, and press the slabs, and keep them cold if they need it. Cut the first board close to service, put the signs and the second knife down, and refill in waves.

When the hour ends, get leftover filled bread into the refrigerator, or send it home with people who can keep it cold. Soak the boards and the pans. A sandwich bar is a backyard main when you treat it as slabs with a plan, not as a deli line. Two fillings, two slabs or four, and a labeled vegetable option will do the whole job, and they are enough. Give the cutting to one person with a clear board, and let the rest of the hour be salad, drinks, and conversation. A sandwich bar should feel like a table you can walk up to, take something good, and leave, not like a project that is still being built as guests arrive. If you can get the slabs filled before you get dressed, you will be glad. The cutting can wait until the last quiet twenty minutes; the filling should not. That split in the work is the whole difference between a bar and a line, and it is worth protecting on the day. The board can wait; the quiet kitchen cannot be invented later.
