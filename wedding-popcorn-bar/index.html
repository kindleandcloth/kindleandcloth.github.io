A popcorn bar is the cheapest crowd-pleaser at a wedding: a few big jars, a stack of paper bags, three flavors, and a sign. It works at cocktail hour, it saves the late-night crowd, and the bags go home as favors. Plan about 2 cups of popped corn per guest, pop it the day before in batches, and fill the jars only an hour before the doors open.

The short version. Plan about 1 cup of popped popcorn per guest for a cocktail-hour-only bar, 2 cups when it runs all evening next to dinner and dessert, and up to 3 when it's the main late-night snack. One pound of kernels makes roughly 60 to 70 cups. Offer three or four flavors: one salty, one sweet, one savory, maybe one seasonal. Pop in batches a day ahead, store it airtight, and set it out in lidded jars with scoops and paper bags. A rented or bought machine is for show and for small refills, not for feeding 150 people from scratch.

Why a popcorn bar works at a backyard wedding

Picture the hour after dinner. The tables have been cleared, the string lights are doing their job, and somebody's uncle has discovered the dance floor. Then the smell of fresh popcorn drifts across the yard, and a line forms at a little table with glass jars, striped bags, and a chalkboard sign. Nobody needed a second dessert. Everybody wants a bag of kettle corn.

That's the case for popcorn. It's cheap per guest, it's familiar to everyone from your grandmother to the flower girl, and it doesn't need to be kept hot or cold. It's naturally gluten-free and vegetarian when you keep the toppings simple. It fills the gap between dinner and the late-night food, or replaces the late-night food entirely at a smaller party.

Here's the turn. Popcorn is easy for ten people and surprisingly bulky for a hundred. Two hundred cups of popcorn is several big contract bags, a lot of oil smoke in somebody's kitchen, and a real staling problem if it sits uncovered in humid evening air. A good bar is mostly a plan: how much, when to pop it, how to keep it crisp, and who refills the jars.

How much popcorn you need

Popcorn guides from wedding planners and popcorn makers mostly land between 1 1/2 and 3 cups of popped corn per guest, depending on what else is out. Popcorn is light, and people graze, so the time the bar is open matters as much as the head count.

Kernel yield varies by variety; most yellow popcorn pops to roughly 60 to 70 cups per pound, and the big "mushroom" kernels used for caramel corn pop a little smaller. Buy a few extra pounds. A 6-pound bag of yellow popcorn kernels keeps for months in a sealed container, so leftover kernels aren't a waste.

Count bags and cones the same way: one per guest, plus about 20 percent, because people come back and kids start over with a fresh one.

Pop it yourself, or rent a machine

There are three honest ways to get the popcorn, and most weddings end up combining two of them.

Our pick for most backyard weddings is to pop the bulk of it at home the day before, then run a small machine at the party for show and for fresh refills. The machine does the thing a jar can't: the sound, the smell, the little crowd that gathers. A home-style 8-ounce kettle machine is fine for that job, and pre-measured 8-ounce kernel packs, with the oil and salt already portioned, mean whoever is running it never has to measure in the dark.

Two practical notes on machines outdoors. First, power: kettle machines typically draw somewhere around 800 to 900 watts, so check the label and put it on its own outdoor-rated circuit, not on the same line as the coffee urn and the DJ. Our backyard wedding power plan walks through the math. Second, wind and safety: keep the machine on a sturdy table out of the walkway, cords taped down, and away from little hands, because the kettle and the glass get hot. Somebody has to own it, empty the unpopped kernels, and turn it off at the end of the night.

Kettle corn for a crowd

Kettle corn is the flavor that disappears first at almost every popcorn bar: sweet, salty, and crunchy, with a thin sugar shell that keeps better than butter. It's made in a covered pot or a stovetop popper with the sugar added right at the start, which is why you have to keep the kernels moving. The recipe is scaled for about 24 guests at 2 cups each; the second column doubles it for about 48.

Sweet and Salty Kettle Corn for a Wedding Crowd

Stovetop kettle corn made in batches in a covered pot or crank popper, with coconut oil, granulated sugar, and fine salt. About 48 cups in three batches for about 24 guests; about 96 cups in six batches for about 48.

Directions

  1. Set up the landing zone. Line two or three rimmed sheet pans with parchment, and set them next to the stove. Measure each batch's kernels, sugar, and salt into separate small bowls before you start, because there's no time once the corn starts popping.
  2. Heat the oil. Put 1/4 cup oil and 3 kernels in a large heavy pot with a lid, or a crank popper, over medium-high heat. When all three test kernels pop, the oil is ready.
  3. Add the kernels and sugar together. Add 1/2 cup kernels and 1/4 cup sugar, stir once to coat, and cover. Shake the pot constantly, or crank the popper without stopping, so the sugar doesn't settle and burn.
  4. Listen. Popping starts in about a minute and gets frantic. When the pops slow to two or three seconds apart, take the pot off the heat immediately. Waiting for the last kernel is how kettle corn burns.
  5. Salt and spread. Pour it straight onto a lined sheet pan, sprinkle with about 1 teaspoon fine salt, and toss with two spatulas. Break up big clumps while it's warm; it hardens as it cools.
  6. Repeat. Wipe out any scorched sugar between batches with a dry paper towel (carefully; the pot is very hot) so it doesn't flavor the next batch.
  7. Cool and store. Let each batch cool completely, about 15 minutes, pick out unpopped kernels, and store airtight.

Make-ahead: kettle corn is best the day it's made and still good the next day if it's cooled completely and stored in airtight bags or lidded containers at room temperature, away from the dishwasher steam and the refrigerator. Don't refrigerate popcorn; it goes soft. If the day is humid, set out smaller amounts and keep refilling from sealed bags. Popcorn is a choking hazard for very young children; many pediatric guidelines advise against it for children under about age 4, so tell the parents and keep a different snack for toddlers. Use a dedicated scoop for each jar, and keep nut or candy mix-ins in their own labeled jar.

Coconut oil is the classic kettle-corn fat because it handles high heat and tastes clean. If you're running a machine too, a jug of popcorn popping oil covers a whole wedding's worth of batches; check the label for allergens before you set out an ingredient card. For the plain butter popcorn, pop with the same oil, then drizzle with melted butter and fine salt right before it goes in the jar; butter is what makes popcorn go soft, so butter it last.

Three or four flavors, not twelve

A long row of flavors looks generous in a photograph and slows the line in real life. Three or four is the right number for most weddings:

  • One salty: butter and sea salt, the crowd-pleaser for people who don't want sweet.
  • One sweet: kettle corn (the recipe above) or a caramel corn.
  • One savory: white cheddar, ranch, or garlic parmesan. A popcorn seasoning variety pack turns plain popcorn into three different flavors with shakers, no extra batches.
  • One seasonal, if you want it: cinnamon sugar for fall, white chocolate and crushed peppermint for a December wedding, or lime and chili salt for summer.

Set out a couple of seasoning shakers on the table for guests who want to doctor their bag, and leave it there. Melted chocolate fountains, pumps of liquid butter, and bowls of loose candy are where a popcorn bar turns sticky and attracts every bee in the yard.

Jars, scoops, bags, and a sign

Use tall glass jars with lids or lidded bins, not open bowls. The lid is the difference between crisp popcorn at 9 p.m. and chewy popcorn at 9 p.m., especially outdoors. Give each jar its own popcorn scoop so nobody reaches in with their hands, and set the jars at slightly different heights on crates or cake stands so the bar reads from across the yard.

For serving, flat-bottom paper popcorn bags are cheap, stand up on their own, and photograph well; paper cones look prettier but tip over unless you have a holder. Put the bags at the start of the table and the napkins at the end. If the bags double as favors, stamp them, or tie them with a tag that says "for the ride home."

A sign tells people what's in each jar and that it's for them. Keep it short: the flavors, a note about nuts if you have any, and "help yourself." When and where to open it

Cocktail hour is the classic slot, while you're off taking photos and guests have time and an empty hand. A cocktail-hour-only bar needs about a cup per guest.

Late night is where popcorn really shines, after dinner when the dancing starts and before anyone gets hungry again. Set it near the dance floor or the exit, and it doubles as the send-off snack. Pair it with one warm item if the party runs late; our late-night wedding snacks guide has the timing, and the wedding pretzel bar is a natural warm partner.

For the kids, a popcorn bar is basically entertainment. If you're planning a kids' table or a movie corner, popcorn is the obvious anchor; see kids at a backyard wedding for the rest of that plan, and remember the toddler rule above.

Wherever it goes, keep it out of direct sun and away from the bar's ice tubs, which make the air around them damp. Assign one person to check and refill the jars every half hour from sealed bags, and to put the lids back on.

What it costs

Popcorn is one of the few wedding foods where the math is almost entirely in your favor. Kernels, oil, sugar, and salt for 100 guests cost less than a single tray of passed appetizers. The real costs are the things you only buy once: jars, scoops, bags, and possibly a machine. Jars and scoops come back out for every birthday party after; a machine is a fun purchase if you'll use it again and a rental if you won't. We won't quote prices here, because they change. Compare a machine rental quote from a local party company with the cost of buying one before you decide.

What to skip

Skip popping everything on site for a big crowd. Skip open bowls outdoors. Skip butter until the last minute. Skip a dozen flavors and a candy buffet next to it. Skip popcorn for toddlers. And skip filling the jars at 3 p.m. for a 9 p.m. bar; an hour before is plenty, with sealed refills behind the table.

Wedding popcorn bar: quick answers

How much popcorn do I need for 100 wedding guests?

About 200 cups of popped popcorn if the bar is open all evening alongside dessert, which is roughly 3 pounds of kernels. Plan about 100 cups for a cocktail-hour-only bar and up to 300 cups if popcorn is the main late-night snack.

Can I make popcorn the day before the wedding?

Yes. Pop it in batches, let it cool completely, and store it airtight at room temperature. Kettle corn and seasoned popcorn hold well for a day; add any melted butter just before serving.

How many flavors should a wedding popcorn bar have?

Three or four: one salty, one sweet, one savory, and one seasonal if you like. More than that slows the line without making anyone happier.

A short popcorn bar checklist

Plan about 2 cups per guest for an all-evening bar. Buy kernels at roughly 60 to 70 cups per pound, plus extra. Pop the bulk a day ahead in batches, cool, and seal. Choose three or four flavors. Set out lidded jars, a scoop for each, paper bags, and a short sign. Run a small machine for the smell if you want one, on its own circuit. Refill from sealed bags every half hour. Then send everyone home with a bag.
