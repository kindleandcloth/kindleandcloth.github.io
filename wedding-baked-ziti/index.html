Baked Ziti for a Wedding Crowd (Make-Ahead Pans That Hold)

Baked ziti is a wedding dinner you can assemble ahead, bake in 9x13 pans, and hold hot. It is a tray meal, not a pasta-bar station: no pot of boiling water at the party, a clearly labeled vegetarian pan if you need one, and sausage kept in its own dishes if you use it.

The short version. Cook the ziti shy of done, fold it with a simple tomato sauce and ricotta, cover it with mozzarella, and bake it in 9x13 pans until the center is hot. Two pans feed about 24; four pans feed about 48. You can assemble them the day before and bake them the day of. Hold at or above 140°F, and reheat leftovers to 165°F. If you want meat, brown sausage and keep it in its own labeled pans, not down one side of the only tray. This is the make-ahead dinner the pasta-bar guide already points to, written out as a recipe and a holding plan.

Top picks

  • Best for most backyard lists: two or four pans of cheese ziti, a salad, and bread, with a thermometer and a way to keep the pans hot.
  • Best if some people want meat: the same ziti, plus separate sausage pans, each labeled, each with its own spoon.

A tray dinner, not a pasta station

The useful purchases for this are ordinary, and they should match the work. A instant thermometer, so you are not guessing at 165°F or at a hold above 140°F, a pyrex 9x13, a lodge dutch oven, a macaroni and cheese pan deep, a lasagna pan deep ceramic, which is the actual tool this part of the day uses, not a generic stand-in, a ziti baking pan, a hotel pan hotel, and a pie server, so slices come off cleanly, instead of with the same dinner knife you just used on a nut pie.

A pasta bar asks you to keep water hot, sauces moving, and a line from stalling. Baked ziti asks you to fill pans and put them in the oven. That is why it belongs at a backyard wedding where the kitchen is also the house, and why it is a poor idea to try to do both on the same afternoon. Pick the tray. The pasta-bar guide already says so. This post is the tray.

What you get, if you do it well, is a familiar, holdable main that children and adults will eat without a speech about the concept. What you get if you do it badly is a dry, pale corner of pasta, a watery center, or a single pan with sausage mixed through it and a vegetarian guest staring at the spoon. The fixes are ordinary: enough sauce, pasta that still has a little bite when it goes into the pan, cheese that actually covers the top, and honest labels.

Two 9x13 pans of the recipe below will feed about 24 as a main, with salad and bread; four pans will feed about 48. If ziti is one of two mains, you can make less, but leftover baked pasta is one of the more useful leftovers a wedding can produce. A pan that looks a little generous in the afternoon looks correct when people come back for a second square after the speeches.

The sauce can be a good jarred tomato sauce you have tasted and seasoned, or a pot you already know how to make. This is not the place to debut a four-hour gravy unless that is a thing you already do calmly. The ricotta should be whole-milk if you can get it. The mozzarella should be grated or torn, not a single damp ball dropped in the middle. There are no eggs in this version, so you are not building a custard; you are still going to bake or reheat the pan to a safe 165°F in the center if it has been sitting cold, because it is a deep, mixed casserole.

How to assemble, bake, and hold it

Boil the ziti in well-salted water until it is a minute or two shy of the package time. It will keep cooking in the sauce and in the oven. Drain it, and do not rinse it. Toss it with enough sauce that every piece is coated and there is still sauce in the bowl; a dry mix will bake into a brick. Dollop the ricotta in layers rather than stirring it into a uniform pink paste, so people get pockets of cheese. Finish with mozzarella and a handful of Parmesan, so the top browns.

You can assemble the pans in the morning, or the day before, and refrigerate them covered. A cold pan needs more time. Bake at 375°F, covered for the first part so the pasta does not dry, then uncovered so the cheese can brown, until a thermometer in the center reads at least 165°F and the edges bubble. Rest ten minutes if you are cutting squares. To hold, cover and keep the pans at or above 140°F in a chafing dish or a 200°F oven. If the top looks dry, a spoon of warm sauce on a serving will help more than a long stay with the lid off.

If you want sausage, brown it separately, drain it, and build dedicated pans: sauce, pasta, ricotta, sausage, mozzarella. Label them. Do not try to make “half a pan” with an imaginary line down the middle. Vegetarian guests, and guests who eat meat but do not want to guess, deserve a clear dish and a clean spoon. The same rule applies if you add a vegetable — spinach, squeezed very dry, or roasted peppers — in some of the pans: say so on the card.

Make-Ahead Baked Ziti for a Wedding Crowd

Ziti folded with tomato sauce and ricotta, baked under mozzarella in 9x13 pans. The smaller batch is two pans for about 24; the larger is four pans for about 48. Sausage, if you use it, goes in its own labeled pans. Hold at or above 140°F; reheat leftovers to 165°F.

Two 9x13 pans (about 24) or four pans (about 48) · Prep 35 minutes · Bake 30 to 45 minutes · Total about 1 hour 15 minutes, plus holding

  1. Heat the oven to 375°F. Oil or butter two 9x13-inch dishes (four for the larger batch). Bring a large pot of salted water to a boil and cook the ziti until it is just shy of al dente, 1 to 2 minutes less than the package suggests. Drain; do not rinse.
  2. If you are using a jarred sauce and want a little more depth, cook the garlic in the olive oil over medium heat for a minute, then stir it into the sauce. Taste the sauce and add salt if it needs it. It should taste like something you would eat on pasta by itself.
  3. Toss the drained ziti with most of the sauce, holding back about 1 1/2 cups (3 cups for the larger batch) for the bottoms and tops of the pans. The pasta should be well coated, with a little sauce left in the bowl.
  4. Spoon a thin layer of the reserved sauce into each pan. Add a layer of sauced ziti, then dollop half of the ricotta over it, and scatter a third of the mozzarella and a little Parmesan. Repeat with the remaining ziti, the rest of the ricotta, more mozzarella, and a last layer of sauce, mozzarella, and Parmesan. If you are making sausage pans, add the browned sausage in an even layer with the ricotta, and keep those pans separate.
  5. Cover with foil, tented so it does not stick to the cheese, and bake 20 minutes (longer if the pans are cold). Uncover and bake until the top is browned in spots and a thermometer in the center reads at least 165°F, 10 to 20 minutes more. Rest 10 minutes. Hold, covered, at or above 140°F. Reheat leftovers to 165°F. Scatter basil or parsley on servings if you have it, not on a pan that will sit.

A cold, assembled pan can take 45 minutes or more to bake through. Trust the thermometer, not the clock. For the larger batch, use two pots to boil the pasta, or cook it in two rounds. Do not put sausage in a pan you also need to be vegetarian.

What to serve with it, and how the table should work

A green salad, a vinaigrette, and bread will do as much as any second casserole. If you want a vegetable in addition, roast a pan of something on a half-sheet — broccoli, peppers, or a simple mix — and keep it in its own dish. Parmesan at the table is welcome. A second red sauce in a small pitcher helps the first, slightly drier servings. You do not need a three-pasta menu. One baked ziti is a dinner.

Set the pans where people can reach them without blocking a door, with a spatula in each, labels on the rims, and towels for the inevitable drip. If you are outside, treat this like any other hot main: out of the wind, with a person watching the heat. If you are inside, protect the table from a pan that is actually hot. Tell people the vegetarian pans from the sausage pans as if it mattered, because it does.

Leftovers should be cooled and stored, not left in a chafing dish into the night. Send some home only if they have been held safely and will be reheated to 165°F. The rest can feed the house the next day, which is one of the quiet virtues of a tray of ziti: the wedding is over, and breakfast can be a square of pasta in a quiet kitchen. That is a better ending than a station you were still boiling water for while people left.

A short baked-ziti checklist

The day before, assemble the pans, label them, and refrigerate them. Make or taste the sauce. Wash the salad greens. Count the spatulas and the bread. The day of, bake the pans until the center is 165°F, then hold them at or above 140°F. Toss the salad close to service. Put a person at the table for the first wave.

After people have eaten, cover what is left and get it somewhere cold, or serve a last round while it is still safely hot. Do not invent a late-night pasta station from the remains. Baked ziti is a complete wedding dinner if the pans are hot, the labels are true, and you did the work when the kitchen was still yours. That is the advantage of a tray, and it is enough. If you are deciding between this and a pasta bar, choose this whenever the house is also the venue and the cook is also a guest. A mac and cheese bar or a vegetarian cottage pie can sit beside a labeled meatless pan if you need more than one main. A station that needs boiling water will pull someone out of the ceremony, or out of a toast, at the wrong time. A pan that is already baked will wait. If you want a second texture on the table, add the salad and the bread, not a second method of cooking pasta in the same hour. One good tray, served hot, will do more for the afternoon than a more ambitious idea you cannot staff, and it will still taste like a meal the next day, which is not a small or unimportant thing in a quiet house that has just been full of a wedding the night before, when you are finally hungry again for something simple.
