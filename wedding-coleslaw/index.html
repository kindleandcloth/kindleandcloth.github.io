The short version. Shred green cabbage and carrot, toss them with a dressing of cider vinegar, oil, a little sugar, and salt, and let the bowl sit, cold, so the cabbage softens and the dressing settles. Top picks

  • Best make-ahead: dress it a few hours ahead, or the night before, and toss it again at service.
  • Best cut: a slicer or a sharp knife, or a coarse grater for the carrots, into a thin shred that will hold, not a mince.
  • Best bowl: an extra-large bowl and a large bowl you can cover for the chill.
  • Useful kit: a salad spinner if the cabbage is wet, a dressing jar, tongs, and a sturdy board.

A slaw that can sit, not another mayonnaise salad

The useful purchases are cutting and holding tools. A slicer or a coarse grater, used carefully, will get a wedding amount of cabbage and carrot into a bowl without taking the whole morning. An extra-large mixing bowl and a large bowl you can cover hold the toss and the chill. A jar mixes the dressing; tongs serve it. A spinner helps if the cabbage is wet, and a sturdy board gives you a place to work. That is the kit this post is actually about.

Coleslaw is the side a backyard barbecue or a tray of fried chicken already assumes, and it is the side almost no one writes down as a recipe until they are staring at two heads of cabbage the night before. It should be bright, cold, and still crunchy enough to feel like a salad, not a sweet, wet heap. A vinegar dressing can sit. That is why this post uses one. A mayonnaise slaw can be excellent, and it is a different hold: it belongs in the refrigerator, or in a small bowl you refill, the way potato salad does, and it should be labeled. If you want that slaw, make it, and keep it cold. The recipe below is the one that can ride out a long buffet with less drama, beside a hot tray of meat.

It is not potato salad, and it is not macaroni salad. If you already have one of those on the table, you may still want this, because a vinegar slaw does a different job: it cuts the richness of a smoked or fried main, and it does not read as a second creamy bowl. If the table is already three pale salads, skip one of them. About four pounds of cabbage, dressed, will serve about twenty-four as a side. About eight pounds will serve about forty-eight. That is a generous scoop, not a garnish. If this is one of several sides, you can make less. If it is the only cool, sharp thing beside barbecue, this scale is about right.

People will ask how much slaw for a hundred, and the honest answer is that a hundred is not a second arithmetic, it is a bigger bowl and a colder hold. Four pounds for twenty-four is a useful unit; you can scale it. What you cannot do is shred cabbage in a teacup, underdress it, and hope a store bag of mix will look like a wedding side once it has sat in a warm bowl. Buy more cabbage than you think, because cores and outer leaves come off, and because a tight head is not all usable shred.

A thin shred, a sharp dressing, and time in the bowl

Green cabbage is the default. It holds. A little purple cabbage is pretty and will color the dressing; use it if you like the look, and know the bowl will turn pink. Carrot is the usual sweetness and color. A little thin-sliced onion or scallion is optional and should be modest, so a scoop is not a mouthful of raw onion. Core the cabbage, slice it thin, or use a slicer you are actually comfortable with — a mandoline is fast and is also a trip to the bandage box if you rush. A sharp knife is enough. Carrots can be grated coarse or cut into thin matchsticks. The shred should look like slaw, not like confetti and not like steaks of cabbage.

If the cabbage is wet from a rinse, spin it or dry it, or the dressing will go thin. Toss the vegetables in a bowl large enough that you can turn them without landing half the salad on the counter. The dressing is cider vinegar, a neutral oil or a light olive oil, a little sugar, salt, and black pepper. Mustard or celery seed is optional and traditional in some bowls. Whisk it in a jar and taste it: it should be sharp, slightly sweet, and salty enough to season a pile of raw cabbage, which is a bland vegetable until you dress it. Pour, toss, and wait.

Time is an ingredient. A vinegar slaw dressed and eaten at once can taste like a salad you have not finished making. An hour or two in the refrigerator, or overnight, lets the cabbage soften slightly and take on the dressing. It will also give off liquid. That is normal. Toss it again before service, and if the bowl looks soupy, lift the slaw with tongs into a serving bowl and leave the extra puddle behind, or pour a little off. Do not keep adding sugar to fix a watery bowl. Fix the liquid, then taste for salt and vinegar again, cold, because a dressing that tasted right at the counter can taste shy after a chill.

Keep it cold. A vinegar slaw is more forgiving than a mayonnaise salad, and it is still a cut vegetable that has been sitting. A cool bowl, a reserve in the refrigerator, and a refill habit will keep it crunchy. Do not park the entire eight pounds on a sunny buffet for three hours and call it rustic. Put out a modest bowl, nested over ice if the day is hot, and toss it when you refill.

Wedding coleslaw for a crowd

The recipe below is for about four pounds of cabbage, about twenty-four as a side, or about eight pounds if you double it. Make it ahead, taste it cold, and toss it again at service. Keep the reserve in the refrigerator.

Wedding Coleslaw

Thinly shredded green cabbage and carrot, tossed with a cider-vinegar dressing and left to sit, cold, so the cabbage softens and the dressing settles. About 4 pounds of cabbage serve about 24 as a side; about 8 pounds, about 48. Toss again before serving, and keep the bowl cool.

Directions

  1. Core the cabbage and shred it thinly. Grate or cut the carrots. If you are using onion or scallion, slice it thin. If the cabbage is wet, dry it well.
  2. In a jar, combine the vinegar, oil, sugar, salt, pepper, and the mustard or celery seed if you are using them. Shake until the sugar and salt dissolve. Taste: it should be sharp and a little sweet, and salty enough to season a pile of raw cabbage.
  3. Put the cabbage and carrot, and the onion if you are using it, in a very large bowl. Pour on the dressing and toss until every shred is coated. Cover and refrigerate at least an hour, or overnight.
  4. Toss again before service. If a puddle has collected, lift the slaw into a serving bowl or pour a little of the liquid off, then taste for salt and vinegar, cold. Serve cool, from a bowl you refill, with tongs.

Double every amount for 8 pounds of cabbage. A mayonnaise slaw is a different recipe and should be kept fully cold, like a potato salad, and labeled if you serve it. Do not leave a large bowl of cut vegetables in the sun for the length of a reception.

A cool bowl, a label, and what not to do

Label it in ordinary words: coleslaw, vinegar dressing, cabbage and carrot. If you added onion, mustard, or celery seed, you can say so. If you also make a mayonnaise version, label that one even more clearly, and do not put them in matching unlabeled bowls. People who need to know will not always ask.

Do not dress a bag of pre-shredded mix three days early and expect it to taste like a plan. Do not hide a wilted bowl under extra sugar. Do not use a red-wine vinegar you have not tasted in this dressing unless you already like that result. Do not put the only tongs at the other end of the table. And do not treat this as a garnish in a tiny cup if it is meant to be the cool side for a plate of barbecue; give it a real bowl.

Leftovers, if they have stayed cold, can go home in a lidded container, as the leftover-food guide describes. A slaw that has sat out in the heat should not be packed as a gift. Be slightly ruthless. Tomorrow you will be glad.

If you need to work ahead of a busy morning, you can shred the cabbage and carrot the day before, keep them dry in a lidded container, and dress them the next day with enough time for the rest. What you should not do is dress the slaw three days out and expect the same crunch. The rest is a few hours, or one night, not a long project in the back of the refrigerator. Taste it the day you serve it. Cabbage will keep taking on salt and losing water, and a bowl that was perfect on Friday night may need a spoon of vinegar and a pinch of salt on Saturday afternoon, or a little liquid poured off, or both.

Serve it with tongs, in a bowl that looks like a side, next to the hot things, not in a tiny cup at the far end of the table. People eat more slaw than hosts expect when the meat is rich, and they should be able to find it without a hunt.

A short coleslaw checklist

Shred the cabbage thin, dry it, and dress it with a sharp cider-vinegar dressing. Let it sit, cold, then toss again and pour off any extra liquid. About four pounds of cabbage serve twenty-four; about eight, forty-eight. Keep a reserve in the refrigerator, serve from a bowl you refill, and label it.

Coleslaw is a humble wedding side, and it is one of the ones people actually eat. If you treat the shred, the dressing, and the rest as part of the recipe, it will taste like you meant it, and it will still have a crunch at the last plate. If you treat it as a bag you dump at the last minute, it will taste like a bag you dumped at the last minute. That work is ordinary, and it is worth doing a few hours early, while the cabbage can still take on the dressing, instead of discovering a dry heap when the meat is already on the table and the only thing left to do is hope a last-minute pour of vinegar will do the work time in the bowl was meant to do.
