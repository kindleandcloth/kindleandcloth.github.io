The short version. Cube slightly stale bread, soak it in a custard of eggs, milk, cream, sugar, and vanilla, and bake it in 9x13 pans until the center is set and the top is browned. Top picks

  • Best make-ahead: assemble in the pans, refrigerate a few hours or overnight, and bake the day of, or bake ahead and reheat gently.
  • Best pans: ceramic 9x13 bakers that can go from refrigerator to oven, or metal 9x13 pans you already have.
  • Best hold: a chafing dish or a low oven, and an insulated carrier if you have to travel.
  • Useful kit: a serrated bread knife, a large measuring pitcher, a whisk, a sauce pitcher if you make the sauce, and a mixing bowl big enough for the custard.

A warm tray, not a pudding cup and not a cobbler

The useful purchases are soak-and-hold tools. Ceramic 9x13 bakers hold heat well and can sit in a low oven. A bread knife cubes a loaf without crushing it. A pitcher and a whisk make the custard in one vessel, poured from a bowl if you need the volume. A chafing dish or a carrier will hold the trays warm. A sauce pitcher is only for a sauce you are actually serving. An insulated carrier is for the ride, not for a two-hour wait in a trunk.

Bread pudding is a wedding workhorse for a quieter reason than cake. It wants to be assembled ahead, so the bread can take the custard. It bakes in the same 9x13 pans you may already be using for a tray dessert, and it can be held warm, which is a kindness at a backyard reception when you do not want a last-minute dessert in a crowded oven during the toasts. It fails when the custard is scrambled, when the center is still liquid, or when a loaf of fresh, soft sandwich bread turns to paste. It also fails when you leave a warm dairy dessert in the sun and call it a hold.

It is not banana pudding, which is a cold, layered dessert with wafers and fruit. It is not peach cobbler, which is fruit and a biscuit topping. It is a custard-soaked bread, baked until it is set, served warm, with or without a simple sauce. If the dessert table is already all cool squares, this is the warm one. If you already have cobbler, pick something else; two warm scooped desserts will read as the same job.

Two 9x13 pans will serve about twenty-four as a generous scoop or square. Four pans will serve about forty-eight. People will take a full serving when it is the only warm dessert. Keep a reserve in a low oven or in a chafing dish, and do not put every pan on a table to cool in the air. If you also offer a cool dessert, you can cut the bread-pudding count a little, but do not bake one pan for forty people and hope the other sweets will carry it.

Slightly stale bread is a virtue here. A dry crumb will take the custard. A very fresh, squishy loaf will sit in it and go heavy. Challah, brioche, or a simple French or Italian loaf all work. Do not use a strongly flavored rye unless that is honestly the pudding you already make. Cut the bread into roughly one-inch cubes, and leave the crusts on if they are tender; a very thick, hard crust can be trimmed.

A soak, a set custard, and a warm hold

Whisk the eggs, milk, cream, sugar, vanilla, cinnamon, and salt until the sugar is not sitting in a layer at the bottom. Pour it over the bread in the pans, or toss the bread with the custard in a large bowl and then divide it. Press the top cubes down once or twice so they are moistened, and let the pans sit, refrigerated, for at least an hour, or overnight. The sit is part of the recipe. A pan that goes straight from pouring to oven can come out dry on top and wet at the bottom.

If you soak overnight, take the pans out of the refrigerator while the oven heats, so they are not ice-cold going into a hot oven. A ceramic pan in particular does not like a violent temperature change, and a glass pan does not either. Bake at 350°F until the center is set — it should puff a little and not ripple like liquid when you nudge the pan — and the top is browned in places. That is often 40 to 50 minutes, a little longer if the pans were very cold. If the top is browning early, tent it loosely with foil for the last stretch.

A low oven, around 200°F, will hold baked pans for a while. A chafing dish with hot water under it will hold scooped pudding at a serving table. The pudding should be served warm, not scalding, and not left to sit in the middle temperature range on a sunny table. If you are traveling, bake at the house if you can, or bake at home, cool, refrigerate, and reheat, covered, until the center is hot, then hold warm. A long, lukewarm car ride is not a hold.

An optional vanilla sauce is melted butter, sugar, a little cream, and vanilla, stirred until smooth and served just warm in a pitcher, so people can add it or skip it. It is not required. A light dusting of powdered sugar is enough for some tables. Ice cream is a different project and needs a freezer, the way it does beside a cobbler. If you offer it, scoop from a freezer, not from a softening tub in the yard.

Raisins, a handful of chocolate, or a spoon of bourbon in the custard are allowed if that is how you already make this, and if you label the pan. Do not add a surprise bourbon to a family dessert without a word on the card. Do not load the custard with so much add-in that the bread cannot soak. This is a bread pudding, not a trail mix in a pan.

Wedding bread pudding for a crowd

The recipe below is for two 9x13 pans, about twenty-four servings, or four pans if you double it. Assemble it ahead, bake it until the center is set, and hold it warm. A simple vanilla sauce is optional.

Wedding Bread Pudding

Cubed slightly stale bread soaked in a custard of eggs, milk, cream, sugar, and vanilla, baked in 9x13 pans until the center is set and the top is browned. Serve warm. Two pans serve about 24; four pans, about 48.

About 24 or 48 servings · Prep 25 minutes plus soaking · Bake 40 to 50 minutes

Directions

  1. Butter two 9x13 pans and fill them with the cubed bread. (For the larger yield, use four pans.)
  2. Whisk the eggs, milk, cream, sugar, vanilla, cinnamon, and salt until the sugar is dissolved. Pour the custard evenly over the bread. Press the top cubes down so they are moistened. Refrigerate at least 1 hour, or overnight.
  3. Take the pans out while the oven heats to 350°F. Bake until the center is set and the top is browned in places, about 40 to 50 minutes. Tent with foil if the top browns too quickly.
  4. Hold warm in a low oven or a chafing dish. Serve with the optional sauce if you are making it: melt the butter with the sugar and cream, stir in the vanilla, and keep the pitcher warm.

Double every amount for four pans. Slightly stale bread soaks better than a very fresh loaf. If you add raisins, use about 1 1/2 cups for two pans, 3 cups for four, and say so on the label. If you add bourbon, start with 2 tablespoons in the custard for two pans, and label the tray.

A warm hold, a label, and a spoon

Put out a modest pan and refill from the warm reserve. A large, cooling tray on a sunny table will dry at the edges and go lukewarm in the middle, which is not a plan for a dairy dessert. Use a spoon or a spatula, and a small stack of bowls or plates. People will manage a square or a scoop if you give them a way to do it without a fight at the pan.

Label it in ordinary words: bread pudding, with wheat, milk, cream, and eggs. If there is bourbon, or raisins, or nuts, say so. People who need to know will not always ask. The same leftover habit you already have in the leftover guide applies: cool leftovers and refrigerate them, and do not leave a half pan on a porch because the night was long.

If this is a cooler-weather or later-evening dessert, it will feel more at home than it will at noon in July. You can still serve it in warm weather if you hold it correctly and keep the service window honest. In high heat, consider a cool dessert instead, or serve the pudding earlier, under cover, and do not let it languish beside a melting garnish.

A winter menu already likes warm sweets. This is the tray version you can actually scale. It is also a way to use bread you bought with a plan, not a way to empty a random bag of heels you do not like. Use bread you would eat. The custard will not hide a loaf you already regret.

One oven may not hold four pans fairly, and a refrigerator must have room for the soak. Clear the shelf before you cube the bread. If you are also holding a hot main, agree in advance which oven is for the pudding and when it has to move. A day-of kitchen that is also baking a tray dessert during the ceremony is a kitchen that will run late. Bake earlier, or assign the oven, or bake the day before and reheat with care.

A short bread-pudding checklist

Cube slightly stale bread, soak it in the custard, and give it time in the refrigerator. Bake until the center is set and the top is browned. Hold it warm, not in the sun. Two pans serve about twenty-four; four, about forty-eight. Label the tray, including bourbon or raisins if you used them, and cool any leftovers into the refrigerator.

Bread pudding is a quiet, old-fashioned generous dish: a familiar pan, a browned top, a custard that holds, and a count you can assemble the night before. If you treat the soak and the warm hold as part of the recipe, it will taste like you meant it. If you pour, bake at once, and leave the pan to fend for itself on a table, it will taste like a hurry. That work is ordinary, and it is worth doing while the loaves can still dry for a day and the refrigerator still has a shelf, instead of discovering a wet center when the coffee is already poured and the only thing left to do is hope another ten minutes in the oven will fix what the soak was meant to do.
