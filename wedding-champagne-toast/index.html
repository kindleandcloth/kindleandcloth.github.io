How Much Champagne for a Wedding Toast (and How to Pour It Without a Bar Staff)

For a short wedding toast, plan about one 750 ml bottle of sparkling wine for every 6 to 8 drinking guests, plus about 10 percent. That's roughly 13 bottles for 100 guests at a 3-ounce pour. The rest of the toast is logistics: cold bottles, glasses on trays, and a sparkling cider for anyone who isn't drinking.

The short version. Count the adults who'll actually drink, not the whole guest list. Decide the pour (a 3-ounce toast gets about 8 glasses per bottle; a 4-ounce pour gets about 6), divide, and add 10 percent. Buy a dry, inexpensive sparkling wine you like, chill it in ice water, and pre-pour it onto trays about ten minutes before the speeches, not half an hour. Put a labeled sparkling cider on every tray, and give one person the job of the toast so the couple never has to think about it.

Why the toast deserves its own plan

Picture the moment: the dinner plates are cleared, somebody taps a glass, and a hundred people turn toward the best man with something bubbly in hand. It's one of the few times in the night when everyone is doing the same thing at once. It's lovely. It's also the moment a backyard wedding finds out whether anyone remembered to chill the bottles.

Here's the honest part. A toast is short, but it asks a lot in a small window: a hundred glasses, poured, carried, and in hands within about ten minutes, while the person giving the speech hovers by the microphone. At a venue, a bar staff does this without you noticing. At home, it's a job, and it needs a person, a count, and a little equipment.

The good news is that the toast is the most predictable drink of the night. Everyone gets one glass, at one time. If you've already built the rest of the bar with our budget wedding bar and drinks guide, this is the easy part to get exactly right.

How much champagne to buy

A standard 750 ml bottle holds about 25 ounces. A toast pour is usually smaller than a glass of wine at dinner, somewhere between 2 and 4 ounces, because people sip it during the speeches rather than drink it with a meal. At 3 ounces you get about 8 pours a bottle; at 4 ounces, about 6. Most planning calculators land on the same handful of numbers.

Start with the adults who'll actually take a glass. Kids, people who don't drink, and anyone driving shouldn't be in the count; they'll get the cider. A rough rule is that about nine in ten adult guests will take a glass, but you know your crowd. Then divide by pours per bottle and add about 10 percent for spills, a second splash for the wedding party, and the guest who wanders over late.

Leftover unopened bottles keep for a long time, so if you're torn between two numbers, buy the higher one. Leftover opened bottles are a different story; more on that in a minute.

Choosing the bottle

You don't need Champagne with a capital C. A dry sparkling wine labeled brut, from anywhere, does the job: cava from Spain, crémant from France, prosecco from Italy, or a domestic sparkling wine. Brut is dry; "extra dry," confusingly, is a little sweeter, which some people prefer with dessert. Taste one bottle a few weeks out. If you'd happily drink a glass on a Tuesday, it's good enough for the toast.

This is not the place to splurge. Most guests will sip a few ounces while they listen to a speech, and nobody will remember the label. If you want one special bottle for the two of you, buy one and keep it at the head table. Everyone else gets the good, inexpensive one.

A practical tip: buy all the bottles from one store, of one wine, and ask whether they offer a case discount or take back unopened bottles. Many do, but ask before the day, not after.

Pouring for a crowd without a bar staff

There are two ways to do this at home, and both work.

Pre-pour on trays. Two or three helpers line up glasses on a table near the kitchen, pour a short measure into each, and carry trays out to the tables. It's the fastest way to get a glass into every hand. The catch is timing: sparkling wine poured thirty minutes early goes flat and warm. Start pouring about ten minutes before the first speech, and have the person running the timeline give the pourers a five-minute warning. Our day-of timeline has room for exactly that cue.

A bottle on every table. Put an opened, chilled bottle and glasses on each table just before the toasts, and ask one guest at each table to pour. This is calmer, uses fewer helpers, and feels a little more like a dinner party. It works best with tables of eight, where one bottle at a 3-ounce pour is about right. Tell the table hosts ahead of time so it doesn't look like you forgot.

Either way, name one person whose job is the toast: chilling, pouring, cider, and clearing the glasses after. That person should not be one of the people giving a speech.

Glasses, trays, and the walk to the tables

Flutes are the classic choice because the narrow shape keeps the bubbles longer and holds a short pour without looking stingy. Coupes, the wide shallow glasses, look gorgeous and spill easily, especially on a lawn. If you love coupes, use them at the head table or for a small display, and give everyone else a flute.

Outdoors, plastic is the honest answer. Disposable plastic flutes that hold a few ounces are lightweight, cheap, and don't shatter on patio stone or in the grass where people are dancing barefoot. If you're keeping waste down, rented glassware is the other good option; ask your rental company for a flute count and a return crate. For a small tower or a head-table moment, a set of plastic coupe glasses gives you the look without the breakage.

Carry glasses on rubber-lined serving trays, which keep a load steady on uneven ground. Fill a tray with fewer glasses than it can technically hold, about twelve to sixteen on a 16-inch round, and walk slowly. Two people making three trips is faster than one person dropping one tray.

Keeping the bottles cold

Sparkling wine tastes best cold, around the temperature of your refrigerator, and it also opens more calmly when it's cold. Warm bottles foam over. A refrigerator full of wedding food usually doesn't have room for fifteen bottles, so plan to chill them in tubs.

Ice alone chills slowly because only a few points of the bottle touch the ice. Ice plus cold water plus a handful of salt surrounds the whole bottle and chills it in roughly 20 to 30 minutes. Two galvanized beverage tubs hold a toast's worth of bottles for a mid-size wedding, and they look at home on a back porch. Put them in the shade and start chilling about an hour before the toast, so you have a margin if dinner runs long.

To open a bottle quietly, keep it cold, point it away from people, keep a thumb on the cork while you remove the cage, cover the top with a cotton towel, and turn the bottle, not the cork. You want a soft sigh, not a pop that lands in somebody's salad. If a bottle is opened but not finished, a sparkling wine stopper keeps it fizzy for the head table later in the evening.

A toast for people who aren't drinking

Every tray and every table should have a non-alcoholic option, poured into the same kind of glass, so nobody has to ask or explain. Sparkling apple cider is the easy, familiar answer; it looks right in a flute and kids love it. Plan about one bottle of cider for every six non-drinking guests, and keep a few extra, because some drinkers will switch.

Keep the cider glasses clearly separate on the tray, either on one side or marked with a small garnish like a single cranberry or a sprig of rosemary, so a helper can hand them to kids and to anyone who's asked for one. If you're building a bigger alcohol-free menu for the night, our guide to non-alcoholic wedding drinks covers a full station.

A sparkling toast you can batch

If you want the toast to feel a little more like a choice you made, a small spoonful of homemade syrup in the bottom of each glass does it. This cranberry-rosemary version is made two days ahead, keeps in the refrigerator, and turns pale sparkling wine a festive pink. It's perfect for a winter or New Year's wedding. The same syrup works with sparkling cider for the alcohol-free glasses.

Cranberry-Rosemary Sparkling Toast for a Crowd

A cranberry and rosemary simple syrup, made ahead and chilled, with a short pour of dry sparkling wine on top. About 24 glasses from one batch; about 48 doubled.

Directions

  1. In a saucepan, combine the water, sugar, cranberries, and rosemary. Bring to a simmer over medium heat, stirring until the sugar dissolves.
  2. Simmer gently for 8 to 10 minutes, until most of the cranberries have popped and the syrup is deep pink.
  3. Turn off the heat and let it steep for 30 minutes. Strain through a fine sieve, pressing lightly on the berries, and discard the solids.
  4. Pour the syrup into a clean bottle or jar, cool, cover, and refrigerate at least 2 hours. It keeps about a week in the refrigerator.
  5. To serve, pour about 1 tablespoon of cold syrup into each flute, then top with about 3 ounces of cold sparkling wine. Pour slowly down the side of the glass so it doesn't foam over.
  6. Drop in two or three cranberries and tuck a small rosemary sprig on top. Pre-pour no more than about 10 minutes before the toast.

For the alcohol-free glasses, use the same syrup topped with chilled sparkling apple cider or sparkling water, and mark them so helpers hand them to kids and non-drinkers. Serve the sparkling wine only to adults of legal drinking age. Taste one glass first: if your sparkling wine is on the sweet side, use a little less syrup.

A spouted bottle makes the pre-pour fast. Decant the syrup into a couple of glass bottles with pour spouts and one helper can dose a whole tray of flutes in a minute, with a second helper following behind with the wine.

A toast timeline

A month out: settle the headcount, taste a bottle, and buy. Rent or buy glasses. Decide whether you're pre-pouring or putting a bottle on each table, and tell the people involved.

Two days before: make the syrup, if you're using it, and refrigerate it. Count the glasses and trays.

The morning of: set the tubs in the shade, and set up a pouring table near the kitchen door with the glasses, trays, towels, and cider.

About an hour before the toasts: fill the tubs with ice and water and nestle the bottles in.

Ten minutes before: the timeline person gives the cue. Pour, tray, and carry. Cider first, so it isn't forgotten.

After the last speech: clear the glasses, cork any open bottles, and take one to the couple. If the toasts lead into dessert, our wedding sheet cake guide has a cake that's ready to cut the moment the glasses go down.

What to skip

Skip buying by the guest count instead of by the drinkers. Skip expensive bottles nobody will taste. Skip pre-pouring thirty minutes early. Skip glass coupes on a lawn full of dancers. Skip warm bottles, which foam over and waste a glass or two each. And skip making the couple responsible for any of it; they'll be sitting at the head table, and that's where they should stay.

A short toast checklist

Count the drinking adults, pick a pour, divide, and add 10 percent. Buy one dry sparkling wine you like. Chill it in ice water in the shade an hour ahead. Pre-pour ten minutes before, or put a bottle on each table. Put sparkling cider on every tray. Name one person to run the whole thing. Then raise a glass, and listen to the speech. Afterward, the evening can move on — a send-off later, or a calm farewell brunch the next morning — without asking the toast to carry the whole party.

Questions couples actually ask

How many bottles of champagne do I need for a wedding toast?

Count the adults who will actually drink bubbles, not the whole guest list. A standard 750 ml bottle pours about six to eight toast-sized glasses (3 to 4 ounces). For 100 drinking adults at a 3-ounce pour, that is about 13 bottles, plus 10 percent for spills and a second sip for the couple. Buy one dry sparkling wine you like; prosecco or cava is a perfectly good toast.

When should the champagne toast happen?

After dinner plates are cleared, before or just as dessert arrives, when people are still in their seats. It is a short window: glasses poured, carried, and in hands within about ten minutes. Put the pour on the day-of timeline so it does not collide with the cake or a dance set.

Do we have to use real Champagne for the toast?

No. Guests are tasting a few ounces during a speech, not doing a tasting flight. A good dry prosecco, cava, or other sparkling wine is the usual backyard choice, and it leaves money for the food. If you want a French bottle, buy a few for the head table and pour the same style of dry sparkling wine for everyone else — but only if you are comfortable with two labels.

What do non-drinkers hold during the toast?

Pour them something that looks like a toast: sparkling water with a citrus twist, or a little of the punch from your non-alcoholic drinks station, in a flute. The point is that no one is empty-handed in the photograph or during a blessing. Set those glasses on the same tray so it is not a special request.

Who gives the toasts, and how many should we have?

Tradition often runs best man, maid of honor, and maybe a parent, but you can have fewer. Two or three short toasts are enough for a backyard dinner; more than that and the bubbles go warm and the evening stalls.
