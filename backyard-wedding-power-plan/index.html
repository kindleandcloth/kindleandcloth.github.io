How to Power a Backyard Wedding Without Tripping the House

The short version: A backyard wedding uses more power than a house Saturday. List what you will actually plug in — lights, a speaker, catering gear, a coffee pot, maybe a trailer or a heater — and find out what the house can safely run. If the list is longer than the circuits, bring a generator or a serious power station, run outdoor-rated cords, and keep them out of the dew and the walkway. Test the whole thing at night, before the day.

Three power choices that keep the lights on

Best for a small, simple yard

The house, if you have walked the breakers, spread the load across more than one circuit, and used outdoor-rated extension cords and a weather-resistant timer or smart plug for the lights. Our lighting plan is the partner guide for hanging and testing.

Best for most receptions with a tent or a band

A rented generator, quietly placed, with a company that knows event loads — not a cheap unit from the garage that no one has started in a year. Ask about fuel, distance from the tent, and who is allowed to refuel it.

Best habit, whatever you use

A written list of every thing that will be plugged in, a night test, and a person who knows where the breaker box is and how to restart the generator. Power is not a morning-of discovery.

Here's the thing Junebug-style backyard advice has been saying for years, in plainer words: a house that runs a family just fine will often trip when you add a wall of lights, a speaker, a pair of chafers, and a coffee urn. The pretty part still needs a circuit.

We love lights over a yard. You probably do too. Just don't ask one patio outlet to be the venue electrician. This is the plan that sits under the lighting guide, the bar, and the tent: what the house can take, when to bring more power, and how to run it without a cord in the aisle.

Make a list before you make a plan

Buy the few things you will actually use, not a themed set you will store once. A string lights, which is the actual tool this part of the day uses, not a generic stand-in, a outdoor extension cords, a string light poles, a outdoor smart plug, a heavy duty outdoor outdoor, an outdoor-rated one, run along an edge, not across the only aisle, a cable cover ramp, a power stake timer, and a cable cover ramp, anywhere a cord has to cross a walk, even a short one.

Write down every thing that will need power, and when. Overhead lights and path lights, often from late afternoon until the end. A speaker or a small band. A laptop or a phone charger on a table. Catering — chafers, a hot box, an induction burner, a blender a well-meaning uncle will want to use. A coffee or cocoa station. Maybe a restroom trailer, a fan, a patio heater that is electric, a photo-booth printer. Maybe a fountain or a sign you didn't think of as a plug.

Then look at when they overlap. Lights plus dinner plus music is the usual peak. A trailer that needs a circuit all evening is a committed load, not a maybe. If a caterer is coming, ask them what they expect, in writing, and where they want to plug in. "We'll just use the kitchen" is how you share a breaker with the refrigerator and lose both.

You do not need an engineering degree. You need honesty and a little spreading out. A string-light canopy on its own is often a modest load. A string-light canopy plus a coffee urn plus a speaker plus two crockpots on one outdoor outlet is a different sentence.

What a house can run — and how to not trip it

Walk the breaker box and label, even roughly, which outlets in the yard and the kitchen share a circuit. Plug the lights into a different circuit from the kitchen if you can. Use the garage or a second outdoor outlet rather than daisy-chaining everything off the porch. GFCI outlets will trip if a cord sits in a puddle or a connection gets damp; that's them doing their job. Plan a dry path for the cords, and have a person who can reset a breaker without a panic.

Use outdoor-rated cords of an appropriate gauge — a long, skinny indoor cord is how lights dim and things get warm. Keep connections off the ground, taped or in a cover if rain is possible, and out of the walk from the cars to the chairs. A cord under a cheap rug in the aisle is a trip. A cord along a fence line, staked, is a plan. A smart plug or an outdoor timer means someone is not hunting for a switch at dusk; test it on the same Wi-Fi or the same timer schedule you'll use on the day.

If the house is old, or you've ever tripped the porch by running a vacuum and a heater, do not improvise a larger load for a wedding. Rent power. The cost of a generator for a night is usually smaller than the cost of a dark reception and a stalled meal.

Get a local quote for a generator if you are in any doubt. Ask about delivery, a quiet (inverter) unit if neighbors are close, how long a tank lasts, and whether a tech is on call. Typical rental ranges vary widely by size and city; treat any number you see online as a sense-check and get a real one. Place the unit downwind of the dinner, away from the ceremony, on firm ground, with a cord cover on the run back to the tent. Never run a generator in a closed garage or in a poorly ventilated tent. Exhaust is the unromantic reason.

A battery power station can be a good helper for a small speaker or a backup for a few lights. It is rarely the whole plan for a 60-person dinner under a tent. If you already own one, test the runtime with the actual gear, not with a phone charger. If you would need to buy a large one only for Saturday, compare that cost to a one-night rental and be honest about storage afterward.

The tent company, the caterer, and the restroom-trailer company should not each assume the house will "just handle it." Someone — a planner, a handy friend, or you, weeks out, not the morning of — has to be the person who adds the loads up. Our rentals checklist is the place to write the questions; this guide is the reason those questions exist.

Cables, weather, and a test that happens in the dark

Run cables the way you would want to walk: along edges, not across aisles. Cover anything that must cross a path. Keep connections dry. If rain is possible, the power plan is part of the weather plan, not a separate hope. Sidewalls, a little elevation for a connection box, and a backup for the lights will matter more than a new decorative strand.

Do a night test. Hang the lights, plug in the speaker, run whatever you can of the kitchen load, and walk the yard. You want to know which breaker trips, which cord is short, and which corner is still dark, while there is still time to rent a generator or move a plug. A week out is better than the day before; the day before is better than an hour before guests. Put the test on the timeline in our day-of notes, even if the actual test is earlier.

Label the important cords and the important outlets, and put a small flashlight or a phone light near the breaker box. The person who knows the system should not be in a formal photo when something goes off.

Who owns the problem, and what to tell vendors

Tell the caterer, the photographer, the officiant with a microphone, and the trailer company what power you can actually offer, and where. If you cannot offer it, say so early enough that they can bring what they need or you can rent it. A surprise "we need two 20-amp circuits at the tent" on Saturday is a fight you can avoid.

If you are hiring a DJ or a band, their power needs are not optional and not small. Ask for the requirement in writing and put it on the same list as the lights. Bands have arrived to a single patio outlet and a hopeful smile; it does not end well.

Neighbors may hear a generator more than they hear a playlist. A quieter unit, a little distance, and a conversation in advance will do more than an apology at 9 p.m. End-of-night shut-down should include the generator, not just the music: who kills it, who locks it, who makes sure it is safe until pickup.

A worked example, then get a real count of plugs

Imagine a 50-guest backyard dinner: a canopy of string lights, path lanterns on a timer, a speaker for the ceremony and the dancing, a cocoa pot, two chafers, and a photographer who would like a place to charge. That is already more than one porch outlet should be asked to do. A reasonable shape is lights on one outdoor circuit, kitchen and chafers on a kitchen or garage circuit that isn't also the refrigerator if you can help it, and a rented quiet generator for the speaker and anything a vendor has told you they need. If a restroom trailer is coming, it may want its own planned feed. Write that down and test the parts you can before you add the trailer.

A 16-guest dinner in the house, with a short outdoor ceremony and a single strand of lights, can often live on the house if you are careful. The mistake is copying a Pinterest backyard that was clearly running on event power and assuming your breaker panel is in the mood.

If you already own a small generator, start it in the week before, under the same load you can safely test, and confirm you have fresh fuel and the right cords. A unit that has sat in a garage since a hurricane five years ago is not a plan. If you don't want to own this problem, rent, and let a company that does this on weekends bring the machine, the fuel plan, and the pickup.

A short backyard-power checklist

  • List every thing that will be plugged in, and when those loads overlap
  • Walk the breaker box and spread lights, kitchen, and music across different circuits if you can
  • Use outdoor-rated cords, keep connections dry, and keep cables out of the aisle
  • Rent a generator if the house is in any doubt, and place it for exhaust, noise, and a safe cable run
  • Tell every vendor what power you can actually offer, in writing
  • Do a night test early enough to change the plan
  • Name a person for the breaker box and for generator shut-down

If you're also hanging lights, booking a tent, or bringing in a restroom trailer, tuck this list into the planning checklist and the lighting plan. Power is the quiet work that makes the visible work look easy.

The best backyard power plans are slightly overbuilt and thoroughly tested. That's how the lights stay on — hung from trees or light poles you can actually weight — the meal stays hot, and no one is standing at a breaker box in a wedding dress. A patio heater, if you use one, is a high draw; give it its own circuit and read the manual before Saturday.
