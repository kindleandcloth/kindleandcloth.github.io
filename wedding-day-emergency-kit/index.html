A Wedding Day Emergency Kit You Can Actually Carry

A wedding-day emergency kit should be a pouch you can actually carry, not a suitcase of "just in case." Fashion tape, a stain pen, a mini sewing kit, and blister pads will solve most of what happens between the getting-ready room and the last dance. Put it in one bag, and give one person the job of knowing where it is.

The short version. Pack a clear zippered pouch with fashion tape, a Tide To Go, a mini sewing kit, blister pads, safety pins, clear elastics, earring backs, a compact mirror, and a small wrinkle spray or access to a steamer. Keep it with the getting-ready room in the morning, then move it to a known spot — a bathroom shelf, a coordinator's bag, a designated friend's purse — for the rest of the day. The bathroom basket for guests is a separate, bigger setup; this pouch is for the people in the wedding.

One pouch, not a suitcase

The temptation is to pack for every possible disaster: a full sewing box, a pharmacy, a shoe store, a toolkit, a sewing machine. That kit will be heavy, it will be left in a car, and nobody will want to dig through it while a strap is slipping. The useful version is closer to a toiletry bag. You can hold it in one hand. You can name every item in it. You can move it from the bedroom to the back hallway without a meeting.

Wedding-party forums are blunt about what actually got used. Fashion tape. A stain pen. A needle and thread, or more often a safety pin. A blister pad. A spare earring back. A hair elastic. Almost nobody tells the story of the emergency button collection, the four kinds of pain reliever, or the sewing scissors they were afraid to use on a dress. Pack the short list well, and put anything medical — actual medication, allergy tablets, a full first-aid box — with a person who already knows someone's needs, not loose in a shared pouch.

This kit is for the couple, the wedding party, and the officiant. The guest bathroom basket is a different, slightly fuller setup, and it should stay in the restrooms. See wedding bathroom basket essentials for that list, and getting ready at home for the food, the steam, and the morning pace. Do not make one basket do every job in every room.

The core four, and a few things worth adding

Fashion tape. A pack of double-sided fashion tape is the item that earns its keep on a neckline, a wrap dress, a slipping strap, or a gap that photographs badly. Cut small pieces. Press them on clean, dry fabric and skin. Put the roll back in the pouch so it does not end up in a bridesmaid's pocket for the rest of the week.

A stain pen and a sewing kit. A Tide To Go takes care of a surprising number of mid-day spots if you get to them quickly and blot, not rub. A mini sewing kit handles a loose button, a hem that steps out, or a pop at a seam. Add more safety pins than the kit includes. In a hurry, a pin is faster than a needle, and it is often enough until the end of the night.

Blister pads. Hydrocolloid pads belong in this pouch as well as in the guest bathroom, because the wedding party is in new shoes for longer, and they may not want to walk across a yard to the restroom in the middle of photos. Put a pair of small scissors in the pouch if the pads need cutting, or buy shapes that do not.

The little clothing and hair extras. Clear elastics, a pack of spare earring backs, a compact mirror, a travel lint roller if you have room, and a small static spray or wrinkle-release spray cover the rest of the ordinary emergencies. A handheld steamer is better than the spray for the morning, but it should stay in the getting-ready room plugged in, not travel in the pouch all day.

Who carries it, and when it moves

Give the pouch to one person who is not in every photograph. A coordinator, a calm friend, a sibling, or a parent who already has a bag is a better choice than a maid of honor in a strapless dress with no pockets. Tell more than one person where it will be — "on the shelf in the hall bathroom after 3 p.m." — so the owner of the pouch can dance, too.

In the morning, the pouch lives in the getting-ready room, unzipped, in a place people can see. When the last person walks out, it zips and moves. It should not go to the ceremony site in someone's arms and then get set down in the grass. A hook, a shelf, or a bag on a reserved chair in the back is enough.

If you are getting ready at a house and marrying in a yard, this is easy. If you are traveling from a hotel to a venue, assign the pouch a seat in the car, not a "we'll remember it" on a bathroom counter. The steamer can stay behind once you leave the room. The pouch cannot.

What not to put in it

Skip a full pharmacy. If someone needs a specific medication, they should bring it, and a person who knows about it should know where it is. A communal bottle of pain reliever in a shared bag is a well-meant idea that gets messy around children, leftover glasses of wine, and other people's medical histories. The same is true of allergy medication, sewing scissors used as a tool by six people, and anything sharp that is not in a little sleeve.

Skip valuables. This is not where the rings live, or the vows, or the house keys, or a backup credit card, unless the person carrying the pouch is also the person you would trust with those things anyway. A ring dish and a designated jewelry person are a better plan than a clear bag that will be opened twelve times in a public hallway.

Skip duplicates of everything already in the bathroom basket, except the four core items. Those four are worth having in both places. A second deodorant, a second lint roller, and a second box of pads can live in the restroom. Your pouch should still close easily and fit under an arm.

And skip the temptation to pack a "just in case" outfit change unless you already planned one. A spare pair of earrings, yes, if they are small. A second dress is a different kind of plan, and it should hang in a closet, not be rolled into an emergency bag.

What to skip

Skip a kit so large it stays in the car. Skip seven people "holding" one item each, which is how the stain pen disappears. Skip hiding the bag so well that nobody can find it. Skip packing it the morning of, in a rush, with whatever is under the sink. And skip assuming the venue will have these things. Some will have a safety pin. That is not a kit.

A short emergency-pouch checklist

Buy a clear pouch and put fashion tape, a stain pen, a mini sewing kit, extra safety pins, blister pads, clear elastics, earring backs, a compact mirror, and a small wrinkle spray in it. Leave the steamer in the getting-ready room. Give the pouch to one person, tell two other people where it will be after you leave the room, and move it on purpose, not as an afterthought. Then go to the ceremony knowing a slipped strap is a two-minute problem, not a crisis.
