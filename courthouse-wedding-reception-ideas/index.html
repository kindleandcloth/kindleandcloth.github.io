Courthouse Wedding Reception Ideas (When the Party Comes After)

The short version: A courthouse or civil ceremony and a later party can be one of the most relaxed ways to get married — if you treat them as two planned pieces, not a ceremony with a question mark after it. Decide when the reception happens, how formal it is, and how you'll tell people, then build a gathering that feels like you: a backyard dinner, a restaurant buyout, a brunch, or a standing toast with really good cake.

Plenty of couples get married at a courthouse, a city hall, or a judge's office on a weekday morning, then wonder what the "reception" is supposed to look like. It doesn't have to look like a truncated ballroom wedding. It can look like the best party you'd throw anyway — just with a reason to raise a glass and a leftover bit of cake. A later party after a private signing is close in spirit to a post-elopement reception; the useful work is naming the day honestly so guests know whether they are coming for vows or for a meal.

This is a practical menu of reception ideas after a civil or courthouse ceremony, including how to time it, how to word the invite, and how to keep it from feeling like an afterthought. If you're still shaping the whole day, start with our small and micro wedding planning checklist.

First, decide what this gathering is

Are you inviting people to celebrate a marriage that already happened, or to stand with you for a short vow reading and then eat? Both are good. They're different invitations. "Join us for dinner after we get married in the morning" is not the same as "We're married — come help us mark it on Saturday." Be honest on the card. Guests would rather know than guess whether they should dress for a ceremony.

The legal ceremony can be just the two of you, plus required witnesses. The reception can be ten people or eighty. That's one of the quiet advantages of a courthouse wedding: the guest list for the party doesn't have to match the guest list for the vows. You can have a private signing and a wide-open Saturday dinner, or a handful of family at the courthouse and a slightly larger lunch. Both counts are allowed to be different, and you don't owe anyone a diagram of who was where.

When should the reception happen?

Same day works if the courthouse is close, the timing is civilized, and someone else is setting the party up while you're signing papers. A lunch after a morning appointment is lovely. A dinner the same evening can be too, as long as you aren't asking guests to hover for six hours.

A week later, a month later, even the next season — that's common, and often kinder. You get photographs in the clothes you wanted, a backyard that isn't rushed, and a day that isn't split between a clerk's counter and a rented tent. If the party is later, say so early, so people don't feel they missed the "real" wedding. There isn't a consolation prize here. There are two events, on purpose.

Reception ideas that actually fit

A backyard or patio dinner

The most natural follow-up, especially if you already like the idea of a wedding at home. Long tables, string lights, a short toast, and a meal you can actually taste. You don't need a processional. You might still want a little entrance, a first dance, or just a moment where someone says your names and people clap. For food formats, see budget wedding food ideas; for the table itself, DIY centerpieces or a rental kit from RENT MY WEDDING will do more than a sweetheart table you don't need.

A restaurant buyout or a private room

This is the lowest-logistics version. You pick a place whose food you already order, take a private room or a long table, and let the restaurant do the plates and the cleanup. It's especially good for a same-day lunch after a morning ceremony. Ask about cake, a small toast window, and whether you can bring flowers. Don't fight the room into looking like a ballroom. Lean into being at a restaurant you love.

Brunch

Coffee, something baked, fruit, a savory main, and a cake at noon can feel more like a celebration than a heavy evening you don't have the energy for. It's also easier on a weekday-after-courthouse timeline, and guests drink less. Put the meal time on the invitation so nobody arrives at 2 expecting dinner.

Cocktails and cake, standing

A two-hour toast in a backyard, a gallery, or a bar's back room: drinks, a few substantial bites, a cake, and a guest book or a stack of photos people can sign. This is ideal when your list is mixed (some local, some in town for the night) and you don't want a seated dinner. Have enough tables for older guests to sit. Two hours is plenty; three is a party.

A picnic or a park lunch

Blankets, a couple of low tables, serious food (not just a baguette and hope), and a plan for restrooms and shade. Pretty, and a little more work than it looks. Permits matter. So does a backup if the grass is wet. Save this for a small list and a park you already know.

An evening at a bar or a favorite music venue

If the two of you would rather dance than sit through courses, host a late toast and let the night be a night. Send a start time and an end time. Arrange a tab or a drink ticket so guests aren't surprised at the bar. This can follow a tiny ceremony earlier in the day, or a courthouse appointment from last month.

How to tell people, and what to wear

The invitation should do four jobs: name the two of you, say you're celebrating a marriage (and whether the ceremony already happened), give the time and place of the reception, and mention dress. "We were married at city hall on Friday — please come to dinner Saturday at six" is clearer than a lot of poetry.

If only some people were at the courthouse, don't make the others feel late to their own invitation. There is no hierarchy you need to announce. A line like "We said our vows in a quiet room downtown; now we want to sit down with you" is enough. A service such as Rizz Invites is handy if the plan changes or you need to message the list about weather.

Dress can be "garden party," "come as you would to a nice dinner," or something more formal if the dinner is formal. Just say it. After a courthouse morning, a lot of couples change into the outfit they wished they could have worn at the clerk's office. That's a feature, not a compromise.

A few details that make it feel finished

You don't need a full wedding arc. You might still want one or two markers so the evening has a shape: a short welcome, a toast, a cake, a song, a bouquet that isn't trying to be a bridal party. A small instant camera on a table can stand in for a photo booth. A framed photo from the courthouse steps is a sweeter guest book than a blank book you have to tend.

Photographs: if you didn't have a photographer at the courthouse, you can still take portraits before the reception, in better light, in the clothes you love. Tell the photographer it's a celebration of a marriage that already happened, so they aren't waiting for a processional that isn't coming.

If the party is in a yard, the unglamorous list still applies — restrooms, power, a weather plan, a neighbor note. The civil ceremony doesn't exempt you from any of that. It just means you already did the legal part, which is a very good position to be in when the lights come on.

What to do with the legal day itself

Even if the party is later, the courthouse morning deserves a little shape. Book the appointment, read the witness rules, and know where you can stand for a photograph on the steps or in a nearby park. Wear something you won't mind being in the pictures, even if it's not "the" outfit. Bring the license, the IDs, and one calm person who isn't you.

If friends or parents will be there, give them a start time and a place to meet that isn't "somewhere in the municipal building." Courthouses are busy. A coffee after, just the four or six of you, can be the sweetest part of the day — and it doesn't have to carry the weight of a reception. Save the speeches for the party, unless someone has something short they really need to say. And breathe. The clerk's office is rarely cinematic, and that's fine. The day is allowed to start quietly.

A simple order of events for the party

  • Guests arrive; drinks and something small to eat are already out
  • The two of you arrive (or just appear from the house) and someone welcomes everyone in one minute, not ten
  • A toast or two, then dinner or the main spread
  • Cake or dessert, and any song you actually want, not a first-dance obligation
  • A clear last-call or send-off so the evening has an edge, especially in a residential yard

That's enough of a timeline for almost any of the formats above. If you want an hour-by-hour for a full backyard wedding day, wait for our day-of timeline — or, for now, borrow the pacing in the planning checklist and cut anything that assumes a processional.

Budget-wise, a civil ceremony plus a later party is often calmer than a single packed Saturday, but it isn't automatically cheaper. You're still feeding people. You may still want a photographer, a cake, and a yard that works at night. What you usually skip is the venue's minimum, the ceremony chair flip, and the pressure to perform a very long day. Put the money where the gathering actually happens. If you want a single number to watch, it's usually the meal and the photographs, not a second set of ceremony chairs you no longer need.

The best courthouse receptions don't try to replay a ballroom timeline at half scale. They pick a format that fits the day you actually had, and then they feed people well: a board when you walk in, a couple of drink dispensers on a side table, and a meal that doesn't need a ballroom kitchen. That's the whole trick. A simple guestbook alternative and a plan for the exit, if you want one, will make the party feel finished without borrowing a courthouse script. Questions couples actually ask

Can you have a reception after a courthouse wedding?

Yes, and it is one of the more relaxed ways to get married. Treat the legal ceremony and the party as two planned pieces: a quiet signing, then a dinner or a backyard party hours, days, or even months later. Name the party honestly on the invitation so guests are not expecting a second ceremony unless you are offering one. A post-elopement reception follows the same idea if the legal day was even smaller.

When should the reception be after a courthouse ceremony?

A lunch or dinner the same day works if the courthouse is in the morning and you are not trying to flip a sidewalk into a cocktail hour. A party a week or a season later works just as well, and it gives far-flung guests a date they can actually travel for. Put a clear arrival time on the invite; people need to know whether they are coming for vows or for a meal.

How many people can come to a courthouse wedding?

That is set by the courthouse, not by you, and it is often a very small number — sometimes only witnesses. Call the clerk and ask about guests, phones, and timing before you invite a crowd to the steps. The reception is where the longer list belongs. If you are deciding between a civil signing and a tiny backyard ceremony, our backyard elopement guide spells out the difference.

Do you still send invitations for a courthouse wedding reception?

Yes, if you are asking people to come to a party. The invitation should say what the day is: a reception following a private civil ceremony, a dinner after we get married at city hall, or whatever is true. Formal paper is optional; a clear card is not. The Champagne Ballroom collection is a good fit if you want the reception to feel like a dressier evening.

What should we wear to the courthouse and to the party after?

Wear what you want to be married in, plus whatever the courthouse requires for security and the weather. Many couples change for the party, or they stay in the same clothes and let the dinner be the more formal hour. Tell guests the dress on the invitation so a weekday civil ceremony does not leave them underdressed for a Saturday night reception. A simple exit or a guestbook alternative at the party will do more than trying to recreate a courthouse moment later.
