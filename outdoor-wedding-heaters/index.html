The short version: Outdoor wedding heaters work when you treat them as equipment, not as decor. For a seated dinner, heat the tent or use the house. Patio heaters can take the edge off a porch, a tent opening, or a short outdoor linger — they will not warm a whole lawn. Rent if you only need them for a night; buy only if you'll use them again. Give every unit clearance, a firm base, and a person who knows how to light and shut it down.

Three heat choices, depending on the space

Best for most receptions

A heated tent, or the house, as the actual dining room. Patio heaters are a supplement at the edges, not a substitute for a warm room. If a tent is the plan, start with our tent rental cost guide and ask the company about heat as a line item, not as a favor.

Best for a porch or cocktail hour

A few mushroom-style propane patio heaters, placed with real clearance, on a firm surface, with tanks you have already checked. A well-reviewed unit such as an outdoor propane patio heater with a stable base and wheels is a reasonable buy if you will use it for years; for a single Saturday, renting is usually calmer.

Best comfort layer, not a heating plan

Throws and hand warmers at the ceremony and near the door. These make the walk and the vows bearable. They do not replace a tent heater. Pair them with the plan in our winter backyard guide.

A heater at a winter wedding is one of those things that looks simple in a photograph — a glow, a gathering, everyone seeming fine in their coats — and is a little more serious in a yard. Fuel, clearance, wind, a tip-over, a tent ceiling, a child in a long dress: these are the details that decide whether the heat is a kindness or a problem.

We love a porch that stays usable after dusk. You probably do too. Just don't ask a single patio heater to do the work of a venue's HVAC. Here's how to choose the right kind of heat, how many you might need, and what to skip.

Start with the room, not with the object

You do not need a specialty aisle of props. You need a short, honest list. A patio heater, if the evening will drop, and you have a safe, level place and a plan for the cord, a hand warmers, a patio heater cover, a propane hose for patio heater, a blanket waterproof picnic, which is the actual tool this part of the day uses, not a generic stand-in, a wooden crates, a market umbrella, and a solar pathway lights, which is the actual tool this part of the day uses, not a generic stand-in.

If people will sit for dinner, they need a room that holds warmth: the house, or a tent with sides and a heater meant for that tent. A row of patio heaters around an open lawn will create a few warm pockets and a lot of guests who are still cold at the table. Stand in the space at the hour you'll actually use it, in weather as close as you can get, and be honest about whether you would want to eat there for an hour.

Cocktail hour can be more flexible. A porch, a tent entrance, or a semi-enclosed patio can work with two or three patio heaters and a way to step fully inside. A fire pit can be an optional linger, not the only warm place, and it needs supervision — more on that below. The ceremony should stay short enough that heaters are a courtesy at the edges, not a requirement for sitting still through readings.

Wind changes everything. A heater's useful radius shrinks in a breeze, and a tall unit can be unstable on soft ground. If the forecast is windy, prioritize sides on the tent and a room with walls. Don't add more open-air heaters and hope.

A pyramid-style glass-tube heater looks dramatic and is easier to bump; for a wedding crowd, the wider, heavier base is usually the calmer shape.

How many, and where they go

Patio heaters warm a radius, not a rectangle. Manufacturers often talk about a reach of something like eight to ten feet in still air; in practice, on a cool night, people will huddle closer than that. For a porch or a cocktail pocket, two units can be enough for a small group. For a long tent aisle or an open patio, you may want more, spaced so people aren't packed around a single pole. Do not line a dinner table with heaters the way you would with candles. Give seats their own room, and put the heat at the edges or at the openings.

Clearance is not optional. Keep heaters away from tent walls, draping, dry wreaths, low branches, paper lanterns, and anything that can blow. Follow the distance in the manual — often several feet from a combustible surface — and then add a little more if you're unsure. Don't put a unit under a low tree. Don't put one next to a bouquet installation. Don't put one in a doorway that is also the only exit.

The ground has to be firm and level. A heater on a slope, a board that flexes, or a soft lawn is a tip risk. If you must use grass, put down a stable pad or choose a different spot. Keep a walkway around each unit so a guest in a long dress isn't brushing the column. If children are at the wedding, treat the heater like a stove that's out in the room: visible, set back, and supervised.

Never use a patio heater, a fire pit, or a barbecue-style propane heater inside a closed room or a poorly ventilated tent in a way the equipment wasn't designed for. Carbon monoxide is the unromantic reason. If a tent company is providing heat, use their units and their instructions, not a garage heater you wheel in "just in case."

Fuel, lighting, and the person who is actually in charge

Propane patio heaters typically run on a standard tank under the base. Ask how long a full tank lasts at the setting you'll use — often a few hours, not an entire day at full tilt — and have a spare for each unit if the night is long. Check that the tanks you have are actually full, days before, not at 3 p.m. when the rental truck has left. If you are renting, confirm who brings the tanks, who takes them away, and whether a swap mid-event is possible.

Electric heaters and infrared "wall" units can make sense on a porch that already has a safe outdoor circuit. They do not usually make sense as the only plan for a lawn, and they will compete with lights, a band, and catering for power. If you go this way, look at the lighting plan for outdoor-rated cords and circuits, and don't daisy-chain extension cords under a wet runner.

Name a person. Someone who is not in the wedding party should know how to light each unit, how to shut it down, where the spare tank is, and what to do if a heater smells wrong, flares, or gets bumped. Put a fire extinguisher where that person can reach it, not in a hall closet. This is ordinary caution, the same way you would not leave a row of candles unwatched under a tent valance.

Do a lighting test in the week before, in daylight first, then at dusk. You want to know the sequence when you are calm, not when guests are arriving and the first unit won't catch.

Fire pits, mushrooms, and what is not a heater

A fire pit is a gathering place. It is not a heating system for a guest list. Used well, it gives people a place to stand with a drink for twenty minutes. Used badly, it is a crowded ring, a spark near a dress, a trip hazard, and a smoke that blows into the dinner. If you want one, keep it optional, keep it away from the main path, and give it a person who is actually watching. Save the s'mores plan for a setup that can be supervised.

Tabletop heaters and small "personal" propane units have a narrow use — a single cocktail table, maybe — and a lot of ways to get in the way. They tip, they get moved by guests, and they don't change the temperature of a room. Skip them for a wedding crowd unless you already know the exact table and the exact unit.

Lanterns, hurricanes, and string lights do important work. They just aren't heat. Use lanterns and string lights for the path and the overhead glow, and let the heaters do the warming. A pretty glow can trick you into thinking a porch is warmer than it is. Your guests' hands will not be fooled.

What to tell guests, and how the evening should feel

Heaters work better when people know what kind of evening they're walking into. A short line on the website — the ceremony is outdoors and short, dinner is in a heated tent, bring a coat — will do more than an extra unit you placed badly. Guests dress more reasonably when they aren't guessing. A surprise uncovered cocktail hour is how you end up lending your own jacket at the door.

You still want the evening to feel like a wedding, not a worksite. Hide the spare tanks, run the hoses where people won't trip, and don't park a unit in the middle of the photograph you care about. A heater can live at the edge of the frame. The tables can still look like tables. Throws in a basket are nicer to look at than a pile of coats on a chair, and they have a job.

If the temperature drops more than you planned, the move is to shorten the outdoor stretch and open the warm room earlier — not to wheel out a garage heater you haven't tested. Our weather plan is for that kind of call. Put a time on it, and tell the officiant the same thing you tell yourselves.

A short outdoor-heater checklist

  • Decide whether dinner lives in a heated tent or in the house, before you shop for patio heaters
  • Get a local rental quote, including tanks, delivery, and who lights the units
  • If you buy, assemble and test the heater days before, and confirm the tank is full
  • Place units on firm, level ground with real clearance from fabric, branches, and exits
  • Name a person for lighting, shut-down, and a spare tank, and put an extinguisher in reach
  • Never use a patio heater as indoor heat in a closed room
  • Still put out throws and hand warmers; they cover the walk and the ceremony the heaters won't

If you're also mapping the rest of a winter day, tuck this into the weather plan, the rentals checklist, and the day-of timeline. Heat is one of those lines that feels easy to postpone and impossible to invent at 4 p.m. when the wind comes up.

The best outdoor-heater plans are a little boring: the right room, a few well-placed units, a full tank, and someone who knows the switch. That's how guests stay long enough to enjoy the part you actually wanted them to see.
